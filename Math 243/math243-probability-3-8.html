Lecture 3

Odds & the Language of Chance

Probability answers "how likely is A out of everything?" Odds answer a narrower, older question: "how does A compare to not-A?" Odds are the language of bookmakers, geneticists, and insurers — get fluent in converting between the two.

3.1What odds actually measure

Odds compare success against failure directly, rather than success against the whole sample space the way probability does. That's the entire conceptual jump.

Core definition
\[ \text{Odds}(A) = \frac{P(A)}{P(\bar{A})} = \frac{P(A)}{1-P(A)} \]

valid whenever A and its complement are the only two outcomes, i.e. \(P(A)+P(\bar A)=1\).

Odds in favor of A
\[ O_A = \frac{n(A)}{n(\bar A)} \]
Odds against A
\[ O_{\bar A} = \frac{n(\bar A)}{n(A)} \]

The two are reciprocals of each other, and odds-in-favor of A is exactly odds-against of B when A and B are complements.

Going back to probability
\[ \text{If odds in favor of A are } a:b, \quad P(A)=\frac{a}{a+b}, \qquad P(\bar A)=\frac{b}{a+b} \]

3.2Try it yourself

Odds → Probability converter
Worked example — bag of balls

A bag has red and blue balls. Odds in favor of red are 3 : 7. Find P(red) and P(blue).

\(n(A)=3,\ n(\bar A)=7,\ n(S)=10\). \(P(\text{red})=\tfrac{3}{10}=0.3,\quad P(\text{blue})=\tfrac{7}{10}=0.7\).

Worked example — race odds

An athlete has probability 0.2 of winning a race. Find the odds against winning.

\(P(W)=0.2,\ P(\bar W)=0.8\). \(O_{\bar W}=\dfrac{P(\bar W)}{P(W)}=\dfrac{0.8}{0.2}=4\), i.e. 4 : 1 — for every 1 chance of winning, there are 4 chances of not winning.

3.3Independent vs. dependent word problems

The classic exam setup: two students X and Y attempt a problem. You're given odds, asked to convert to probabilities, then combine — first assuming independence, then with a stated conditional probability.

Worked example — odds against X are 8:6, odds in favor of Y are 14:16

\(P(X)=\tfrac{3}{7},\ P(\bar X)=\tfrac{4}{7}\) and \(P(Y)=\tfrac{7}{15},\ P(\bar Y)=\tfrac{8}{15}\).

Possible exam questions
The odds against an event are 5 : 2. Find P(event) and P(not event).
Odds against 5:2 means odds in favor are 2:5, so \(P(A)=\tfrac{2}{7}\), \(P(\bar A)=\tfrac{5}{7}\).

Explain, in one sentence, why "odds of 3:1" is not the same statement as "probability of 3/1".
Probability can never exceed 1 because it's success ÷ total; odds compare success ÷ failure directly, so odds can be any positive ratio, including numbers bigger than 1:1 without breaking any rule.
Brainstorm

If odds against an event are 4:1, what are the odds in favor? (Just flip the ratio — 1:4.) Now: is P(A) = 1/4? No. That's the single most common exam trap in this chapter — odds-in-favor 1:4 gives \(P(A)=\tfrac{1}{1+4}=\tfrac15\), not \(\tfrac14\). Always add numerator and denominator to get the total before dividing.

Lectures 3–4

Joint Probability & Contingency Tables

Joint probability asks about two events at once — "both happen." It's the workhorse behind survey cross-tabs, quality-control tables, and every 2×2 grid you'll see for the rest of this course.

4.1Definition

For events A and B, \(P(A\cap B)\) is the probability that both occur simultaneously.

Independent events
\[ P(A\cap B)=P(A)\times P(B) \]

Coin + die: \(P(\text{heads})\times P(\text{4})=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac16=\tfrac1{12}\).

Dependent events
\[ P(A\cap B)=P(A)\,P(B|A) \]

Two aces, no replacement: \(\tfrac{4}{52}\cdot\tfrac{3}{51}=\tfrac{1}{221}\).

4.2The joint-probability table

Any two binary events can be laid out in a 2×2 grid where each interior cell is a joint probability and the margins are the individual (marginal) probabilities.

Lecture 5

Conditional Probability & Bayes' Theorem

This is the module where probability starts to feel like reasoning. Every medical test, spam filter, and diagnostic system on earth runs on the formula in this section.

5.1Conditional probability

\(P(A|B)\) — "probability of A given B has occurred" — restricts the sample space down to B, then asks what share of that smaller space belongs to A too.

Definition
\[ P(A|B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)},\ P(B)\ne0 \]
\[ P(B|A)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(A)},\ P(A)\ne0 \]
Multiplication law (rearranged from the definition)
\[ P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B|A)=P(B)P(A|B) \]

Extends to any number of events: \(P(A_1\cap\cdots\cap A_k)=P(A_1)P(A_2|A_1)P(A_3|A_1\cap A_2)\cdots P(A_k|A_1\cap\cdots\cap A_{k-1})\).

Worked example — dice

A pair of dice is thrown. Find P(sum ≥ 10 | first die shows 5).

\(B=\{(5,1)\ldots(5,6)\}\), \(A\cap B=\{(5,5),(5,6)\}\). \(P(A|B)=\dfrac{2/36}{6/36}=\dfrac13\).

Worked example — TV show couple

P(husband watches)=0.4, P(wife watches)=0.5, P(husband | wife watches)=0.7. Find P(both watch) and P(at least one).

\(P(W\cap H)=P(W)P(H|W)=0.5\times0.7=0.35\). \(P(W\cup H)=0.4+0.5-0.35=0.55\). Also \(P(W|H)=\tfrac{0.35}{0.4}=0.875\) — note it's not equal to \(P(H|W)\); conditional probability is not symmetric.

5.2Bayes' theorem

Bayes' theorem flips a conditional probability: it lets you get from \(P(\text{evidence}|\text{cause})\) — which is usually what's measured — to \(P(\text{cause}|\text{evidence})\) — which is usually what you actually want to know.

The formula
\[ P(A|B)=\frac{P(B|A)P(A)}{P(B|A)P(A)+P(B|A^c)P(A^c)} \]

General form for k mutually exclusive causes \(A_1,\ldots,A_k\): \[ P(A_i|D)=\frac{P(A_i)P(D|A_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{k}P(A_j)P(D|A_j)} \]

The classic — blood test

A test is 90% effective at detecting a disease when present, but gives a false positive 5% of the time in healthy people. Only 1% of the population actually has the disease. Given a positive result, what's P(disease)?

\[ P(D|+)=\frac{0.01\times0.90}{(0.01\times0.90)+(0.99\times0.05)}=\frac{0.009}{0.009+0.0495}\approx 0.15 \] Only 15% — even a "90% accurate" test is mostly wrong when the disease is rare. This is the base-rate fallacy, and it's the single most testable idea in this module.

Brainstorm

Try to say Bayes' theorem in plain English before you memorize the fraction: "your updated belief is your old belief, rescaled by how much more likely the evidence is under your hypothesis than under everything else." If P(evidence) is dominated by false positives from the huge "no disease" population, your posterior stays low no matter how "accurate" the test sounds. That single sentence answers most Bayes' exam questions without needing the exact formula in front of you.

Random Variables & Probability Distributions

Up to now events have been described in words ("heads", "defective", "male teacher"). A random variable is the bridge that turns those outcomes into numbers you can compute with — averages, variances, and eventually calculus.

6.1What a random variable is

A random variable assigns a definite number to every outcome of a chance experiment. Formally, it's a function from the sample space to the real numbers.

6.2The probability mass function (pmf)

Definition & the three rules
\[ f(x)=P(X=x) \]
  • \(f(x)\ge0\) for every x
  • \(\sum_x f(x)=1\)
  • \(P(X=x)=f(x)\)
Worked example — drawing black balls

A bag has 10 balls, 4 black. Draw 3 without replacement; X = number of black balls drawn.

\(f(x)=\dfrac{\binom{4}{x}\binom{6}{3-x}}{\binom{10}{3}}\) for \(x=0,1,2,3\).

Test: \(f(x)=\dfrac{2x-1}{8}\), x = 0,1,2,3.

Summing gives 1, but \(f(0)=-\tfrac18 < 0\) — not a valid pmf. A negative probability breaks rule 1 even if the total is correct. Always check every individual value, not just the sum.

\(f(x)=\alpha\left(\tfrac34\right)^x,\ x=0,1,2,\ldots\) Find α.

Brainstorm

A random variable is often called "random" but its distribution is completely fixed and deterministic — what's random is which value shows up on a given trial, not the rulebook (f(x)) that governs the odds. Exam questions love to test whether you can tell the difference between "find P(X=3)" (read the pmf) and "find k" (solve the constraint that the pmf must sum to 1).

Lecture 7

Distribution Functions: CDF & PDF

The pmf tells you the probability of hitting one exact value. The cumulative distribution function (CDF) tells you the probability of being at or below a value — the "running total" view, essential for medians, percentiles, and continuous variables where individual points have zero probability.

7.1Discrete CDF

Definition & properties
\[ F(x)=P(X\le x)=\sum_{t\le x} f(t) \]
  • Monotonic increasing: \(a\le b \Rightarrow F(a)\le F(b)\)
  • \(\lim_{x\to-\infty}F(x)=0,\quad \lim_{x\to+\infty}F(x)=1\)
  • \(0\le F(x)\le1\) everywhere

Lecture 8

Joint, Marginal & Conditional Distributions

The finale: two random variables at once. This is where the whole course converges — the joint pmf/pdf you build here decomposes back into the marginal distributions of §06 and the conditional probabilities of §05, and finally lets you formally test independence.

8.1Joint probability distribution

Discrete: joint pmf
\[ f(x,y)=P(X=x,Y=y) \]
  • \(f(x,y)\ge0\)
  • \(\sum_x\sum_y f(x,y)=1\)
Continuous: joint pdf
\[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x,y)\,dx\,dy=1 \]
  • \(f(x,y)\ge0\)
  • Joint CDF: \(F(x,y)=P(X\le x,Y\le y)\)
Worked example — coin tossed three times

X = number of heads, Y = number of tails in the last two tosses.

8.3Conditional distributions

Definitions
\[ f(y|x)=\frac{f(x,y)}{g(x)} \]
\[ f(x|y)=\frac{f(x,y)}{h(y)} \]
Worked example — conditional pmf from a table

Given \(h(1)=\tfrac{3}{7}\) and the row \(f(x,1)\) values, the conditional distribution of X given Y=1 works out to \(f(0|1)=\tfrac12,\ f(1|1)=\tfrac12,\ f(2|1)=0\) — note it still sums to 1, because it's a genuine probability distribution over X, just restricted to the slice where Y=1.

8.4Independence — the formal test

Test
\[ X \perp Y \iff f(x,y)=g(x)\,h(y) \quad \text{for every } (x,y) \]

Equivalently: \(f(x|y)=g(x)\) or \(f(y|x)=h(y)\) — conditioning changes nothing.

Worked example — independent case

Joint table with column sums 0.40/0.60 and row sums 0.25/0.50/0.25 reproduces every interior cell exactly as \(g(x)h(y)\) (e.g. \(0.40\times0.25=0.10=f(2,1)\)) — independent.

Worked example — dependent case

\(f(x,y)=\tfrac{x+y}{8},\ 0not independent. One mismatched point anywhere is enough to disprove independence.

Possible exam questions
From a joint table, compute the marginal distribution of X and verify it sums to 1.
Sum each column of the joint table (holding X fixed, summing over Y) to get g(x); the column sums by construction always total 1 if the joint table itself is valid.
Given \(f(x,y)=\tfrac{6}{5}(x+y^2)\) on the unit square, are X and Y independent?
\(h(y)=\tfrac65(\tfrac12+y^2)\) depends on y in a way that doesn't factor cleanly out of f(x,y) alongside a clean g(x) — check \(f(x|y)\) at two different y values; if it changes, they're dependent. (Working through it: it is not independent.)

Brainstorm

Independence is a strict "for every point" claim — a single mismatched cell in a joint table is enough to disprove it, but you need to check every cell to prove it. On an exam, if you're asked to test independence and spot even one \(f(x,y)\ne g(x)h(y)\), you can stop immediately and declare "not independent" — no need to check the rest.
