Chapter 1 · Central Tendency

1.1  Introduction

Why do we need a single number to describe a whole dataset — and which number should it be?

Background

Measures of central tendency show the tendency of some central value around which data tends to converge. Instead of staring at hundreds of raw observations, we collapse them into one representative figure that speaks for the whole mass of data. Think of it as the "center of gravity" of your dataset.

Key Points
  • Objective 1: obtain one single value that describes the characteristics of the entire dataset.
  • Objective 2: make it easy to compare two or more datasets at a glance.
  • There are five classical measures: Arithmetic Mean, Median, Mode, Geometric Mean, Harmonic Mean.
  • Central tendency tells you where the data sits — it says nothing about how spread out it is (that's dispersion) or what shape it has (that's Chapter 2).
  • No single measure is "best" universally — the right choice depends on data type and whether outliers are present.

1.2  Arithmetic Mean

The workhorse average — total the values, divide by the count.

Background

The arithmetic mean, often just called "the mean," is the total of all observed values divided by how many observations there are. It's the measure most people mean when they say "average," and it uses every single data point — which is both its strength (efficient use of information) and its weakness (sensitive to outliers).

Formula — Ungrouped Data
$$\bar{x} = \dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} X_i = \dfrac{\sum X}{N}$$
Formula — Grouped / Frequency Data
$$\bar{x} = \dfrac{\sum f X}{\sum f} = \dfrac{\sum fX}{N}$$
N = total frequency = Σf
Short-cut (Step-Deviation) Method — for grouped data
$$\bar{x} = A + \left(\dfrac{\sum fd}{N}\right)\times h \qquad d = \dfrac{X-A}{h}$$
A = assumed mean (pick the mid-value of a central class)  ·  h = class width  ·  d = step deviation
Key Points
  • Direct method is fine for a handful of classes; the short-cut method saves arithmetic when values are large or classes are numerous — and gives an identical answer.
  • The mean uses every observation, so one extreme outlier can pull it noticeably off-center.
  • Mean is the natural choice for symmetric, roughly bell-shaped data without extreme outliers.

1.3  Median

The value that sits exactly in the middle once the data is sorted — immune to outliers.

Background

The median is the middle value of a dataset arranged in ascending (or descending) order. Unlike the mean, it doesn't care how extreme the tail values are — only how many observations lie above and below it. That single property makes it the go-to measure whenever a distribution is skewed or contains outliers (income, house prices, wait times).

Ungrouped Data

Sort the data first, then count N observations:

$$N \text{ odd:}\quad \text{Median}=\left(\dfrac{N+1}{2}\right)^{\text{th}} \text{observation}$$
$$N \text{ even:}\quad \text{Median}=\dfrac{\left(\frac{N}{2}\right)^{\text{th}}+\left(\frac{N}{2}+1\right)^{\text{th}}}{2}$$
Grouped Data
$$\text{Median} = L + \left(\dfrac{\frac{N}{2}-c.f.}{f}\right)\times h$$
L = lower boundary of the median class (the class containing the (N/2)th observation)
c.f. = cumulative frequency just before the median class
f = frequency of the median class  ·  h = class width

Worked Example

Problem

Weights of 150 students are distributed as below. Find the median.

Weight30–4040–5050–6060–7070–8080–90
Frequency18374527158
Cum. Freq.1855100127142150

$N/2 = 75$. The 75th observation falls in class 50–60 (cumulative reaches 100 there, having been 55 just before) — this is the median class.

So $L=50,\; c.f.=55,\; f=45,\; h=10$:

$$\text{Median}=50+\left(\dfrac{75-55}{45}\right)\times 10 = 50+4.44$$

Median ≈ 54.44 kg
Key Points
  • Median is robust — a single huge outlier barely nudges it.
  • Best choice for describing "typical" income, house price, or exam score when the distribution is skewed.
  • The median is also called the second quartile (Q₂) and the 50th percentile (P₅₀) — a preview of the next topic.

1.4  Quartiles, Deciles & Percentiles

The median cuts data in half — these cut it into finer slices.

Background

If the median splits ordered data into two equal halves, why stop there? Quartiles split it into four parts, deciles into ten, and percentiles into a hundred. All three are members of the same family — "partition values" — and they share one general formula, just with a different divisor.

General Formula — Grouped Data
$$Q_i = L+\left(\dfrac{\frac{iN}{4}-c.f.}{f}\right)h \;\; (i=1,2,3) \qquad D_i = L+\left(\dfrac{\frac{iN}{10}-c.f.}{f}\right)h \;\; (i=1..9)$$
$$P_i = L+\left(\dfrac{\frac{iN}{100}-c.f.}{f}\right)h \;\; (i=1..99)$$
Same L, c.f., f, h meaning as the median formula — only the target rank (iN/4, iN/10, iN/100) changes. Q₂ = D₅ = P₅₀ = Median.

Worked Example

Problem

Profits (in lakhs) earned by 100 companies. Find Q₁, Median (Q₂), and Q₃.

Profit20–3030–4040–5050–6060–7070–8080–9090–100
No. Companies481830151087
Cum. Freq.4123060758593100

Q₁: target = N/4 = 25th obs → class 40–50 (L=40, c.f.=12, f=18, h=10)
$Q_1 = 40+\left(\frac{25-12}{18}\right)10 = 47.22$

Median: target = N/2 = 50th obs → class 50–60 (L=50, c.f.=30, f=30, h=10)
$Q_2 = 50+\left(\frac{50-30}{30}\right)10 = 56.67$

Q₃: target = 3N/4 = 75th obs → class 60–70 (L=60, c.f.=60, f=15, h=10)
$Q_3 = 60+\left(\frac{75-60}{15}\right)10 = 70$

Q₁ = 47.22   Q₂ = 56.67   Q₃ = 70 (lakhs)
Key Points
  • Quartiles/deciles/percentiles are essential for grading, ranking, and standardized scoring — "you scored in the 90th percentile" is a percentile statement.
  • The interquartile range Q₃−Q₁ (met again in dispersion) captures the spread of the "middle 50%" of the data, ignoring extreme tails.
  • These same values feed directly into Bowley's coefficient of skewness in Chapter 2.
Exam Radar — likely questions
  1. Compute Q₁ and Q₃ (or a specified decile/percentile) for a grouped frequency table.
  2. Which percentile is numerically identical to the median? Justify.
  3. Distinguish clearly between a quartile, a decile, and a percentile.

1.5  Mode

The most crowded value — the only average that works on categories, not just numbers.

Background

The mode is simply the value that occurs most frequently — the peak of the crowd. For ungrouped data you can often spot it by eye; for grouped data, we locate the "modal class" (the class with highest frequency) and interpolate using the crowding on either side of it.

Grouped Data
$$\text{Mode} = L + \left(\dfrac{f_1}{f_1+f_2}\right)\times h$$
L = lower boundary of the modal class (highest frequency)
f₁ = (modal freq.) − (frequency of preceding class)
f₂ = (modal freq.) − (frequency of following class)
h = class width

Worked Example

Problem

Same 150-student weight table as before — find the mode.

Weight30–4040–5050–6060–7070–8080–90
Frequency18374527158

Highest frequency = 45, in class 50–60 → modal class. $L=50,\;h=10$

$f_1 = 45-37 = 8 \qquad f_2 = 45-27=18$

$$\text{Mode}=50+\left(\dfrac{8}{8+18}\right)\times 10 = 50+3.08$$

Mode ≈ 53.08 kg
Key Points
  • Mode can be non-unique — a bimodal distribution often hints at two underlying subgroups mixed together (e.g. men's and women's shoe sizes combined).
  • It's the only central tendency measure that works on purely categorical/nominal data (e.g. "most popular product color").
  • Can be undefined if every value appears with equal frequency.

1.6  Geometric & Harmonic Mean

Two specialist averages — one for growth rates, one for rates and speeds.

Background

The arithmetic mean assumes values combine by addition. Some real quantities don't — growth rates compound multiplicatively, and speeds over fixed distances average reciprocally. Geometric Mean (GM) and Harmonic Mean (HM) are built for exactly those situations.

Geometric Mean
$$GM = \text{Antilog}\left(\dfrac{\sum \log X}{N}\right) \quad\text{(ungrouped)} \qquad GM=\text{Antilog}\left(\dfrac{\sum f\log X}{N}\right)\quad\text{(grouped)}$$
Harmonic Mean
$$HM = \dfrac{N}{\sum\left(\frac{1}{X}\right)} \quad\text{(ungrouped)} \qquad HM=\dfrac{N}{\sum\left(\frac{f}{X}\right)}\quad\text{(grouped)}$$
Key Points
  • GM is the right tool for average growth rates, ratios, and index numbers — anything that compounds.
  • HM is the right tool for averaging rates (speed, price-per-unit) when the "other side" (distance, quantity) is held equal.
  • The classical inequality: $AM \ge GM \ge HM$, with equality only when all values are identical.
Exam Radar — likely questions
  1. State a real scenario where GM is preferred over AM, and explain why.
  2. A car covers equal distances at two different speeds — compute the correct "average speed" and explain why it isn't the simple arithmetic mean.
  3. State the AM–GM–HM inequality and the condition for equality.
Brainstorm

A car drives the first half of a trip at 40 km/h and the second half (equal distance) at 60 km/h. Most people instinctively guess the average speed is 50 km/h. Work through why it's actually the harmonic mean (≈48 km/h) — and where the intuition goes wrong.

1.7  Empirical Relation Between Mean, Median & Mode

A shortcut that lets you back out any one of the three averages from the other two.

Background

For moderately skewed, unimodal distributions, an empirical (observation-based, not derived from first principles) relationship links the three central-tendency measures tightly enough to be genuinely useful — especially when computing one of them directly is inconvenient.

The Relation
$$\text{Mode} = 3\,\text{Median} - 2\,\text{Mean}$$
Rearranged forms: Mean = (3·Median − Mode)/2  ·  Median = (2·Mean + Mode)/3
Worked Example — applying the relation

From the weight distribution used earlier, we found Median ≈ 54.44 and Mode ≈ 53.08. Estimate the mean using the empirical relation instead of recomputing Σfd from scratch.

$$\text{Mean}=\dfrac{3(54.44)-53.08}{2}=\dfrac{163.32-53.08}{2}=\dfrac{110.24}{2}$$

Estimated Mean ≈ 55.12 kg
Key Points
  • This is an approximation, not an identity — exact only for perfectly symmetric distributions (where all three collapse to one value) and reasonably accurate for mild skew.
  • It breaks down for heavily skewed or multimodal data — don't lean on it there.
  • Handy in exams as a cross-check: compute mean and median directly, then verify the mode you calculated is in the right ballpark.
Exam Radar — likely questions
  1. State the empirical relation and use it to find the missing measure given the other two.
  2. For a perfectly symmetric distribution, show the empirical relation holds trivially.
  3. Under what conditions does this relation become unreliable?
Brainstorm

If Mean = Median = Mode for a dataset, what does that already tell you about its skewness — before you've computed anything in Chapter 2?

Chapter 2 · Skewness, Moments & Kurtosis

2.1  Introduction: Beyond Center and Spread

Central tendency tells you where. Dispersion tells you how spread out. Neither tells you the shape.

Background

Central tendency gives us the central value; dispersion gives us the amount of variation. Neither one tells us how that variation is arranged around the center. Two distributions can share the same mean and the same standard deviation yet look completely different — one symmetric, one lopsided. That's the gap skewness fills. Measures of variation tell us the amount of variation; measures of skewness tell us the direction of variation.

Key Points — the three shape signatures
  • Symmetrical distribution: Mean = Median = Mode.
  • Positively skewed (tail stretches right): Mean > Median > Mode.
  • Negatively skewed (tail stretches left): Mean < Median < Mode.

2.2  Measures of Skewness

Three ways to put a number — sign and magnitude — on asymmetry.

Background

Knowing a distribution is "skewed" is qualitative. To compare skewness across datasets, or to say precisely how skewed, we need a coefficient: a signed number that's zero for symmetry, positive for a right tail, negative for a left tail.

Karl Pearson's Coefficient of Skewness
$$S_k = \dfrac{\text{Mean}-\text{Mode}}{S.D.} \qquad \text{(alt., when mode is ill-defined)} \quad S_k = \dfrac{3(\text{Mean}-\text{Median})}{S.D.}$$
S_k > 0 → positively skewed  ·  S_k < 0 → negatively skewed  ·  S_k ≈ 0 → symmetric
Bowley's (Quartile) Coefficient of Skewness
$$S_k = \dfrac{Q_3+Q_1-2\,\text{Median}}{Q_3-Q_1}$$
Kelly's (Percentile) Coefficient of Skewness
$$S_k = \dfrac{P_{90}+P_{10}-2\,P_{50}}{P_{90}-P_{10}}$$

Worked Example

Problem

Profits of 1000 companies. Calculate Karl Pearson's coefficient of skewness.

Profit (Lakhs)100–120120–140140–160160–180180–200200–220220–240
Companies17531991943272082

Using A = 170, h = 20, N = 1000: Σfd = 393, Σfd² = 1741.

$$\text{Mean}=170+\left(\dfrac{393}{1000}\right)20 = 177.86$$

Modal class 180–200 (highest freq. 327): L=180, f₁=327−194=133, f₂=327−208=119, h=20
$$\text{Mode}=180+\left(\dfrac{133}{252}\right)20 = 190.56$$

$$S.D.=h\sqrt{\dfrac{\sum fd^2}{N}-\left(\dfrac{\sum fd}{N}\right)^2}=20\sqrt{1.741-0.154}=25.20$$

$$S_k = \dfrac{177.86-190.56}{25.20}=\dfrac{-12.70}{25.20}$$

Sₖ ≈ −0.50

The mode exceeds the mean by about 50% of one standard deviation — a moderately negatively skewed distribution.

Key Points
  • All three coefficients are unit-free — that's what makes them comparable across datasets measured in different units.
  • Rule of thumb: |Sₖ| under ~0.5 → mild skew; 0.5–1 → moderate; above 1 → strong skew.
  • Pearson's version needs the mode (can be unreliable if the modal class is ambiguous); Bowley's and Kelly's sidestep that by using only ranked positions.

Chapter 2 · Skewness, Moments & Kurtosis

2.3  Moments

One unifying family of quantities that ultimately produces mean, variance, skewness AND kurtosis.

Background

A moment is a descriptive measure that, taken together with others in the family, characterizes a distribution's shape uniquely. Rather than treating mean, variance, skewness and kurtosis as four unrelated formulas, moments reveal them as the 1st, 2nd, 3rd and 4th moments of the same underlying idea.

Raw Moments (about an assumed value A)
$$m_r' = \dfrac{1}{N}\sum X^{'r} \;\text{(ungrouped)} \qquad m_r' = \dfrac{1}{N}\sum f X^{'r} \;\text{(grouped)}, \quad r=1,2,3,4$$
For grouped data via step-deviation: compute Σfdʳ/N in d-units first, then convert to original units by multiplying by hʳ.
Central Moments (about the mean x̄)
$$\mu_r = \dfrac{1}{N}\sum f(X-\bar{x})^r, \quad r=1,2,3,4$$
μ₁ = 0 always (deviations from the mean cancel out)
μ₂ = Variance (σ²) — the familiar spread measure
μ₃ → feeds the skewness coefficient
μ₄ → feeds the kurtosis coefficient
Key Points
  • Raw moments about an assumed value are just a computational stepping-stone — like the step-deviation trick from Section 1.2 — not meaningful on their own.
  • Central moments are what actually describe shape: μ₂ = spread, μ₃ = asymmetry, μ₄ = peakedness.
  • This is why moments matter: the same machinery that gave you the mean, extended one step further, hands you skewness and kurtosis for free.
Exam Radar — likely questions
  1. Define raw moments and central moments, and state why μ₁ is always zero.
  2. What familiar quantity does the 2nd central moment μ₂ represent?
  3. Why do we compute moments about an assumed mean first, rather than the true mean directly?

2.4  Relation Between Raw and Central Moments

The conversion formulas that turn "moments about A" into "moments about the mean."

Background

We compute raw moments m₁′, m₂′, m₃′, m₄′ about an assumed mean A because it's arithmetically convenient (exactly the step-deviation logic from Section 1.2). Binomial expansion of (X−x̄) in terms of (X−A) gives clean conversion formulas to get from raw moments to the central moments we actually want.

Conversion Formulas
$$\mu_2 = m_2' - (m_1')^2$$
$$\mu_3 = m_3' - 3m_1'm_2' + 2(m_1')^3$$
$$\mu_4 = m_4' - 4m_1'm_3' + 6(m_1')^2 m_2' - 3(m_1')^4$$

2.5  Kurtosis

How peaked, or how flat, is the distribution compared to the normal curve?

Background

Kurtosis refers to the degree of flatness or peakedness in the region about the mode of a frequency curve, measured relative to the bell-shaped normal curve. It's an entirely separate question from skewness — a distribution can be perfectly symmetric and still be unusually peaked or unusually flat.

Key Points — three kurtosis types
  • Mesokurtic — same peakedness as the normal curve ("meso" = middle). This is the baseline.
  • Leptokurtic — more peaked, with heavier tails than normal ("lepto" = slender/narrow). Think: a tall, narrow mountain.
  • Platykurtic — flatter and more spread out than normal ("platy" = broad). Think: a wide, flat plateau.

Chapter 2 · Skewness, Moments & Kurtosis

2.6  Measuring Skewness & Kurtosis Using Moments

Where μ₃ and μ₄ finally cash out into precise, comparable numbers.

Background

Central moments μ₃ and μ₄ carry shape information, but they aren't unit-free — their scale depends on the units of X. β and γ coefficients standardize them by dividing out powers of μ₂ (the variance), making them comparable across any dataset.

Skewness Coefficients
$$\beta_1 = \dfrac{\mu_3^2}{\mu_2^3} \qquad \gamma_1 = \sqrt{\beta_1} = \dfrac{\mu_3}{\mu_2^{3/2}}$$
β₁ is always ≥ 0 (magnitude only, no direction — it's squared). γ₁ carries the sign of μ₃: γ₁ = 0 → symmetric, γ₁ > 0 → positively skewed, γ₁ < 0 → negatively skewed.
Kurtosis Coefficients
$$\beta_2 = \dfrac{\mu_4}{\mu_2^2} \qquad \gamma_2 = \beta_2 - 3$$
The normal curve has β₂ = 3 exactly, which is why γ₂ ("excess kurtosis") is centered at zero. γ₂ > 0 → leptokurtic, γ₂ = 0 → mesokurtic, γ₂ < 0 → platykurtic.

Worked Example — the full capstone calculation

Problem

Weekly earnings (tk) of employees. Find the first four moments, convert to central moments, then compute γ₁ and γ₂ and comment.

Earnings50–7070–9090–110110–130130–150150–170170–190
Employees481220673

A = 120, h = 20, N = 60. Step-deviation sums: Σfd = −11, Σfd² = 141, Σfd³ = −41, Σfd⁴ = 825.

Raw moments (original units): m₁′ = −3.667, m₂′ = 940, m₃′ = −5466.67, m₄′ = 2,200,000

$$\mu_2 = 940-(-3.667)^2 = 926.56$$

$$\mu_3 = -5466.67-3(-3.667)(940)+2(-3.667)^3 = 4774.84$$

$$\mu_4 = 2200000-4(-3.667)(-5466.67)+6(-3.667)^2(940)-3(-3.667)^4 \approx 2{,}195{,}104$$

$$\gamma_1 = \dfrac{4774.84}{926.56^{1.5}} \approx 0.17 \qquad \gamma_2 = \dfrac{2{,}195{,}104}{926.56^2}-3 \approx -0.44$$

γ₁ ≈ +0.17 (mild positive skew)   γ₂ ≈ −0.44 (platykurtic)

The distribution is slightly skewed to the right — not perfectly symmetric — and, since γ₂ < 0, it is platykurtic (flatter than the normal curve).

Key Points
  • γ₁ (not β₁) is the one you report when direction matters — β₁ can't distinguish left-skew from right-skew.
  • γ₂ = 0 is the normal-curve benchmark; report γ₂ (excess kurtosis), not raw β₂, unless asked specifically.
  • This is the same μ₂, μ₃, μ₄ pipeline from Section 2.4 — moments aren't a side-topic, they're the engine behind every shape statistic in this chapter.

2.7  Self-Check Quiz

Five more questions to lock in the chapter before the formula sheet.

1. Karl Pearson's coefficient of skewness is computed as −0.62. This indicates:
Negative Sₖ means Mean < Mode, i.e. the tail stretches to the left — negatively skewed.
2. Which central moment is ALWAYS equal to zero?
μ₁ = (1/N)Σf(X−x̄) — deviations from the mean always sum to zero by definition of the mean.
3. A distribution has γ₂ (excess kurtosis) = +0.8. It is:
γ₂ > 0 means β₂ > 3 — more peaked with heavier tails than normal, i.e. leptokurtic.
4. Why is β₁ = μ₃²/μ₂³ alone not enough to describe skewness?
β₁ tells you magnitude of asymmetry but not which way it points — you need γ₁ = √β₁ signed by μ₃'s sign for direction.
5. Bowley's coefficient of skewness uses:
Bowley's formula is (Q₃+Q₁−2·Median)/(Q₃−Q₁) — purely quartile-based, no mean or mode needed.

Formula Sheet

Arithmetic Mean

$$\bar{x}=\dfrac{\sum fX}{N}$$
$$\bar{x}=A+\dfrac{\sum fd}{N}h,\; d=\dfrac{X-A}{h}$$

Median (grouped)

$$Me=L+\dfrac{\frac{N}{2}-c.f.}{f}h$$

Mode (grouped)

$$Mo=L+\dfrac{f_1}{f_1+f_2}h$$

Quartile / Decile / Percentile

$$Q_i=L+\dfrac{\frac{iN}{4}-c.f.}{f}h$$

Geometric & Harmonic Mean

$$GM=\text{Antilog}\left(\dfrac{\sum f\log X}{N}\right)$$
$$HM=\dfrac{N}{\sum(f/X)}$$

Empirical Relation

$$Mo = 3\,Me-2\bar{x}$$

Karl Pearson's Skewness

$$S_k=\dfrac{\bar{x}-Mo}{S.D.}$$

Bowley's Skewness

$$S_k=\dfrac{Q_3+Q_1-2Me}{Q_3-Q_1}$$

Raw & Central Moments

$$m_r'=\dfrac{\sum fd^r}{N}\times h^r \qquad \mu_r=\dfrac{\sum f(X-\bar{x})^r}{N}$$

Raw → Central Conversion

$$\mu_2=m_2'-(m_1')^2$$
$$\mu_3=m_3'-3m_1'm_2'+2(m_1')^3$$
$$\mu_4=m_4'-4m_1'm_3'+6(m_1')^2m_2'-3(m_1')^4$$

Moment Skewness

$$\beta_1=\dfrac{\mu_3^2}{\mu_2^3} \qquad \gamma_1=\dfrac{\mu_3}{\mu_2^{3/2}}$$

Moment Kurtosis

$$\beta_2=\dfrac{\mu_4}{\mu_2^2} \qquad \gamma_2=\beta_2-3$$
Last-minute sanity checks
  • μ₁ is always 0. If yours isn't, recheck your mean.
  • AM ≥ GM ≥ HM — if this order breaks, recheck your arithmetic.
  • Normal curve: γ₁ = 0, γ₂ = 0 (β₂ = 3). Any dataset "close to normal" should hover near these.
  • Q₂ = D₅ = P₅₀ = Median — always, by definition.
