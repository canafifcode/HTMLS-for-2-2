Why software needs a process at all

Before any diagram, sit with the one idea everything else here builds on.

Software is embodied knowledge — it starts out scattered across a client's head, a competitor's product, a stack overflow thread, your teammate's intuition, and nobody's documentation. It is incomplete before you begin, on purpose: nobody has fully thought it through yet.

So building software is not a manufacturing task where you assemble known parts. It's a social learning process — the team and the client learn what the product actually needs to be while building it, and that learning happens through iteration, not through a single moment of perfect specification.

A process model is simply the technique you use to break that learning into distinct, manageable phases — a visual road map for organizing both the project (schedules, people, risk) and the product (what gets built, in what order).

Professor's framingEvery process model you'll see today is really just a different answer to one question: "When do we let reality — client feedback, a working build, a discovered risk — correct our assumptions?" Waterfall answers "at the very end." Agile answers "every couple of weeks." Keep asking that question as you go through each model and the whole unit organizes itself.
Common exam confusionStudents often conflate three different things that this course keeps separate: a framework activity (one of 5 universal jobs — Communication, Planning, Modeling, Construction, Deployment), a process flow (how those 5 activities are sequenced — linear, iterative, evolutionary, parallel), and a prescriptive process model (a named, complete recipe — Waterfall, Incremental, Prototyping, Spiral — that picks one of those flows and commits to it). Sheet 01 covers the first two; Sheet 02 covers the third.

02

Prescriptive process models

Named, complete recipes built on top of the flows you just saw. Four models, each solving for a different amount of uncertainty.

Waterfall

A strictly linear flow. Requirements are gathered once, up front, and the team walks straight through Communication → Planning → Modeling → Construction → Deployment without looping back.

Use when: requirements are well-known and stable Best for: small, well-understood projects
Advantages
  • Simple to use and implement
  • Easy, linear workflow to manage
  • Manageable precisely because requirements are locked before work starts
Disadvantages
  • Hard for clients to state all requirements up front
  • Testing and client evaluation happen last → risk surfaces late, when it's expensive
  • No built-in room for iteration
Exam trap"High risk" in Waterfall doesn't mean the model is risky to build — it means risk is discovered late. If a requirement was misunderstood, you won't find out until Construction or Deployment, after most of the budget is already spent.

Incremental

Ship a core product first (basic requirements only), collect client feedback, then ship increment after increment — each one running the full 5-activity pipeline again and adding functionality — until the final version is complete.

Use when: client wants working software with basic features, fast
Advantages
  • Flexible to changing requirements
  • Errors caught early — client assesses after every increment
  • Working software available from the early stage
  • Easy to test in pieces; lower initial cost
Disadvantages
  • Deciding how to slice the product into increments is genuinely hard
  • Total project cost ends up high
  • Needs complete upfront planning despite the iteration
  • Refining requirements each round can strain the architecture

Evolutionary models

Used when requirements will keep changing and a full product can't be finished by the deadline — but a limited version can ship early. Unlike Incremental (requirements fixed upfront, refined each round), evolutionary models let requirements evolve.

Prototyping model

Build a quick, rough prototype from currently-known requirements → client evaluates it → feedback and new requirements roll into the next prototype → repeat until it evolves into an acceptable final product.

+ active client involvement+ no need for detailed upfront requirements
– can slow the process down– frequent changes add complexity– dissatisfaction can scrap prototypes entirely

Spiral model

Also delivers a series of increasingly mature versions — but the defining feature is that every loop starts with risk analysis. Loop 1 usually yields a basic prototype; each later loop matures the product further, spiraling outward until it's acceptable.

+ rigorously tackles project risk+ changes absorbed even late in the process
– costly and complicated– needs real risk-assessment expertise
The question examiners actually ask"How is Spiral different from Prototyping if both build a series of versions?" Answer: risk analysis is the spiral's defining ring. Prototyping cycles communication → quick design → build → feedback with no formal risk step. Spiral inserts planning-with-risk-analysis into every single loop — that's the one fact that separates the two models on paper.

03

Why Agile, and what it actually values

Every model above is slow by modern-business standards. Agile is the industry's answer to "the requirements will keep moving faster than any of those models can react."

Agile literally means "moving quickly." The emphasis shifts to rapid, incremental delivery and continuous, active communication with the client — and correspondingly away from heavy upfront analysis, design, and documentation.

Practically: the project team recognizes its own plans are short-lived. Some features survive, some change, some get dropped — so the team leans its energy toward Construction more than Planning or Modeling, and lets the product emerge through small increments shaped by client feedback.

A myth to kill on sightAgile does not mean "no planning, no documentation, just start coding." That's the cynical Dilbert-strip version. Real Agile still plans and still documents — just in small, frequent, disposable doses instead of one giant upfront phase.

04

Agile process models

Three named agile flavors, each answering "how do we actually run the sprint-to-sprint machinery?" a little differently.

Extreme Programming (XP)

Kent Beck's idea: whatever engineering practices are known to help, push them to the extreme, from day one. XP prioritizes customer satisfaction above all, values simplicity, constant feedback, and mutual respect — and famously uses pair programming: two developers sharing one machine, designing, coding, and testing a single user story together.

Nested planning/feedback loops — the outer ring plans in months, the innermost ring iterates in seconds. That compression is the whole point of "extreme."

Advantages
  • Simple code that stays easy to modify
  • Testing from day one → more genuinely agile delivery
  • Energizing, high-morale team environment
Disadvantages
  • Extreme focus on coding can crowd out design, hurting product quality
  • Thin documentation risks repeating the same mistakes later

Scrum

Named after the rugby formation where players pack tightly to move the ball forward together. It's the most widely used agile model in industry today — lightweight, iterative, incremental, built around a fixed-length cycle called a sprint.

A sprint (2 weeks – 1 month) pulls items from the product backlog into a sprint backlog, then the team runs Planning → Implementation → Review → Retrospective, with a Daily Scrum stand-up synchronizing progress every single day. Unfinished sprint-backlog items return to the product backlog for next time.

Advantages
  • Flat hierarchy → developers get real freedom to self-organize
  • Built for adapting to rapid requirement changes
  • Faster time-to-deliverable
  • Heavy client involvement; retrospectives catch mistakes before they repeat
Disadvantages
  • Works well mainly with small project teams

Kanban

Japanese for "signboard." Where Scrum works in timeboxed sprints, Kanban is a continuous flow — no sprints, no iterations. Every task becomes a card on a shared board; anyone free "pulls" the next card from left to right as they have capacity.

Try it — drag a card rightward (or tap the → button), then compare what happens with a full board vs. a lean one.

The lesson this board is built to teachNotice how slow "In Progress" clears when the To-Do column is packed — that's a work-in-progress (WIP) problem. The original slide shows it directly: overload every column and the pipeline crawls; keep the workload per card lean and the same board finishes fast. Kanban's whole discipline is limiting how much is "in progress" at once.
Advantages
  • Encourages leadership from every participant — flatter, more expressive team culture
  • Adapts to almost any team's working style
  • Board makes lead time visible, so workload per card can be tuned
Disadvantages
  • No fixed timeframe → a project can drift without a deadline forcing function
Scrum vs. Kanban — the one-line answerScrum = fixed-length sprints with a daily stand-up and a retrospective ritual. Kanban = continuous pull, no sprints, discipline enforced by limiting work-in-progress instead of by a clock.
Jira — built by Atlassian, Jira is the issue-tracking tool most teams actually run their Scrum or Kanban board inside; it's tooling, not a process model itself.
