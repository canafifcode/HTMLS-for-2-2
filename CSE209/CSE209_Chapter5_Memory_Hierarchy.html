Large and Fast: Exploiting Memory Hierarchy

Every idea in this chapter is really just one idea, dressed up in four or five different costumes: keep the stuff you need close, and only go far away when you absolutely have to. That's it. That's the whole chapter. Everything else — caches, tags, associativity, page tables, TLBs, coherence — is just the engineering detail of making that one idea work automatically, in hardware, billions of times a second, without you ever noticing it's happening.

So instead of jumping straight into circuit diagrams, we're going to keep coming back to one picture throughout this guide: you, pulling an all-nighter before an exam.

You've got facts memorized in your head, a few papers on your desk, a backpack under the table, a bookshelf across the room, a store-room down the hall, and the BUET central library across campus. Every single concept in this chapter — hits, misses, tags, associativity, page tables, TLBs, even multi-core cache coherence — has a direct, obvious equivalent in that scene. We'll build it up piece by piece, and by the end the "hardware version" will feel like the obvious way to do it, not a wall of new vocabulary.

1 · The Big Picture §5.1 Introduction

Why a "memory hierarchy" exists at all — and it starts with something you already do every time you study.

Start With What You Already Do

Picture yourself solving a stack of Signals and Systems problems at 2am. You don't spread every book, notebook, and past paper you own evenly across your desk. Instead, without even thinking about it, you keep the one formula sheet you're currently using right in front of you, and you keep flipping back to the same page over and over. When you move to the next problem, you usually reach for a page near the one you just used — not a completely random chapter from a different course.

That instinct — keep re-using what you just used, and grab things near it — has a name in computer architecture: the principle of locality. It's not a hardware trick. It's just an observed fact about how real programs (and real students) behave: at any given moment, you only care about a small slice of everything that exists. The entire memory hierarchy — every idea in this chapter — is hardware built specifically to bet on that instinct and win.

🎒 The running example — your all-nighter

Here's the scene we'll keep coming back to: you're studying at home, the exam is tomorrow. There's a stack of places your notes could physically be sitting right now, and they differ enormously in how fast you can get to them:

  • In your head — formulas you've already memorized. Instant. Zero delay.
  • On your desk — the two or three papers you're actively using right now. Reach out, grab it, done in a second.
  • In your backpack under the table — a bit more stuff, but you have to unzip it and dig around. A few seconds.
  • On your room's bookshelf — more books than your bag can hold. You have to get up and walk over. Maybe 20 seconds.
  • In the store-room down the hall — old semester's books, boxed up. A proper trip. A minute or two.
  • The BUET central library, across campus — has basically everything that's ever been published. Getting there and back could eat twenty minutes of your night.

Notice the pattern: the closer something is, the less of it you can fit, and the more expensive (per book) that shelf-space is to build. Your desk is tiny, but it cost you nothing to clear a spot on it. The central library holds a nation's worth of knowledge, but building and running a library is a massive undertaking compared to owning a desk. That trade-off — small-and-fast vs. big-and-cheap — is exactly the trade-off every memory technology in a computer faces too, and it's why no single technology can be both.

Two Kinds of Locality

Split that studying instinct into its two components, because both get used constantly for the rest of the chapter:

  • Temporal locality — "I just used this, I'll probably need it again very soon." Think of a formula you re-derive from three times in one problem, or a loop counter that's touched on every single pass through a loop.
  • Spatial locality — "I just used something on this page, I'll probably need the stuff right next to it too." Think of reading a chapter start to finish, or a program walking through consecutive elements of an array.

A subtle but important point: when you grab a formula sheet off your desk, you don't tear out and carry just the one line you need — you pick up the whole page. That's not laziness, it's efficiency: since spatial locality means you'll probably want the neighbouring formulas soon anyway, grabbing the whole page up front saves you a second trip. Hardware does exactly this too — memory is never moved one byte at a time between levels; it's always moved as a chunk. That chunk has a name.

And here's the mechanism that makes it all self-organizing, with no one "in charge" deciding what goes where: whatever you just touched (and the stuff physically near it) automatically gets pulled up closer to you — a page you keep re-reading migrates from "in the store-room" to "on my desk" simply by virtue of you fetching it and leaving it out. Nobody pre-plans your desk's contents at the start of the night; it just fills up with whatever you've actually needed recently. A cache works exactly the same way — it's not programmed with what to hold, it just accumulates whatever was recently, or spatially nearby, requested.

The Vocabulary, Attached to the Analogy

Every term below maps onto something you already intuitively understand from the desk scenario — the formal definition is really just giving a name to something you already do:

  • Block (a.k.a. line) — the "whole page," not just the one line — the unit that actually gets moved when something migrates up a level. A block can hold several words, exactly like a page holds several formulas.
  • Hit — you reach for something and it's already on your desk. Hit ratio = hits / accesses — what fraction of the time you didn't have to get up.
  • Miss — you reach for it, it's not there, and you have to go fetch it from further away. Miss ratio = misses / accesses = 1 − hit ratio.
  • Miss penalty — literally, how long that fetching trip costs you. Grabbing from your backpack costs a few seconds; a library run costs twenty minutes. Same idea, very different penalty depending on which level you had to go to.
📌 What actually happens on a miss

Say you reach for your desk and the page isn't there. You don't just give up — you 1) realize it's missing (miss), 2) go get it from wherever it actually is — backpack, shelf, or worse (this trip is the miss penalty), and 3) bring it back and put it on your desk before using it. Notice you still end up reading it from your desk, even after a miss — the miss just describes the extra fetching step that had to happen first. Hardware behaves identically: even a value that missed the cache is still ultimately delivered to the CPU from the cache, one level closer than where it started.

📖 One thing worth appreciating before we go further

Here's the part that should feel almost unfair once it clicks: your CPU has no idea any of this is happening. A single load instruction looks completely identical whether the value was sitting right there in L1 or had to be dragged all the way from disk after a page fault. The instruction set never even mentions "cache" or "which level" — correctness never depends on where the data currently lives. Only speed depends on it. That's precisely why this whole hierarchy can exist without programmers having to manage it by hand: it's a performance illusion sitting quietly underneath a program that has no idea it's there.

Self-check: If a cache sees 1,000 accesses with a hit ratio of 0.97, how many misses occurred, and what is the miss ratio?
Miss ratio = 1 − 0.97 = 0.03. Misses = 0.03 × 1000 = 30 misses (hits = 970, and 970 + 30 = 1000 checks out). In desk terms: out of 1000 times you reached for something, 30 times it wasn't on your desk and you had to get up.
Self-check: Why can't your whole "study setup" just be one giant desk — why bother with a backpack, shelf, and library at all?
Because desk space is expensive in a way library space isn't — a desk demands everything on it be instantly grabbable, which is a much stricter (and costlier) requirement than "eventually findable." Physically, SRAM (used for caches/your desk) needs about 6 transistors per bit just to hold its state without decaying, versus DRAM's single transistor-plus-capacitor per bit — so a desk-sized amount of SRAM is already expensive, and a library-sized amount would be unaffordable and enormous. You get both speed and size only by accepting that the "fast" part has to be small.
💭 Brainstorm

You grab the whole formula page, not just one line — because spatial locality means the neighbouring formulas are likely wanted soon too, and a second trip back to the same spot would be wasteful. Now push that idea to a silly extreme: what if your "page" was actually your entire notebook — every time you needed one formula, you hauled the whole 200-page notebook onto your desk? Would your desk still feel "fast"? What have you gained, and what have you quietly lost? Hold onto this — we come back to it with real numbers in §3.

2 · Memory Technologies §5.2

Why your "desk" and your "library" have to be built out of physically different materials — you can't just build a bigger desk.

Why You Can't Cheat and Just Build a Bigger Desk

It's tempting to think "fine, let's just make SRAM cheaper and build the whole memory system out of it — then everything's fast." The reason that doesn't work comes down to how each technology physically stores a single bit:

  • SRAM (what your desk — the cache — is built from) holds a bit using a small loop of transistors actively fighting to keep their state. It never forgets, never needs topping up, and responds instantly — but that constant "actively holding a pose" costs roughly six transistors per bit. Six transistors, for one 1-or-0. That's an expensive way to store anything at scale.
  • DRAM (your backpack/shelf/store-room — main memory) is almost lazy by comparison: one transistor and one tiny capacitor per bit. The capacitor just holds a charge — cheap, dense, lets you pack a huge amount into a small chip. The catch: that charge slowly leaks away, like air seeping out of a balloon, so the whole chip must be periodically refreshed — read out and written straight back — purely to top the charge up before it's lost. SRAM never has this problem; DRAM always does.
  • Magnetic disk (the library) doesn't store bits electrically at all — it stores them as microscopic magnetized spots on a spinning platter, read by a physical arm that has to move. Astonishingly cheap per gigabyte, but bound by mechanical motion, which is why it's measured in milliseconds instead of nanoseconds.

DRAM Up Close: Living With a Leaky Bucket

Because a DRAM cell is just a capacitor slowly leaking charge, the whole chip needs a background "top-up" routine constantly running — that's the refresh cycle mentioned above. A few clever tricks squeeze much more performance out of this humble one-transistor design than you'd expect:

  • Bits sit in a rectangular array, and an entire row gets opened at once — like opening a whole drawer instead of picking out one item.
  • Burst mode — once a row is open, grab several words from it in a row with reduced extra delay, since you've already "opened the drawer."
  • Double Data Rate (DDR) DRAM sends data on both the rising and falling edge of the clock — basically getting two deliveries per clock tick instead of one, doubling bandwidth for free.
  • Row buffer, synchronous DRAM (SDRAM), and banking all chase the same goal from different angles: keep that opened "drawer" working for you as much as possible, and let multiple drawers be open in parallel, rather than re-paying the "open the drawer" cost on every single access.
📌 The DDR generational trend

Each DDR generation (DDR → DDR2 → DDR3 → DDR4…) roughly doubles bandwidth and lowers operating voltage versus its predecessor. The slide deck's historical DRAM-price table tells the same story numerically: 64 Kbit in 1980 cost about $1.5 million per GB; by 2007, 1 Gbit cost about $50 per GB. That's not a typo — it's the same exponential cost collapse Moore's Law predicts for logic, just applied to memory.

Dependable Memory: Why DRAM Needs Error Correction

Here's an unsettling fact about that leaky-bucket design: DRAM cells can occasionally be flipped by something as mundane as background cosmic radiation striking the chip — nothing broke, no wear occurred, a stray particle just deposited enough charge to silently turn a 0 into a 1. These are called soft errors, precisely because the hardware itself is fine; only the stored value is momentarily wrong.

Server and workstation memory defends against this with an error-correcting code (ECC) — a handful of extra redundant bits stored alongside each word that let the memory controller silently detect and fix a single flipped bit before your CPU ever sees it. The typical design point is SEC/DED (Single-Error-Correcting, Double-Error-Detecting): one flipped bit gets fixed automatically; two flipped bits in the same word get flagged as an error rather than corrected. It's the exact same "add redundancy to survive corruption" idea used in disk RAID and network checksums, just applied one word at a time.

⚠ Exam trap

Don't merge refresh and ECC into one idea — they solve two unrelated problems. Refresh fights a known, predictable, constant physical process (the charge always leaks, on a schedule). ECC fights a rare, unpredictable event (radiation striking at some random moment). A system can — and server-grade systems typically do — need both running simultaneously, for entirely different reasons.

Flash Storage — The Departmental Library, Not the Central One §6.4

Flash sits in an interesting middle spot: nonvolatile like disk (it remembers everything with the power off), but semiconductor-based like SRAM/DRAM, which makes it much faster than a spinning platter — roughly 100×–1000× faster than disk, smaller, lower power, with no moving parts to break. The trade-off: still more expensive per GB than disk (though cheaper than DRAM).

A flash cell physically wears out after roughly thousands of write/erase cycles — think of repeatedly folding a single sheet of paper until it tears along the crease. This is why flash can't simply replace RAM or disk everywhere. Wear leveling is the fix: the controller deliberately rotates writes across less-used cells, like spreading the folding damage evenly across many sheets instead of destroying one. If asked "why not use flash for everything," write-endurance — not just cost — is the answer being fished for.

Disk Storage — The Actual Trip Across Campus §6.3

Magnetic disk is nonvolatile, rotating storage — physically the closest thing to "walking to the library." And just like a real trip, an access has several additive stages, each with its own delay:

  1. Queuing delay — waiting your turn if the library's already busy serving someone else's request (zero if it's empty/idle).
  2. Seek time — walking to the right shelf (moving the read/write head to the correct track).
  3. Rotational latency — waiting for the right book to spin around to where you're standing (the platter has to rotate the target sector under the head).
  4. Data transfer time — actually reading the pages off the book.
  5. Controller overhead — the librarian's fixed paperwork time, every single visit.
✎ Worked example — timing an actual "library trip"

Given: 512 B sector, 15,000 rpm, 4 ms average seek time, 100 MB/s transfer rate, 0.2 ms controller overhead, disk idle (no queue).

Rotational latency — on average, the book you want is half a rotation away when you arrive at the shelf:

Rotational latency = ½ ÷ (15,000/60 rev/s) = ½ ÷ 250 rev/s = 2 ms

Transfer time:

512 B ÷ 100 MB/s = 0.005 ms

Total average read time:

4 ms (seek) + 2 ms (rotation) + 0.005 ms (transfer) + 0.2 ms (controller) = 6.2 ms

And if the library staff already knows roughly where you tend to go (locality helping the "actual" average seek drop to 1 ms instead of the worst-case 4 ms average), the whole trip drops to 3.2 ms — nearly half, just from not wandering randomly.

⚠ Exam trap — rotational latency formula

Use ½ a revolution, never a full one — on average the target sector is halfway around when the head arrives. Also convert rpm → rev/s by dividing by 60 before inverting. Forgetting the ½ is the single most common arithmetic slip here, and it silently doubles your rotational-latency term.

Real disks soften the pain a little further: controllers remap physical sectors behind a clean logical interface (SATA/SCSI/ATA), and disks carry their own small on-board cache that prefetches — grabs sectors it guesses you'll want next, the same speculative "grab the whole page" instinct from §1, just implemented by the disk itself.

Self-check: Why does DRAM need a "row buffer" but SRAM does not?
DRAM's whole array is organized in rows that must be opened together, and reading a row is even destructive to the stored charge — so the row buffer captures an entire opened row, letting several words be read (or refreshed) without repeatedly re-opening it. SRAM cells hold their state actively and can be addressed individually with no "opening" step at all.
Self-check: A disk has 6ms average seek, 10,000 rpm, 200MB/s transfer, 0.1ms controller overhead, and reads a 2KB block from idle. Find the average access time.
Rotational latency = ½ ÷ (10000/60) = 0.5 ÷ 166.67 = 3 ms. Transfer time = 2048B ÷ 200MB/s ≈ 0.01 ms. Total = 6 + 3 + 0.01 + 0.1 = 9.11 ms.

3 · Direct-Mapped Caches §5.3 The Basics of Caches

Your desk needs an organizing rule — you can't just pile things randomly and hope to find them again fast. Direct mapping is the simplest possible rule.

Give Your Desk Labeled Slots

Imagine your desk isn't just open space — it has exactly 8 labeled slots cut into it, like a mail sorter. Every subject you might need has a rule that sends it to exactly one specific slot, no exceptions, no choice involved. That's a direct-mapped cache:

Cache index = (Block address) modulo (#Blocks in cache)

Because the number of slots is always a power of two, this "modulo" is completely free in hardware — no division circuit needed, you just read off the low-order bits of the address and that number is the slot. It's the equivalent of a rule like "subject code mod 8 tells you which drawer" — mechanical, instant, no thinking required.

How You Know What's Actually in a Slot

Here's the catch with only 8 slots: far more than 8 subjects exist, so lots of different subjects are all assigned to the same slot number. Slot 3 might currently hold your Signals notes — or it might hold your Software Engineering notes instead — the slot number alone doesn't tell you which. So every slot also carries a small label: the tag, recording exactly which subject is sitting there right now. And for a slot that's simply never been used yet, a valid bit (1 = something's actually here, 0 = empty) stops you from confidently reading garbage out of an empty drawer.

Hit = valid bit set and the tag matches what you're looking for
Miss = slot empty, or the tag says it's the wrong subject
⚠ Exam trap

A tag mismatch is still a miss, even with the valid bit set to 1 — that's the "wrong subject is currently sitting in this drawer" case. Students sometimes check only the valid bit and forget the tag has to match too. Both conditions are required for a hit.

Look at step 8 again. Address 26 was a hit just two steps earlier — you'd just used it, it was sitting right there, textbook temporal locality. So far each "page" was one word. Real blocks usually hold several words, which splits the address into three fields instead of two:

✎ Worked example — Larger Block Size

Given: 64 blocks, 16 bytes/block. Question: which block does byte address 1200 map to?

Block address = Byte address ÷ Bytes/block = 1200 ÷ 16 = 75
Cache index = Block address mod #Blocks = 75 mod 64 = 11

With a 32-bit address: offset needs 4 bits (2⁴=16 bytes/block), index needs 6 bits (2⁶=64 blocks), leaving 32−6−4 = 22 bits for the tag.

Why "Grab a Bigger Page" Isn't Always Better

Back to the brainstorm from §1 — what if you grabbed your whole notebook instead of one page? Bigger blocks capture more spatial locality per fetch, which sounds like a pure win. But your desk is a fixed size: if each item you place on it is now a whole notebook instead of a single page, far fewer distinct items fit at once. Fewer slots filled means more subjects competing for the same drawers — conflict misses go up, not down, even though each individual fetch is "smarter." And each miss now costs more too, since you're hauling a whole notebook back each time instead of one page (a bigger miss penalty). Real hardware softens this with tricks like early restart (start working the moment your specific word arrives, don't wait for the whole block) and critical-word-first (fetch the actually-requested word first, rest of the block after) — but the underlying trade-off never goes away: there's a sweet spot, and going too big backfires.

Self-check: In the worked trace above, would address 18 have conflicted with address 26 on a fully-associative desk (any slot allowed) of the same total size?
No. On a fully-associative desk, address 26's data could simply sit in a different slot, with no forced eviction based on shared slot numbers — as long as the desk isn't completely full. This exact miss (one that "would not occur in a fully associative cache of the same total size") is precisely the textbook definition of a conflict miss, formalized in §12.
Self-check: A direct-mapped cache has 32 blocks (5-bit index) and 8 bytes/block (3-bit offset), for a 32-bit address. How many tag bits, and what index/tag does byte address 0x00000148 produce?
Tag bits = 32 − 5 − 3 = 24. 0x148 = 0000 0001 0100 1000₂. Offset (low 3 bits) = 000; Index (next 5 bits) = 01001 = 9; the remaining 24 high-order bits form the tag.

4 · Handling Misses & Write Policies §5.3

Reading from your desk is one thing — but what happens the moment you actually correct something on a page? Does the "official" copy back on the shelf need to know right away?

What Happens the Instant You Reach for Something Missing

On a hit, nothing dramatic happens — you just keep working. On a miss, you actually pause what you're doing (the pipeline stalls), go fetch the block from further away, and then continue: if it was an instruction you needed, you simply resume fetching where you left off; if it was data, you finish the load or store now that it's finally on your desk.

Write-Through: Sprint to Fix the Master Copy Immediately

Say you spot an error in a formula and fix it on your desk copy. The most cautious policy: immediately run down the hall and correct the store-room's master copy too, so the two never disagree even for a second. Safe — but if "running down the hall" costs real time, doing it after every single correction adds up brutally fast.

✎ Worked example — the cost of sprinting every time

Base CPI = 1, 10% of instructions are stores, and writing to memory costs 100 cycles:

Effective CPI = 1 + 0.10 × 100 = 11

CPI 1 → CPI 11 is an 11× slowdown — obviously unworkable. The fix is a write buffer: instead of sprinting yourself, you drop the correction in an outgoing mail tray and keep working; someone else delivers it to the store-room in the background. You only actually stall if that tray is already full.

Write-Back: Just Fix Your Desk Copy, Deal With It Later

The lazier (and usually smarter) policy: correct only your desk copy, and don't bother the store-room at all — yet. Just stick a little flag on that page marking it "dirty" (edited, doesn't match the official copy anymore). Only when that page is finally about to leave your desk (evicted to make room for something else) do you bother walking it back and updating the master copy — and if it was never marked dirty, you don't even need to; the store-room's copy was already correct, so you can just toss your desk copy and move on.

Write Allocate: Do You Even Bother Fetching It First?

A different question entirely: you're about to overwrite a whole page you don't currently have on your desk — do you bother walking to fetch the old version first, just to immediately scribble over it?

  • For write-through: two reasonable choices — write allocate (fetch it onto your desk first, then write) or write around / no-write-allocate (just send the new version straight to the store-room, don't bother bringing the old page to your desk at all). Write-around makes sense because you're often about to overwrite the entire page anyway (like zero-initializing a fresh worksheet) — fetching the old contents first would be pure wasted effort.
  • For write-back: you usually do fetch it, because you expect to keep editing that page for a while, and having it on your desk means all those future edits get the fast write-back treatment instead of each one separately racing to the store-room.
⚠ Exam trap

Write-allocate and write-back are independent choices, even though they're usually paired together (write-back + write-allocate, write-through + no-write-allocate are the common real-world combos). An exam can absolutely ask about write-through + write-allocate as a valid, if less common, combination — don't assume they're welded together.

Self-check: Why does write-back need a "dirty" flag, but write-through never does?
Write-through keeps the store-room's master copy current after every single edit, so by the time a page leaves your desk, the master is already correct — nothing needs writing back. Write-back deliberately lets your desk copy drift ahead of the master; the dirty bit is exactly the note-to-self that says "this one's different from the official version, don't discard it without updating the master first."
Self-check: With base CPI = 1, 15% stores, write-through with a write buffer that only actually stalls the CPU 2% of the time (each stall costing 50 cycles), what is the effective CPI?
Effective CPI = 1 + 0.15 × 0.02 × 50 = 1 + 0.15 = 1.15. The write buffer converted almost all of what would have been a 100-cycle-per-store penalty into background work you never wait for — you only pay when the outgoing tray is already full.

5 · Measuring Cache Performance: AMAT & CPI §5.4

How much of your total study time is actually spent studying, versus how much quietly vanishes into walking back and forth?

The Core Idea: Every Trip Adds to Your Night

If you never had to get up from your desk, one problem would take you a fixed, predictable amount of time — call that the base CPI of "an ideal, always-on-your-desk study session." But you do have to get up sometimes, and every single trip tacks extra time onto the night:

Actual CPI = Base CPI + Memory-stall cycles per instruction
Memory-stall cycles per instruction = Miss rate × Miss penalty  (per access type)
✎ Worked example — combining instruction and data trips

Given: I-cache miss rate = 2%, D-cache miss rate = 4%, miss penalty = 100 cycles (same for both), base CPI (ideal cache) = 2, loads & stores are 36% of instructions.

Every single instruction requires fetching the instruction itself (100% of the time you're "reading the next line of your problem set"), but only some of those instructions also touch data (36% are loads/stores — "look something up or jot something down"):

I-cache stall cycles/instr = 1.00 × 0.02 × 100 = 2
D-cache stall cycles/instr = 0.36 × 0.04 × 100 = 1.44
Actual CPI = 2 (base) + 2 (I) + 1.44 (D) = 5.44

A perfect, no-trips-ever version of you would finish 5.44 / 2 = 2.72× faster — the walking-around time alone costs more than the actual problem-solving time!

✎ Follow-up — you get smarter, but your desk doesn't get closer

Now suppose you personally get faster at solving each step (base CPI drops from 2 to 1 — better focus, better technique), but nothing about your desk/backpack/shelf setup changes at all:

Actual CPI = 1 + 2 + 1.44 = 4.44

The ideal version of you is now 4.44× faster than the real you — an even bigger relative gap. And the fraction of your night eaten by walking around actually rose:

3.44 / 5.44 = 63%  →  3.44 / 4.44 = 77%

That's the whole lesson of this section in one sentence: the faster you personally get, the more your unchanged desk setup dominates your night. A brilliant student with a badly-organized desk still loses most of the night to walking around — you cannot judge total study time while ignoring how the desk is arranged.

⚠ Exam trap

Miss rate is given per access to that specific cache, not per instruction — that's exactly why the D-cache term needs the extra ×0.36 (only 36% of instructions even touch data) while the I-cache term doesn't (100% of instructions fetch an instruction). Forgetting to weight the D-cache miss rate by the load/store fraction is the single most common mistake on this style of question.

Average Memory Access Time (AMAT)

A close cousin of the CPI calculation — instead of a full breakdown, this collapses everything into one number: "on average, how long does one reach-for-something actually take, counting the occasional trip?"

AMAT = Hit time + Miss rate × Miss penalty
✎ Worked example — AMAT

CPU with a 1ns clock, hit time = 1 cycle, miss penalty = 20 cycles, I-cache miss rate = 5%:

AMAT = 1 + 0.05 × 20 = 2 ns = 2 cycles per instruction

Here "2 cycles" and "2 ns" happen to be the same number only because the clock is exactly 1ns — don't assume that shortcut holds in general; always check whether a question wants cycles or time.

Why This Keeps Mattering More, Not Less

Three separate trends all push in the same direction — cache behaviour becomes more important as CPUs improve, never less:

  • You get faster at the actual work (lower base CPI) → the walking-around time becomes a bigger share of the total (63% → 77%, as above).
  • Your clock ticks faster (higher clock rate) → since the real-world delay of a trip to the shelf doesn't shrink just because you're thinking faster, that same trip now "costs" more cycles than it used to.
  • Put together: as the CPU side of the equation keeps improving, the memory side becomes the bottleneck you can no longer ignore.
Self-check: Base CPI = 1.5, I-cache miss rate = 3%, D-cache miss rate = 8%, miss penalty = 50 cycles, loads/stores = 30% of instructions. Find actual CPI.
I-cache: 1.00 × 0.03 × 50 = 1.5. D-cache: 0.30 × 0.08 × 50 = 1.2. Actual CPI = 1.5 + 1.5 + 1.2 = 4.2.
Self-check: Clock = 2 GHz (0.5 ns/cycle), hit time = 1 cycle, miss penalty = 40 cycles, miss rate = 4%. What is AMAT in both cycles and nanoseconds?
AMAT = 1 + 0.04 × 40 = 2.6 cycles. In time: 2.6 × 0.5 ns = 1.3 ns. Keep "cycles" and "ns" as separate answers until a problem explicitly ties them together via the clock period.

6 · Associativity §5.4

The fix for the "slot fight" from §3: stop forcing every subject into exactly one drawer.

Give Each Subject a Small Set of Allowed Drawers

Remember addresses 18 and 26 endlessly evicting each other in §3, purely because they were both assigned to the same single drawer? The obvious fix: instead of one fixed drawer per subject, allow a small set of drawers, and let the subject go in whichever one of that set happens to be free.

  • Direct mapped (1-way) — what we had: exactly one legal drawer. Cheapest, fastest to check, worst at avoiding fights.
  • n-way set associative — the desk is grouped into sets, each set containing n drawers. A subject's set is still fixed by its address, but within that set it can land in any of the n drawers. You check all n drawers in the set at once (n comparators — more hardware, but bounded and manageable).
  • Fully associative — no sets at all, a subject can go absolutely anywhere on the whole desk. But now finding it again means checking the label on every single drawer — one comparator per drawer, which gets expensive fast as the desk grows.

That's exactly why real L1 caches typically stop at 2-way to 8-way instead of chasing fully associative: past a certain point, you're paying for a lot of extra comparator hardware to chase a shrinking benefit.

Choosing Who Gets Evicted

When a set is full and something new needs a spot, who leaves?

  • Direct mapped: no decision to make — there's only ever one slot.
  • Set associative: prefer an empty drawer if one's available in the set; otherwise pick among the occupied ones using:
    • Least Recently Used (LRU) — evict whichever item hasn't been touched the longest. Easy to track for 2-way, still manageable for 4-way, but tracking a full "who's oldest" ordering across 8+ drawers gets expensive fast.
    • Random — just pick one arbitrarily. Surprisingly, at high associativity this performs almost as well as true LRU, for a fraction of the hardware cost.
⚠ Exam trap

"More associativity always means better performance" is false as a blanket statement. More freedom lowers miss rate, but checking more drawers at once also costs real hardware — more comparators, a wider mux to pick the matching one — which can increase access time. Whether overall performance actually improves depends on whether the miss-rate savings outweigh that slower hit time — the same trade-off logic that reappears in §12's design table.

Self-check: Why does fully-associative placement need more comparators than set-associative, and why does that limit how big a fully-associative cache can practically be?
Fully associative has to check every single drawer's label at once, since the item could be anywhere — one comparator per drawer. Set-associative only ever checks the drawers within one already-determined set (n comparators, n = associativity). As the desk grows, fully-associative comparator count grows right along with it — which is exactly why fully-associative designs are only practical for small structures (like a TLB), never for a multi-megabyte L2/L3 cache.

7 · Multilevel Caches §5.4

Continuing the brainstorm from §5: what if, before making the full trip to the store-room, you checked your backpack first?

Insert a Middle Step

Right now our story only has two speeds: "on my desk" (fast) or "all the way to the store-room" (slow). Real desks don't work that way, and neither do real computers — there's almost always a backpack under the table as a middle ground:

  • Primary (L1) cache — your desk. Small, but built purely for speed — you want zero delay reaching it.
  • Level-2 (L2) cache — your backpack. Bigger than the desk, a little slower to dig through, but still miles faster than a store-room trip.
  • Main memory — the store-room, reached only when even the backpack doesn't have it.
  • High-end systems add an L3 — think of it as a shared shelf in the hallway that your whole household uses, one more stop before the store-room.
Fig. 4 — Each level only ever gets touched when the level above it comes up empty.
✎ Worked example — adding a backpack (L2 cache)

Given: CPU base CPI = 1, clock rate = 4 GHz (cycle time = 0.25 ns), miss rate to L1 = 2% per instruction, main-memory access time = 100 ns.

Step 1 — desk only, no backpack:

Miss penalty = 100 ns ÷ 0.25 ns/cycle = 400 cycles
Effective CPI = 1 + 0.02 × 400 = 9

Step 2 — now add the backpack (L2), 5 ns access time, with a global miss rate to main memory of just 0.5% (i.e. only 0.5% of all instructions ever have to make the full store-room trip):

Backpack-check cost (paid on every desk miss) = 5 ns ÷ 0.25 ns = 20 cycles
Extra cost when the backpack also fails = 400 cycles (still have to go to the store-room)
CPI = 1 + (0.02 × 20) + (0.005 × 400) = 1 + 0.4 + 2 = 3.4

Versus the desk-only design: 9 / 3.4 ≈ 2.6× faster — one backpack more than doubled your effective study speed. That's how much a single extra cache level is worth in real processors.

⚠ Exam trap — local vs. global miss rate

The 2% figure is "how often the desk fails," measured against all instructions. The 0.5% figure is the global backpack-miss rate — also measured against all instructions, not just the ones that already failed the desk. The local backpack miss rate (the more "intuitive" one — of the times you actually opened the backpack, how often did that fail too?) would instead be 0.5% ÷ 2% = 25%. Every multilevel-cache CPI formula in this course multiplies the global rate directly against total instructions — mixing up local and global is the single most common way to wreck this calculation. If a question ever gives you a local rate instead, convert first: global rate = local rate × (miss rate of the level above).

Different Levels, Different Priorities

  • L1 (the desk): optimise for minimal hit time — it's checked on literally every access, so keep it small and dead simple.
  • L2 (the backpack): optimise for low miss rate instead — its whole job is to prevent the expensive store-room trip, and since it's already only reached after a desk-miss, its own speed matters comparatively less.
  • Consequence: L1 ends up smaller than a single-level design would use, and L1's "pages" (block size) are typically smaller than L2's — small pages keep the desk fast, while the backpack can afford bigger pages to grab more spatial locality per trip.

What If You're Multitasking? (Out-of-Order CPUs)

A modern CPU doesn't necessarily freeze completely while waiting on a miss — much like you wouldn't just sit frozen waiting for a page to arrive from your backpack. A pending write-down waits in your "to-write" pile; steps that depend on the missing page wait their turn; but steps that don't need it can carry on regardless. This makes the real-world cost of any given miss depend heavily on exactly what else was going on at the time — too tangled for one clean formula, which is why architects fall back on full simulation to evaluate this kind of overlap accurately.

Self-check: In the worked example, what would CPI become if the backpack-check itself got slower — 10ns instead of 5ns — with everything else unchanged?
New backpack penalty = 10 ns ÷ 0.25 ns = 40 cycles. CPI = 1 + (0.02 × 40) + (0.005 × 400) = 1 + 0.8 + 2 = 3.8. Notice the store-room term (0.005 × 400 = 2) doesn't change at all — only the desk-miss/backpack-hit term does, since that's the only term that depends on the backpack's own speed.

8 · Software Optimization: Blocking §5.4

Everything so far has been about hardware decisions. This section is about you — how the order you choose to work through material changes how often you're forced to get up.

How much you have to walk around isn't just decided by desk size and drawer rules — it's also decided by how you choose to work. A canonical, very real example from scientific computing: matrix multiplication (specifically DGEMM, Double-precision GEneral Matrix Multiply), a routine used constantly in simulation and graphics.

The Naive Approach: Sweep Everything, Every Time

for (int j = 0; j < n; ++j)
{
  double cij = C[i+j*n];
  for (int k = 0; k < n; k++)
    cij += A[i+k*n] * B[k+j*n];
  C[i+j*n] = cij;
}

Think of this as reviewing your syllabus by scanning the entire textbook for every single sub-question before moving to the next one — technically thorough, but by the time you circle back to page 1, whatever was on your desk from that visit is long gone. The goal of blocking is exactly what your studying instinct already tells you to do: get as much use as possible out of material while it's still sitting on your desk, before it gets swept off to make room for something else. In the naive version, as the matrix (n) grows large, the total ground you cover before returning to reuse anything can vastly exceed what your desk can hold — producing a wave of avoidable capacity misses.

The Fix: Study in Small, Complete Chunks

The smarter approach: instead of sweeping across the whole syllabus in one giant pass, break your revision into small BLOCKSIZE × BLOCKSIZE chunks that do fit comfortably on your desk, and fully finish one chunk — squeezing out every bit of reuse — before moving to the next:

#define BLOCKSIZE 32
void do_block(int n, int si, int sj, int sk, double *A, double *B, double *C)
{
  for (int i = si; i < si+BLOCKSIZE; ++i)
    for (int j = sj; j < sj+BLOCKSIZE; ++j)
    {
      double cij = C[i+j*n];          /* cij = C[i][j] */
      for (int k = sk; k < sk+BLOCKSIZE; k++)
        cij += A[i+k*n] * B[k+j*n];   /* cij += A[i][k]*B[k][j] */
      C[i+j*n] = cij;                 /* C[i][j] = cij */
    }
}
void dgemm(int n, double* A, double* B, double* C)
{
  for (int sj = 0; sj < n; sj += BLOCKSIZE)
    for (int si = 0; si < n; si += BLOCKSIZE)
      for (int sk = 0; sk < n; sk += BLOCKSIZE)
        do_block(n, si, sj, sk, A, B, C);
}
Unoptimized — full row/column sweep
Blocked — small tile reused repeatedly
Fig. 5 — Unblocked DGEMM roams across a whole strip of "syllabus" before anything can be reused; blocking confines your attention to one small, fully-digestible chunk that stays on your desk throughout.
📌 Why this actually works

Blocking touches nothing about the desk itself — it's a pure study-habit change, not a hardware upgrade. By shrinking your working set at any given moment to fit what your desk can actually hold (choosing BLOCKSIZE with your desk's real size in mind), you get to reuse everything on it many times over while it's still sitting right there, converting what would have been repeated store-room trips into quick desk-reaches.

Self-check: If BLOCKSIZE is chosen far too large — bigger than your desk can hold all three subjects' worth of material simultaneously (A, B, and C) — what happens to the benefit of blocking?
The benefit shrinks or disappears entirely: if the A, B, and C chunks no longer all fit on your desk at once, pieces start getting swept off before you've actually finished reusing them — you're right back to capacity misses, just organized slightly differently. Picking BLOCKSIZE correctly requires knowing your actual desk (cache) size — which is exactly why real high-performance math libraries auto-tune their block size for each specific CPU they run on.

9 · Virtual Memory Fundamentals §5.7

Zoom out from one student's desk to an entire hall of residence, with hundreds of students sharing the same building — and needing to never see each other's stuff.

The New Problem: Sharing the Building Safely

Everything so far has been about one student (one program) and their own desk-to-library hierarchy. Now picture an entire dorm: hundreds of students (programs) all sharing the same physical building (physical memory) at once. Two new problems appear immediately that never came up for a single student alone:

  • Sharing — the hall office needs to hand out storage space to everyone fairly, without students needing to know or care which actual room number they've been given.
  • Protection — Student A should never be able to wander into Student B's room and read (or worse, scribble on) their notes, whether by accident or on purpose.

Virtual memory solves both at once: every student gets their own private, personal numbering system for their stuff — their virtual address space — and the hall office (working with the CPU hardware) silently translates that private numbering into wherever their things are actually sitting in the real building, on the fly, every time. VM uses main memory itself as a "cache" for disk, in exactly the same hit/miss spirit as before — just one more level removed, and now with an administrator in the loop.

Cache "block" ↔ VM page
Cache "miss" ↔ VM page fault
📖 Why this got invented in the first place

Historically, three separate headaches showed up together the moment computers started running several programs at once: programs needed relocation (able to run without knowing in advance which physical room would be free), the system needed protection (one student's bug shouldn't corrupt another's notes), and programmers wanted the freedom to write something bigger than the physical building could hold at once, without personally managing an overflow warehouse. Virtual memory's private-numbering-plus-translation trick solves all three simultaneously with one mechanism — which is exactly why it's a joint hardware/OS design: translation itself is naturally hardware-speed, while deciding who gets what room and enforcing the rules is naturally an OS/administrator decision.

Addresses Split Just Like Before

Memory is divided into fixed-size pages (commonly 4 KB) — think of a page as one "room" in the dorm. A virtual address splits into a virtual page number (which room, in the student's own private numbering) and a page offset (where within that room — this part never changes, since a room's internal layout doesn't move just because the room itself got reassigned). Translation only ever has to convert the room number; the "where inside the room" part passes straight through unchanged.

Fig. 6 — Only the "which room" portion of the address is translated; "where inside the room" never changes.

Page Faults Are the Worst-Case Trip Imaginable

A page fault means the room's contents aren't even in the building anymore — they've been shipped off to a remote warehouse (disk), and someone has to physically go retrieve them, costing millions of cycles, handled entirely by OS software rather than hardware (building dedicated fast hardware for something this rare and this slow simply isn't worth it). Because a page fault is so catastrophically expensive, VM design goes to lengths that would be wasteful anywhere else:

  • Fully associative placement — a page can go in any free room in the building. The "search cost" of checking everywhere is trivial next to a multi-million-cycle warehouse trip, so there's no reason not to take the benefit.
  • Smart (software) replacement — since getting it wrong is so expensive, it's worth spending real time choosing carefully who gets evicted.
  • Always write-back, never write-through — writing to the remote warehouse after every single edit would be absurd; only ship a room's contents back when it's actually being vacated.
⚠ Exam trap

If fully-associative search is so expensive for hardware caches (§6), why is it suddenly fine here? Because the cost comparison flips completely: a cache's search must finish in a fraction of a nanosecond on every single access, so extra comparators are a real, constant tax. A page-table search is done by software, and only happens on a page fault — an event so rare and already so expensive (millions of cycles regardless) that the extra software search time is essentially free by comparison. Same idea (search everywhere to cut misses), completely different cost trade-off.

The Hall Office's Ledger: Page Tables

A page table is simply the hall office's ledger — an array of page table entries (PTEs), one per virtual page a student owns, indexed by that student's private room number. A page table register in the CPU points to wherever this particular student's ledger currently lives in physical memory.

  • If the room is currently in the building: the PTE records the real physical room number, plus a couple of status flags (has it been visited recently? has it been written to?).
  • If the room isn't in the building: the PTE instead points to where it's stored in the remote warehouse.
📌 Choosing who to evict, and writing back — mirrors the cache exactly

Just like §6, VM prefers something LRU-like when a room must be freed up. Rather than tracking exact recency (too expensive at this scale), a simple reference bit in each PTE gets set to 1 whenever that room is visited, and is periodically cleared to 0 by the OS — a room whose reference bit still reads 0 clearly hasn't been visited "recently" (since the last clearing), giving a cheap approximate answer. On the write side: since a warehouse trip is so slow, entire rooms get shipped back as a whole, not item by item, and a dirty bit in the PTE tracks whether a room's contents were actually changed and therefore need shipping back before eviction — the exact same idea as the cache's dirty bit in §4, just one level further out.

Self-check: Why is write-through categorically ruled out for virtual memory, when it was merely "usually slower" for caches?
Write-through for a cache costs roughly a hundred extra cycles per store — expensive but survivable, especially with a write buffer. Write-through for virtual memory would mean shipping something to the remote warehouse on every single edit, at a cost of millions of cycles each time — the machine would become unusable. The gap between "in the building" and "at the warehouse" is so much bigger than the gap between desk and backpack that write-back isn't just preferred here, it's the only workable option.

10 · TLBs: Fast Address Translation §5.7

Asking the hall office "where's Room 482 actually located?" every single time you want to visit would be absurd. So... don't.

The Obvious Shortcut

Taken literally, every single memory access now seems to need two trips: one to the hall office to look up where a room really is, then a second trip to actually go there. That would double your effective travel time before you even start talking about hits and misses!

But here's the save: you keep asking about the same rooms over and over — the exact same locality instinct from §1, just applied to translations instead of data. So build a small, fast, dedicated lookup you keep in your pocket — a personal cheat-sheet of "room-number → real-location" pairs you've already looked up recently. That cheat-sheet is the Translation Look-aside Buffer (TLB).

What a TLB Miss Actually Tells You

A TLB miss just means your pocket cheat-sheet didn't have that room written down — it says nothing yet about whether the room even still exists in the building. Two very different outcomes follow:

  1. Room is in the building, you just hadn't noted it down — copy the entry from the office ledger (page table) into your cheat-sheet, then retry. Cheap: 10–100 cycles, handled in hardware or via a lightweight software exception.
  2. Room isn't in the building at all — this is a genuine page fault, and now the OS has to get involved for real.

Page Fault Handler, Step by Step

  1. Use the faulting address to identify the relevant ledger entry.
  2. Find where the contents actually are in the remote warehouse.
  3. Choose a room to clear out to make space.
  4. If that room's contents were edited (dirty), ship them to the warehouse first.
  5. Bring the requested contents into the newly-freed room, and update the ledger.
  6. Let the process resume.
  7. Restart execution from the instruction that faulted.
⚠ Exam trap

"TLB miss" and "page fault" are not the same event, though it's tempting to conflate them. Every page fault necessarily also misses the TLB — you can't have a cheat-sheet entry for a room that isn't even in the building. But the reverse is not true: the overwhelming majority of TLB misses simply find the entry sitting quietly in the office ledger, costing maybe 10–100 cycles, with no warehouse trip involved at all. Only when the ledger itself comes up empty does a genuine, multi-million-cycle page fault occur.

For a concrete anchor: the Intel Core i7 profiled in §14 implements exactly this "pocket cheat-sheet, then bigger backup cheat-sheet" pattern — a tiny 64-entry data TLB and 128-entry instruction TLB (both sub-cycle) backed by a larger, still-fast 512-entry unified L2 TLB. Only missing all the way out of that triggers the full page-table walk described above.

Self-check: Why does the handler restart the entire faulting instruction rather than just resuming mid-instruction from where the fault happened?
The memory access hadn't actually completed yet — nothing about that instruction's effects has been committed. Restarting the whole instruction is simple and correct: once the page is resident, the identical instruction re-executes, this time hitting on both the TLB and the (now-updated) page table, and completes normally.

11 · VM + TLB + Cache Together, and Protection §5.7

Three mechanisms, one hallway — and the rule that stops one student from ever wandering into another's room.

How the Layers Cooperate

Virtual memory and the cache aren't independent systems bolted together — they form one coordinated pipeline with a strict rule: nothing can be sitting on your desk unless the room it came from is currently in the building. The moment the hall office ships a room off to the warehouse, it actively enforces this:

  • It clears any desk contents belonging to that room the instant it's shipped out.
  • It updates the ledger and your cheat-sheet so that if you ever reach for that room again, you correctly trigger a fresh page fault instead of silently reading old, wrong leftovers.

A Typical Trip, End to End

A real access threads translation and caching together, and there's a neat shortcut: if the cache's "which drawer" bits fall entirely within the untranslated "where-in-the-room" portion of the address, the desk-drawer lookup can start at the same time as the office-ledger lookup, since that part of the address never changes during translation — only the final label-check has to wait for the translated room number.

📌 Four possible outcomes for one access

1) Cheat-sheet hit + desk hit → fastest path, everything you needed was already close. 2) Cheat-sheet hit + desk miss → you knew where the room was, but its contents weren't on your desk — fetch from a lower cache level or memory. 3) Cheat-sheet miss + desk hit → surprisingly possible: the ledger walk finds the room, and it turns out its contents were already separately sitting on your desk from an earlier, differently-numbered visit. 4) Cheat-sheet miss + page fault → the slow path: an actual warehouse trip is required before anything else can happen.

Protection: Why You Can't Just Wander Into Someone Else's Room

Students can legitimately share parts of the building (a common study lounge, say) — but the system still has to guard against errant access, accidental or deliberate, into rooms a student has no business entering. This needs real enforcement, not just good manners, backed by hardware:

  • A privileged supervisor mode (kernel mode) — think of it as the hall warden's own authority level, distinct from any student's.
  • A set of privileged instructions — actions only the warden, never a student, is allowed to perform.
  • The office ledger itself and other critical records are accessible only in supervisor mode — an ordinary student simply has no key that opens the records room, full stop.
⚠ Exam trap

Protection here rests on hardware-enforced privilege levels, not "the OS being careful." If a student could execute warden-only actions or directly read the records room, no amount of clever translation logic would stop them from reading someone else's private notes. Always mention the hardware/software partnership when asked "how is memory protection achieved": hardware enforces the mode bit and instruction restrictions; the OS uses that enforcement to implement the actual security policy on top.

Self-check: Why must the office clear desk contents when a room is shipped to the warehouse, rather than just updating the ledger and leaving old desk copies alone?
If stale desk contents survived, a later access could return wrong data even after the ledger was correctly updated — the whole point of the "nothing on the desk without a valid room" rule is to guarantee this can never silently happen. Clearing on migration keeps that invariant airtight across the entire pipeline, no exceptions.

12 · A Common Framework & the 3 C's §5.8

Step back and notice: your desk, your backpack, and the entire dorm-room system have all been answering the exact same four questions this whole time, just with different answers.

Four Questions, Every Level

Whether you're looking at your desk, your backpack, the TLB cheat-sheet, or the whole virtual memory system, the same four design questions come up every time:

  1. Placement — where in the structure is a given item even allowed to go?
  2. Finding it — once it's there, how do you locate it again?
  3. Replacement — when something new needs room, who gets evicted?
  4. Write policy — how do edits eventually propagate down to the level below?

1 · Placement (determined by associativity)

  • Direct mapped — one fixed drawer, no choice.
  • n-way set associative — n choices, within one determined set of drawers.
  • Fully associative — anywhere on the whole desk.

More freedom lowers miss rate, but costs more hardware and, usually, more time to check.

3 · Replacement

  • LRU — evict whoever's been untouched the longest. Accurate, but the bookkeeping gets expensive fast at high associativity.
  • Random — nearly matches LRU's performance at high associativity, for far simpler hardware.
  • Virtual memory uses the reference-bit LRU approximation from §9 — true LRU across thousands of rooms would be absurdly expensive to track exactly.

4 · Write Policy

  • Write-through — sprint the correction down immediately; simple, but needs a write buffer to stay fast.
  • Write-back — fix your copy, flag it dirty, ship it back only on eviction.
  • Virtual memory: only write-back is feasible — a write-through warehouse trip on every edit would be unworkable.
📌 The one-sentence version of this whole chapter

Every level — desk, backpack, TLB, virtual memory — answers the same four questions, but the specific answer at each level is dictated entirely by one ratio: how bad is it if you get this wrong (the miss penalty) versus how much are you willing to spend to get it right (the search/associativity cost)? Your desk favours cheap, fast, approximate answers because a desk-miss only costs seconds. Virtual memory favours expensive, thorough answers because a page fault costs a warehouse trip measured in millions of cycles.

⚠ Exam trap — the precise test for "conflict miss"

The exact test: would this specific miss still have happened on a fully-associative desk of the same total size? If yes — it's a capacity miss (there genuinely wasn't room; more freedom wouldn't have helped). If no — only the rigid drawer rule caused it while total capacity was actually sufficient — it's a conflict miss. Revisit §3's trace: 18 and 26 fighting forever is textbook conflict, since a fully-associative desk of the same 4 slots handled the identical sequence with only 3 misses instead of 5.

Conflict misses. By hypothesis, the working set fits on a "more associative" desk of the same total capacity — so the direct-mapped desk's extra misses come purely from its rigid one-drawer-per-item rule, not from genuinely insufficient total space. That's exactly the definitional test above.
Self-check: Which of the 3 C's is fundamentally unavoidable, no matter how cleverly you design the desk?
Compulsory misses. The very first time you ever need a page, it can't already be on your desk — no organisation, associativity, or replacement policy can pre-load something you've genuinely never touched before. (Prefetching can hide the delay of a compulsory miss by guessing ahead, but it can't eliminate the miss itself.)

13 · Multiprocessor Memory: Coherence & Consistency §5.10

Now give two roommates each their own desk, sharing one set of official notes between them. A brand-new correctness problem shows up that never existed for a single student alone.

The Study-Group Problem

Suppose you and your roommate are prepping from the same shared master notes, and you've each photocopied a page onto your own desk (your own private cache) so you don't have to keep fighting over the original. Walk through this sequence:

Right after step 3, the master notes correctly say 1 (you dutifully write-through'd the correction) — but your roommate's photocopy still says 0. If they glance at their own desk, they get the wrong answer, even though "the master copy" was updated. Simply making both desks write-through wasn't enough — this is the cache coherence problem, and it only shows up the moment more than one desk exists.

Coherence, Defined Properly

Informally: everyone's reads should return the most recently written value. Formally, three conditions must all hold:

  1. You write X, then you read X with no one else writing in between → you get back what you just wrote.
  2. You write X, your roommate reads X sufficiently later (enough time for the update to actually propagate) → they get your written value. (This is precisely the rule your roommate's stale photocopy violated in step 3.)
  3. You and your roommate both write X → you both see the two writes land in the same order, so you converge on the same final value instead of silently disagreeing forever.

Two Ways to Keep Everyone in Sync

A coherence protocol has to manage two things: letting each roommate keep a local copy for speed (migration, reduces trips to the shared master), and letting both hold read-only copies at once without contention (replication). Two broad strategies exist:

Protocol familyKey ideaBest suited for
SnoopingEveryone in the room overhears everyone else's updates out loud. If you hear your roommate is about to correct a page you also have, you immediately update or cross out your own copy.A small room where everyone can genuinely hear everyone — small-scale systems on one shared bus.
Directory-basedA dedicated notice-board records who currently holds a copy of what. You check the board instead of shouting across the room every time.A huge shared library floor — large-scale systems, where "shouting to everyone" would be complete chaos.

The Fix: Invalidating Snooping

The common approach: before correcting a page, you must first announce "I'm about to change X — cross out your copies!" — an invalidate message. Anyone else holding that page marks their copy invalid; their next read of it correctly misses and fetches the fresh version instead of trusting stale ink.

⚠ Exam trap

Notice the master notes in Table 8 only get updated at the very last step, not the moment you corrected X — this trace assumes write-back-with-invalidation, where your desk supplies the fresh value directly on the next miss rather than forcing an immediate master update. Don't assume coherence always implies write-through; invalidating protocols pair naturally with write-back for exactly the bandwidth reasons from §4.

Consistency: A Subtler Question Than Coherence

Coherence tells you what a read of one address must return. Consistency asks something subtler: when does your correction become visible to your roommate, and in what order do corrections to different pages become visible relative to each other? A correction can't become visible to everyone instantaneously, so the rules need explicit assumptions:

  • A write only counts as "done" once everyone has actually seen it.
  • You never reorder your own writes relative to your other actions.

Consequence: if you correct X and then correct Y, anyone who sees your new Y is guaranteed to also see your new X (since X's correction logically came first, and writes never get reordered). Reads can be reordered — writes never can.

Self-check: In the broken Table 7 scenario, which specific formal coherence condition was violated?
Condition 2: "you write X, roommate reads X sufficiently later → they must see the write." You corrected X to 1 at step 3, but your roommate's next read (from their own stale desk) would still return 0 — a direct violation.
Self-check: Why is directory-based coherence preferred for large multiprocessor systems over snooping?
Snooping requires everyone to overhear every single update, which only works while the whole group genuinely shares one "loud enough to hear" bus. As the group grows, broadcasting every correction to everyone floods the shared channel. A directory instead keeps a record of exactly who's holding a copy of what, so you can notify only the specific roommates who actually need to know — avoiding the broadcast storm entirely.

14 · Real Designs, Pitfalls & Concluding Remarks §5.13 – §5.16

How the theory shows up in actual silicon, the classic mistakes examiners love to test, and — finally — the one-paragraph version of everything above.

Real Processors: Two Very Different "Desks"

Both the ARM Cortex-A8 and Intel Core i7 (the first-generation "Nehalem" i7, the chip this edition of the textbook profiles) build every idea from this chapter into real silicon: multiple on-chip cache levels, a 2-level TLB (exactly the pocket-cheat-sheet-plus-backup pattern from §10), and multi-banked caches supporting multiple accesses per cycle — as long as those accesses don't collide on the same bank. Concrete numbers make the abstractions click:

Two things fall directly out of ideas you already have. First, Cortex-A8 skips L3 entirely — it's a single/dual-core "one student, one desk" design; an L3 is only worth building once you've got several cores that all need a shared backpack before paying the full main-memory trip (exactly §7's logic, just extended to three levels and shared across roommates). Second, notice associativity climbs the further out you go in both designs (4-way → 8-way → 16-way) — precisely what §12's trade-off table predicts: outer levels can afford the extra drawer-checking hardware because their speed is already off the critical path, so architects spend that budget buying a lower miss rate instead.

Core i7-specific cache tricks worth remembering:

  • Return requested word first — critical-word-first from §3: don't make the CPU wait for the whole page if it only needs one line right now.
  • Non-blocking cache — keeps serving new requests even mid-miss, enabling the "keep working while waiting" overlap from §7.
  • Data prefetching — speculatively grabs data it predicts you'll want soon, before you've even asked — the hardware version of grabbing the next page before you've finished the current one.
📖 Textbook intuition — the whole hierarchy is invisible to the ISA

Worth restating now that you've seen two real, very differently-organized chips: no instruction in ARM or x86 ever names a cache level, a TLB, or a page table. A load looks identical whether it hits in L1 or walks all the way to a page fault. That's exactly why Cortex-A8 and Core i7 can implement wildly different setups (4-way vs. 8-way, with or without L3, one-level vs. two-level TLB) while both correctly run the exact same compiled binaries. Software only ever "feels" the hierarchy through timing — which is the whole reason the AMAT/CPI formulas from §5 exist in the first place.

Pitfalls

✎ Pitfall 1 — Byte vs. word addressing

Consider a 32-byte direct-mapped cache with 4-byte blocks (so 32 ÷ 4 = 8 total drawers). Which drawer does address 36 map to?

If 36 is a BYTE address: block = (36 ÷ 4) mod 8 = 9 mod 8 = block 1
If 36 is a WORD address (and a block = 1 word = 4 bytes): block = 36 mod 8 = block 4

Same number "36," two completely different answers, purely from an unstated assumption about the addressing convention. Nasty precisely because the arithmetic itself is trivial. Always confirm which convention a problem is using before touching a single formula.

✎ Pitfall 2 — Ignoring memory-system effects in your own code

Iterating over a 2D array column-by-column when it's stored row-major (as C arrays are) produces a huge stride between successive accesses — like insisting on reading page 1, then page 41, then page 81 of a book instead of reading it straight through. Row-by-row iteration gets unit stride and excellent spatial locality; column-by-column destroys it. This is exactly the motivation for blocking in §8 — locality-aware ordering is a free win that costs nothing but how you structure the work.

✎ Pitfall 3 — Shared caches in multiprocessors need MORE associativity, not less

When several cores share an L2/L3, if associativity is lower than the number of cores contending for it, you get inflated conflict misses — roommates fighting each other for the same limited drawers. As core counts rise, associativity has to rise too, or the shared cache becomes a bottleneck purely from cross-core interference.

✎ Pitfall 4 — AMAT breaks down for out-of-order processors

As established in §7, an out-of-order CPU can keep working on independent instructions during a miss — AMAT's simple additive model assumes every miss fully stalls everything, ignoring that overlap entirely. Using AMAT to compare out-of-order designs systematically overstates the true cost of misses; full system simulation is the correct tool here, not closed-form arithmetic.

✎ Pitfall 5 — Extending address range with segments

Historically (e.g. the Intel 80286), designers extended a small address space with segments — but a segment isn't always big enough for what a programmer wants to put in it, and segmented addressing badly complicates pointer arithmetic. Mostly a historical pitfall now, but it's a good illustration of why flat, large virtual address spaces won out over bolted-on segments.

✎ Pitfall 6 — Virtualizing an ISA that wasn't designed for it

Implementing a Virtual Machine Monitor (VMM) on an ISA never designed with virtualization in mind runs into trouble when non-privileged instructions can still touch hardware resources directly, breaking the VMM's illusion of isolation. The fix is either extending the ISA with proper virtualization support, or requiring guest OSes to avoid the problematic instructions.

Background, in one line: a VMM (hypervisor) is the same idea as virtual memory, one floor further up — where VM gives every process a private address space, a VMM gives every guest operating system the illusion of its own private, complete computer, while several guests actually share one physical machine. This pitfall is the exact same isolation-breaking failure as the protection problem from §11, just one level higher: protecting whole operating systems from each other, instead of protecting students from each other within one dorm.

Concluding Remarks — Back to the One Idea

Strip away every acronym in this chapter and you're left with the sentence we opened with: keep the stuff you need close, and only go far when you absolutely have to. Fast memory is small; large memory is slow; you want both at once, and caching is the trick that gives you the illusion of having both, built entirely on the observation that you only ever need a small slice of everything at any given moment. Stack enough "desks" on top of each other — L1 → L2 → … → main memory → disk — and the whole system behaves, on average, almost as if it were built entirely out of the fastest material at the very top, without you ever consciously managing where anything actually lives. And the moment you add a second student sharing the same building, memory-system design stops being purely a speed question and becomes a correctness question too — which is exactly why coherence and consistency earn their own first-class place in modern computer architecture.

Final self-check: A friend says "bigger blocks always reduce miss rate, so we should always use the biggest possible blocks." Where does this break down?
It ignores the block-size trade-off from §3/§12: on a fixed-size desk, bigger blocks mean fewer distinct items fit at once, which increases conflict competition and can eventually raise overall miss rate ("pollution") instead of lowering it — the classic U-shaped curve. It also ignores that bigger blocks increase the miss penalty itself (more to haul back per trip), which can hurt overall AMAT/CPI even in the cases where miss rate genuinely does fall. "Lower miss rate" and "better performance" are not automatically the same claim — Table 6 in §12 exists specifically to catch students who conflate the two.
