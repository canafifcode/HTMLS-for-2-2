§1.1 · Introduction

The Computer Revolution

Why a "computer architecture" course exists, and what has made computing the most transformative technology of the last sixty years.

Welcome to Chapter 1. Before we touch a single instruction or logic gate, we need to build the right mental model of why this field matters and where it is heading — because everything in CSE209 is really an answer to one question: how do we keep making computers faster, cheaper, and more capable, generation after generation?

1.1.1Progress in Computer Technology

The entire discipline of computer architecture is underpinned by one empirical observation, commonly called Moore's Law: the number of transistors that can be economically placed on an integrated circuit roughly doubles every two years. This is not a law of physics — it is an economic and engineering trend that the semiconductor industry has managed to sustain for decades through relentless innovation.

⚠ Exam Trap

Moore's Law is about transistor count doubling, not directly about "computers getting twice as fast." Students routinely write "performance doubles every two years" — that is a common but imprecise restatement. The precise statement is about transistor density/count. Performance gains follow as a consequence of more transistors (enabling more parallelism, more cache, more functional units), but the law itself is about counting transistors, not timing programs.

Because raw computing capability keeps improving, applications that were once science fiction become routine engineering. The slide deck highlights a few landmark examples of what this exponential progress made feasible:

  • Computers embedded in automobiles (engine control, safety systems, and now driver assistance)
  • Cell phones — from bulky analog handsets to full computers in your pocket
  • The Human Genome Project — sequencing 3 billion base pairs was only tractable because of massive gains in processing and storage
  • The World Wide Web — a direct product of cheap networked computation
  • Search engines — indexing and querying web-scale data in milliseconds

The umbrella takeaway: computers are pervasive. They are no longer a specialist tool locked in a research lab — they are woven into transportation, communication, medicine, entertainment, and science. This course is about understanding what happens underneath all of that — the layers of hardware and software abstraction that make it possible.

★ Professor's Intuition

Think of Moore's Law as a "budget" that keeps growing. Every two years, architects are handed roughly twice as many transistors to spend. The central design question of computer architecture is: what is the smartest way to spend that budget? More cache? More cores? Wider pipelines? Better branch predictors? Chapter 1's "Eight Great Ideas" (coming in §1.2) are essentially the field's best answers to that spending question.

Moore's Law states that computer performance doubles every two years. True or false — and why?
Answer

False, strictly speaking. Moore's Law is a statement about the number of transistors on an integrated circuit roughly doubling every two years. Performance improvements are a downstream effect of having more transistors available (more cores, larger caches, wider execution units) — but the law itself does not directly measure or guarantee execution-time speedup. On an exam, always tie your answer back to "transistor count," not "speed."

Classes of Computers

The same fundamental components appear in every computer — but the constraints they're designed under differ enormously.

The textbook divides computers into four broad classes. What matters for exams is not just the names, but the optimization target each class is designed around — because that target explains every downstream architectural decision.

1.1.2The Four Classes

Table 1.1 — Classes of computers and their defining constraint
ClassDefining TraitPrimary ConstraintScale
Personal Computer (PC)General purpose, runs a wide variety of softwareCost / performance tradeoffSingle-user desktop/laptop
ServerNetwork-based, serves many clientsCapacity, performance, reliabilitySmall servers → building-sized data centers
SupercomputerHigh-end scientific / engineering computationRaw capability, cost is secondaryHighest capability, but a tiny fraction of the market by unit count
Embedded ComputerHidden inside another system (a car, a microwave, a router)Power, performance, and cost — all simultaneously stringentBy far the most numerous class in the world
⚠ Exam Trap

A very common multiple-choice trap: "Which class of computer represents the largest fraction of the overall computer market (by units sold)?" Students instinctively answer "PC" or "Server" because those are the most visible. The correct answer is embedded computers — there are vastly more microcontrollers quietly running your car, your washing machine, your router, and your thermostat than there are PCs and servers combined. Conversely, supercomputers represent the highest capability but the smallest fraction of the market.

Why reliability matters more for servers

A single PC crashing inconveniences one user. A server crashing can mean thousands of simultaneous users losing access, financial transactions failing mid-flight, or an entire company's infrastructure going dark. This is why server design invests heavily in redundancy — extra power supplies, RAID storage, ECC memory, hot-swappable components — concepts that map directly onto one of the "Eight Great Ideas" you'll meet shortly: Dependability via Redundancy.

Why embedded systems are the hardest design problem

Embedded computers face the toughest simultaneous constraint set in the entire field: they must be cheap (produced by the millions), power-efficient (often battery-powered or passively cooled), and still performant enough to do real-time control (like anti-lock braking). Unlike a supercomputer, you can't just throw more transistors and power at an embedded problem — every milliwatt and every cent counts.

A company wants to build a system for real-time flight control on an aircraft. Which class of computer is this, and what is its primary design constraint?
Answer

This is an embedded computer. It is hidden as a component of the larger aircraft system, and it must satisfy stringent, simultaneous constraints on power, performance, and cost — with the added real-world requirement of hard real-time correctness (missing a deadline is a safety failure, not just a slowdown).

§1.1 · Introduction

The Post-PC Era

Computing shifted from a device on your desk to a device in your pocket, tethered invisibly to a warehouse full of servers.

The textbook frames a historic transition: the Post-PC Era, where the dominant computing device is no longer a desktop tower but a battery-powered, internet-connected handheld — and where a large portion of the "computing" doesn't even happen on your device at all.

1.1.3Personal Mobile Devices (PMDs)

  • Battery operated — power efficiency becomes a first-class architectural concern, not an afterthought
  • Connects to the Internet — the device is rarely useful in isolation; it's a terminal into a much larger networked system
  • Costs hundreds of dollars — far cheaper than traditional PCs, driving enormous unit volumes
  • Examples: smartphones, tablets, electronic glasses (wearables)

1.1.4Cloud Computing

The counterpart to the lightweight PMD is the Warehouse Scale Computer (WSC) — literally a building filled with thousands of servers, functioning as one enormous computer. This pairing enables Software as a Service (SaaS), where:

  • A portion of the software runs on the PMD (the lightweight front-end / UI)
  • A portion runs in the Cloud (the heavy computation, storage, and data at scale)
  • Companies like Amazon and Google are given as the canonical examples of organizations operating at WSC scale
Fig. 1.1 — Software as a Service splits work between a lightweight PMD and a Warehouse-Scale Computer
★ Professor's Intuition

Whenever you see "SaaS," ask yourself: which part runs locally, and which part runs remotely, and why? The local part is thin because PMDs are power- and cost-constrained. The remote part is heavy because WSCs have (comparatively) unlimited power and cooling infrastructure. This split is itself an application of "make the common case fast" and resource-appropriate design — themes that recur constantly in this course.

Why can't a smartphone simply do all of Google Search's ranking computation locally, on-device?
Answer

Because a PMD is fundamentally constrained: it is battery-powered, must stay within tight thermal/power budgets, and has orders of magnitude less compute and storage than a warehouse full of servers. Search ranking over a web-scale index requires the aggregate capacity of a Warehouse Scale Computer. The PMD instead sends a lightweight request and renders the returned result — the classic SaaS split.

What You Will Learn & Understanding Performance

A roadmap for the entire course — and the four ingredients that jointly determine how fast a program runs.

1.1.5What You Will Learn in CSE209

The introductory slides set expectations for the whole semester. By the end of this course, you should understand:

  • How programs written in a high-level language are translated into machine language
  • How the hardware actually executes those machine instructions
  • The hardware/software interface — the contract between the two worlds
  • What determines program performance, and how it can be improved
  • How hardware designers improve performance at the architecture level
  • What parallel processing is, and why modern architecture leans on it so heavily

Notice the throughline: this course moves from software concepts (translation, instructions) down to hardware realities (execution, performance, parallelism). Everything you learn is really tracing one continuous path from a line of C code down to electrons moving through silicon.

1.1.6Understanding Performance

"Performance" feels like a single number, but it is actually the product of decisions made at four independent layers of the system. This is one of the most exam-favorite lists in the entire chapter:

Table 1.2 — What determines program performance
LayerWhat it Determines
AlgorithmThe number of operations executed (e.g., O(n²) vs O(n log n))
Programming language, compiler, architectureThe number of machine instructions executed per operation
Processor and memory systemHow fast instructions are actually executed
I/O system (including the OS)How fast I/O operations are executed
⚠ Exam Trap

A frequently tested confusion: students conflate "algorithm" and "compiler" as both affecting instruction count and think they're interchangeable. They are not the same lever. The algorithm determines the number of high-level operations (independent of any machine). The compiler/language/architecture determines how many machine instructions are needed to realize each of those operations. A brilliant algorithm compiled badly can still perform poorly, and vice versa — these are orthogonal design decisions, and exam questions love asking you to identify "which layer is responsible" for a described performance problem.

✎ Brainstorm

Suppose a program runs slowly. Walk through each of the four layers as a checklist: Is the algorithm asymptotically inefficient? Is the compiler generating bloated code (poor optimization)? Is the processor/memory stalling on cache misses? Is the program I/O-bound, waiting on disk or network? Real performance debugging is exactly this kind of layered elimination process — and this four-layer model is the mental checklist professional engineers use.

A programmer switches from bubble sort to quicksort and the program speeds up dramatically. Which of the four performance layers did they change?
Answer

The algorithm. Bubble sort is O(n²); quicksort is O(n log n) on average. This changes the number of operations executed for a given input, independent of compiler, processor, or I/O system.

§1.2 · Eight Great Ideas in Computer Architecture

Eight Great Ideas

The unifying framework for the entire course. Almost every technique you'll learn in CSE209 is a specific instance of one of these eight ideas.

Memorize this list well — professors love building entire exam sections around "identify which Great Idea this technique demonstrates." Treat it the way you'd treat a periodic table: a small set of primitives that everything else in the course is built from.

  1. Design for Moore's Law — anticipate that transistor budgets will keep growing, and design so that your architecture can scale to exploit tomorrow's silicon, not just today's.
  2. Use abstraction to simplify design — hide lower-level detail behind clean interfaces (this is literally the subject of §1.3, coming up next).
  3. Make the common case fast — don't spend equal engineering effort on rare and frequent operations; optimize what actually happens most often.
  4. Performance via parallelism — do multiple things at once (multiple cores, multiple ALUs, multiple memory banks).
  5. Performance via pipelining — overlap the stages of executing multiple instructions, like an assembly line.
  6. Performance via prediction — sometimes it's faster to guess and be right most of the time (with a cheap recovery path when wrong) than to always wait for certainty. Branch prediction is the flagship example.
  7. Hierarchy of memories — balance speed, cost, and capacity by layering small/fast memory (registers, cache) on top of large/slow memory (RAM, disk).
  8. Dependability via redundancy — duplicate critical components so the system tolerates individual failures (this is exactly why server design emphasizes reliability, as we saw in §1.1).
★ Professor's Intuition

Group these eight into three buckets to memorize them faster:

Design philosophy (ideas 1–2): Moore's Law, Abstraction.
Performance techniques (ideas 3–6): Common case, Parallelism, Pipelining, Prediction — notice these four literally all start with a "P" sound (common-case is the odd one out, but three of the four performance techniques are the "3 P's": Parallelism, Pipelining, Prediction).
System-level concerns (ideas 7–8): Memory hierarchy, Redundancy/dependability.

⚠ Exam Trap

Parallelism and pipelining are frequently confused. Parallelism means doing independent work at the same time using duplicated resources (e.g., 4 cores each running a different instruction stream, or 4 ALUs computing 4 independent additions simultaneously). Pipelining means overlapping the stages of processing a sequential stream of work — like a car factory where car #2 enters the paint stage while car #1 is already at the assembly stage. Pipelining does not require duplicate full resources for each stage; it requires splitting one task into stages and overlapping them across different items in a stream. If a question describes multiple independent cores → parallelism. If it describes overlapping stages of a single instruction stream → pipelining.

A CPU guesses which way a branch will go and starts executing down that path before the branch condition is actually known. If the guess is right, execution continues at full speed; if wrong, the work is discarded and redone. Which Great Idea is this?
Answer

Performance via prediction (branch prediction is the textbook's flagship example of this idea).

A processor keeps a small, extremely fast L1 cache close to the core, backed by a larger, slower L2 cache, backed by even larger, even slower main memory. Which Great Idea does this illustrate?
Answer

Hierarchy of memories — balancing speed, capacity, and cost by layering memory technologies.

Below Your Program

Tracing the full stack — from the code you type down to the bits that actually move through hardware.

1.3.1Software Layers

  • Application software — written in a high-level language (HLL); this is what end-users and most programmers interact with directly.
  • System software
    • Compiler — translates HLL source code into machine code (or into assembly, which is then assembled)
    • Operating System — provides services common to all programs: handling input/output, managing memory and storage, scheduling tasks, and sharing resources safely among programs
  • Hardware — the processor, memory, and I/O controllers that ultimately do the work
Fig. 1.2 — "Below Your Program": the three layers between your code and the silicon

1.3.2Levels of Program Code

A single piece of source code passes through three representations before it can run:

LevelDescription
High-level languageCloser to the problem domain; provides productivity and portability (e.g., C, Java, Python)
Assembly languageA textual, human-readable representation of individual machine instructions
Hardware representationBinary digits (bits) — the actual encoded instructions and data the processor consumes

The compiler handles the first translation (HLL → assembly/machine code); an assembler handles turning assembly text into binary machine code. You'll get hands-on with this exact pipeline in Chapter 2 (MIPS ISA) and again when we discuss translation, linking, and loading.

1.4.1Components of a Computer

Regardless of whether you're looking at a desktop, a server, or an embedded controller, the same fundamental components recur:

Fig. 1.3 — Components of a computer: Input, Output, Memory, and Processor (Datapath + Control)
  • Input/Output includes user-interface devices (display, keyboard, mouse), storage devices (hard disk, CD/DVD, flash), and network adapters for communicating with other computers.
  • The Processor is itself made of two parts you will study in depth later this course: the Datapath (which performs operations on data) and the Control (which orchestrates the datapath, memory, and I/O).
⚠ Exam Trap

Storage devices (hard disk, flash, CD/DVD) are frequently miscategorized. Students want to call them "Memory," but in this taxonomy they fall under I/O (specifically, storage I/O) — "Memory" in this diagram refers specifically to the fast, directly-addressable main memory the processor reads instructions and data from. Long-term/secondary storage is treated as an I/O device the processor talks to indirectly.

Which two sub-components make up "the Processor" in the classic computer components diagram?
Answer

Datapath (performs the actual operations on data) and Control (directs the datapath, memory, and I/O devices according to the instructions being executed).

§1.4 · Under the Covers — The Big Picture

Abstractions: ISA & ABI

Three precisely distinct terms that examiners love to test against each other.

Abstraction is how we tame the staggering complexity of a modern computer — by hiding lower-level detail behind well-defined interfaces so that each layer of the system only needs to know about the layer directly below it. Chapter 1 introduces three specific abstraction concepts that you must be able to tell apart cold.

TermDefinition
Instruction Set Architecture (ISA)The hardware/software interface — the complete set of instructions, registers, and behaviors a processor exposes to software. This is the contract.
Application Binary Interface (ABI)The ISA plus the system software (OS) interface — includes calling conventions, system call interfaces, and binary formats. A compiled binary conforming to a given ABI can run unmodified on any machine implementing that ABI.
ImplementationThe actual hardware details realizing an interface — the specific circuits, pipeline design, and microarchitecture underneath.
★ Professor's Intuition

Think of the ISA as a restaurant's menu: it tells you exactly what dishes (instructions) you can order and what you'll get back, without telling you anything about the kitchen. The implementation is the kitchen itself — two different restaurants (say, Intel and AMD) can serve the exact same menu (the x86 ISA) using completely different kitchens (microarchitectures) — different pipeline depths, different cache designs, different out-of-order execution logic — yet a customer (a compiled program) ordering off that menu gets a compatible result either way. The ABI extends the analogy: it's the menu plus the restaurant's ordering procedure (how you pay, how you're seated) — matching both means your "order" (binary) works without any changes across compatible restaurants.

⚠ Exam Trap

The classic trick question: "If two processors implement the same ISA, will a compiled binary always run identically fast on both?" No. Same ISA guarantees correctness (the same instructions produce the same architectural results) — it does not guarantee the same performance, because performance depends on the implementation (pipeline design, cache sizes, clock rate), which can differ freely underneath a shared ISA. This is precisely why Intel and AMD chips both run x86 software correctly, yet have very different performance profiles.

A software vendor ships a single compiled binary that runs, unmodified, on any Linux machine with an x86-64 CPU — regardless of the specific CPU model. What abstraction makes this possible?
Answer

The Application Binary Interface (ABI) — because it fixes not just the ISA but also the OS-level interface (system calls, calling conventions, binary/executable format), a binary built against a stable ABI runs unmodified across any compliant hardware/OS combination.

§1.6 · Performance

Defining & Measuring Performance

"Performance" sounds simple until you have to define it precisely — and precision here is where most exam points are won or lost.

1.6.1Defining Performance: Which Airplane Wins?

The textbook opens with a deliberately provocative question: which airplane has the best performance? The answer depends entirely on what you're optimizing for.

Table 1.3 — Airplane performance depends on your metric
AirplanePassenger CapacityCruising Range (mi)Cruising Speed (mph)Passengers × Speed
Boeing 7773754,630610228,750
Boeing 7474704,150610286,700
BAC/Sud Concorde1324,0001,350178,200
Douglas DC-8-501468,72054479,424

If your metric is raw speed, the Concorde wins outright. If your metric is throughput (passengers × speed — how many passenger-miles you deliver per hour), the Boeing 747 wins. If your metric is range, the DC-8-50 wins. This is not a trick — it is the entire point: you cannot discuss "performance" without first fixing the metric. The same discipline applies directly to computers.

1.6.2Response Time vs. Throughput

  • Response time (latency) — how long it takes to complete one task, start to finish.
  • Throughput (bandwidth) — total work completed per unit time (e.g., transactions per hour).
⚠ Exam Trap — the single most common Chapter 1 exam question

Replacing the processor with a faster version improves both response time (each task finishes sooner) and throughput (more tasks complete per unit time) — a rare "everybody wins" upgrade.

Adding more processors primarily improves throughput (more tasks can run concurrently) but does not, by itself, reduce the response time of any single task. Analogy: adding more checkout lanes at a grocery store lets more customers check out per hour (higher throughput), but it does not make any individual customer's own checkout any faster (unchanged response time) — it just reduces how long they wait in line beforehand if the store was congested.

1.6.3Relative Performance

Performance = 1 / Execution Time

To say "X is n times faster than Y," compute:

n = Execution TimeY / Execution TimeX
✓ Worked Example

A program takes 10s on Computer A and 15s on Computer B. How much faster is A?

Execution TimeB / Execution TimeA = 15s / 10s = 1.5

A is 1.5 times faster than B.

⚠ Exam Trap

Students very frequently invert the ratio. The rule to memorize: put the slower machine's time on top (numerator) and the faster (reference) machine's time on the bottom (denominator). If you're claiming "A is faster," A's time belongs in the denominator. Getting this backwards silently flips your answer to a number less than 1, which should immediately look wrong if you're claiming something is "faster."

1.6.4Measuring Execution Time — Elapsed vs. CPU Time

  • Elapsed time (wall-clock time) — total response time including everything: processing, I/O, OS overhead, and idle time. This is what a user actually experiences and what determines overall system performance.
  • CPU time — time spent actually processing a given job, discounting I/O time and time spent running other jobs. It further splits into user CPU time and system CPU time.

Different programs are affected very differently by CPU performance versus system (OS/I/O) performance — a compute-bound scientific simulation lives and dies by CPU time, while a program that spends most of its life waiting on disk or network is dominated by elapsed time factors the CPU has little control over.

A data center operator adds 8 more identical servers behind a load balancer. What improves — response time, throughput, both, or neither?
Answer

Throughput improves — more requests can be served concurrently. The response time of any individual request is not directly improved by adding more processors (it may improve indirectly if the system was previously congested/queued, but the fundamental per-task processing time is unchanged).

Computer P runs a benchmark in 8 seconds. Computer Q runs the same benchmark in 20 seconds. How many times faster is P than Q?
Answer

20s / 8s = 2.5. P is 2.5 times faster than Q.

§1.6 · Performance

The CPU Performance Equation

The single most important equation in Chapter 1 — and the one that shows up in nearly every exam, in some form, for the rest of the course.

1.6.5CPU Clocking

Digital hardware operates under a constant-rate clock. Every cycle, data transfer and computation happen, followed by a state update.

Fig. 1.4 — A constant-rate clock signal; one clock period = one clock cycle
  • Clock period — the duration of one clock cycle. Example: 250ps = 0.25ns = 250×10⁻¹² s
  • Clock frequency (rate) — cycles per second, the reciprocal of the period. Example: 4.0GHz = 4000MHz = 4.0×10⁹ Hz
Clock Period = 1 / Clock Frequency
⚠ Exam Trap

Unit conversions are where numeric points are lost, not conceptual misunderstanding. Keep a mental conversion table on hand during exams: 1 GHz = 10⁹ Hz, 1 ns = 10⁻⁹ s, 1 ps = 10⁻¹² s. A clock period given in picoseconds must be converted to seconds before it can be combined with a CPU time given in seconds — mixing units mid-calculation is the single most common arithmetic error on this topic.

1.6.6CPU Time

Performance can be improved by reducing the number of clock cycles or by increasing the clock rate — and hardware designers constantly trade one against the other, because a design decision that increases clock rate (e.g., a deeper pipeline) often also increases the number of cycles needed per instruction.

✓ Worked Example — CPU Time

Computer A: 2GHz clock, 10s CPU time. You are designing Computer B, targeting 6s CPU time, but the faster design causes 1.2× as many clock cycles. How fast must B's clock be?

Step 1 — find A's clock cycle count:

Clock CyclesA = CPU TimeA × Clock RateA = 10s × 2×10⁹ Hz = 20×10⁹ cycles

Step 2 — scale by the 1.2× cycle-count penalty:

Clock CyclesB = 1.2 × 20×10⁹ = 24×10⁹ cycles

Step 3 — solve for B's required clock rate to hit the 6s target:

Clock RateB = Clock CyclesB / CPU TimeB = 24×10⁹ / 6s = 4.0 GHz

Computer B must clock at 4.0 GHz — double A's clock rate, even though it needs less than double the raw speedup, because of the extra 1.2× cycle overhead.

1.6.7Instruction Count and CPI

  • Instruction Count (IC) — determined by the program, the ISA, and the compiler.
  • Average Cycles Per Instruction (CPI) — determined by the CPU hardware. When different instructions take different numbers of cycles, the average CPI is affected by the instruction mix (which instruction types actually get executed, and how often).
CPU Time = Instruction Count × CPI × Clock Cycle Time = (Instruction Count × CPI) / Clock Rate
Fig. 1.5 — The three independent levers of CPU performance, and who controls each
✓ Worked Example — Comparing CPI

Computer A: Cycle Time = 250ps, CPI = 2.0. Computer B: Cycle Time = 500ps, CPI = 1.2. Same ISA (so same Instruction Count, call it IC). Which is faster, and by how much?

CPU TimeA = IC × 2.0 × 250ps = 500 × IC ps CPU TimeB = IC × 1.2 × 500ps = 600 × IC ps CPU TimeB / CPU TimeA = 600/500 = 1.2

A is faster, by a factor of 1.2. Notice B has a lower CPI, yet A still wins overall — a lower CPI alone tells you nothing if the clock cycle time is worse. This is exactly why the full three-term equation matters; no single term determines performance in isolation.

1.6.8CPI in More Detail — Weighted Average CPI

When different instruction classes take different numbers of cycles, we compute a weighted average CPI:

CPI = Σᵢ (CPIᵢ × ICᵢ) / Instruction Counttotal = Σᵢ (CPIᵢ × Fᵢ) where Fᵢ = ICᵢ / Instruction Counttotal (relative frequency)
✓ Worked Example — Two Compiled Sequences

Two compiled code sequences use instructions from classes A, B, C:

ClassABC
CPI for class123
IC in Sequence 1212
IC in Sequence 2411

Sequence 1: IC = 2+1+2 = 5

Clock Cycles = (2×1) + (1×2) + (2×3) = 2 + 2 + 6 = 10 Avg. CPI = 10 / 5 = 2.0

Sequence 2: IC = 4+1+1 = 6

Clock Cycles = (4×1) + (1×2) + (1×3) = 4 + 2 + 3 = 9 Avg. CPI = 9 / 6 = 1.5

Sequence 2 wins — fewer total clock cycles (9 vs 10) — despite having a larger Instruction Count (6 vs 5).

⚠ Exam Trap — the "more instructions ≠ slower" fallacy

The worked example above is a deliberately designed trap: students see IC = 6 > IC = 5 and reflexively assume Sequence 2 is slower. It is not — because Sequence 2 leans more heavily on the cheap Class-A instructions (CPI = 1) and less on the expensive Class-C instructions (CPI = 3). Instruction count alone never tells you performance — you must always weigh instruction count against CPI together. This single insight is also the conceptual seed for why "MIPS as a metric" is a pitfall, discussed in the next section.

Computer X: Cycle Time = 400ps, CPI = 1.5. Computer Y: Cycle Time = 300ps, CPI = 2.2. Same ISA, same Instruction Count (IC). Which is faster, and by what factor?
Answer

CPU TimeX = IC × 1.5 × 400ps = 600·IC ps.
CPU TimeY = IC × 2.2 × 300ps = 660·IC ps.
Ratio Y/X = 660/600 = 1.1. X is faster, by a factor of 1.1.

§1.9–1.10 · Concluding Remarks & Fallacies/Pitfalls

Pitfalls & Concluding Remarks

Where naïve performance reasoning breaks down — and a formula sheet to carry into the exam.

1.6.9Performance Summary — The Big Picture

Performance depends on a chain of decisions, each affecting different terms in the CPU time equation:

FactorAffects
AlgorithmInstruction Count, possibly CPI
Programming languageInstruction Count, CPI
CompilerInstruction Count, CPI
Instruction Set ArchitectureInstruction Count, CPI, and Clock Cycle Time (Tc)

Only the ISA touches all three terms — a reminder of just how foundational ISA design decisions are to everything downstream.

1.10.1Pitfall: Amdahl's Law

A dangerously intuitive but wrong assumption: improving one aspect of a computer proportionally improves overall performance by the same amount. It can't be done — the improvement is always capped by how much of the total execution time that aspect actually accounts for.

Speedupoverall = 1 / [ (1 − Fenhanced) + Fenhanced/Speedupenhanced ]
✓ Worked Example — The "5× Overall" Trap

In a 100-second program, multiply operations account for 80 seconds of that time (F = 0.8). How much would multiply performance need to improve to get a 5× overall speedup?

5 = 1 / [ (1 − 0.8) + 0.8/Speedupmult ] 5 = 1 / [ 0.2 + 0.8/Speedupmult ] 0.2 + 0.8/Speedupmult = 0.2 0.8/Speedupmult = 0 → Speedupmult → ∞

It is impossible. The non-multiply portion alone already takes 20 seconds — so even if multiply took zero time (infinite speedup), the absolute best possible overall time is 20s, which is exactly a 100/20 = 5× speedup. You can only approach 5× as a limit; you can never exceed it by speeding up multiply alone. This is Amdahl's Law's core lesson, stated as a hard ceiling.

⚠ Exam Trap — the "ceiling" insight

The single most exam-relevant consequence of Amdahl's Law: the maximum possible speedup from improving only one component is bounded by 1 / (1 − Fenhanced) — the reciprocal of the un-enhanced fraction. In the example above, 1/(1−0.8) = 1/0.2 = 5×, which is exactly the ceiling we derived. Memorize this shortcut — it lets you answer "what's the best possible speedup?" questions in one line, without solving the full equation.

Corollary: make the common case fast. Amdahl's Law is precisely why this corollary matters — optimizing a component that's only active 5% of the time can never yield large overall gains, no matter how aggressively you optimize it.

1.10.2Pitfall: MIPS as a Performance Metric

MIPS (Millions of Instructions Per Second) looks like an intuitive speed metric, but it is a textbook pitfall because it fails to account for:

  • Differences in ISAs between computers — one instruction on Machine A might do the work of three instructions on Machine B, so raw instruction throughput isn't comparable across ISAs.
  • Differences in instruction complexity — not all instructions do equal amounts of "work," so counting them equally is misleading.
  • CPI variation between programs — even on the same CPU, different programs exercise different instruction mixes, so a single MIPS number can't characterize a machine's performance across all workloads.
⚠ Exam Trap

A machine can post a higher MIPS number while executing a program more slowly in actual wall-clock time — if it does so using many more, simpler, low-CPI instructions instead of fewer, more powerful ones. Never assume "higher MIPS = faster" without checking what those instructions were actually accomplishing. This is exactly the same trap as the Sequence 1 vs Sequence 2 example from §1.6.8 — instruction-counting metrics without CPI context are always suspect.

1.9Concluding Remarks

  • Cost/performance continues to improve, driven by underlying technology development (Moore's Law).
  • Both hardware and software are organized as hierarchical layers of abstraction — the theme running through the entire chapter.
  • The Instruction Set Architecture is the critical hardware/software interface.
  • Execution time is the best performance measure — prefer it over proxy metrics like MIPS.
  • Power is a limiting factor in modern design — you can't simply add transistors and clock speed without a thermal/energy budget.
  • Modern architecture increasingly relies on parallelism to keep improving performance, since single-core clock scaling has slowed dramatically.

Answer
