Undecidability

From Hilbert's broken dream to a language no machine can recognize. We'll build the diagonalization argument from the ground up — encode Turing machines as bit strings, enumerate them, and then construct a language that no row of the resulting table can possibly describe.

Sheikh Azizul Hakim · adapted from Atif Hasan Rahman Sipser Ch. 4 / Hopcroft–Ullman Ch. 9 Stops at: proof that Ld is not RE
Part I · Origins

01Hilbert's Program

Every story about the limits of computation starts with someone believing there were no limits at all. In 1900, the German mathematician David Hilbert stood in front of the International Congress of Mathematicians in Paris and asked a question that sounds almost naive today: is there an algorithm that can settle the truth or falsehood of any mathematical proposition?

Historical context

Hilbert identified 23 open problems and posed them as a challenge for the coming century. His broader philosophical stance — sometimes called Hilbert's program — was that mathematics should be complete (every true statement provable) and decidable (a mechanical procedure exists to settle any statement). The tenth problem on his list asked for a process to determine whether a multivariate polynomial such as

6x³yz² + 3xy² − x³ − 10

has integer roots — a Diophantine equation. Hilbert assumed the answer would be "yes, here's the algorithm." It wasn't.

We now know — via the 1970 work of Yuri Matijasevič, building on Martin Davis, Hilary Putnam, and Julia Robinson — that no algorithm exists for this task. It is algorithmically unsolvable. This is the first thread of today's lecture: some questions have no algorithmic answer, not because we haven't found one yet, but because none can exist, and this can be proven.

Why should a CS student care about a 1900 math problem?

Because the tenth problem's resolution needed a rigorous, formal notion of "algorithm" — and building that notion (independently, three different ways) is exactly what gave us Turing machines, the λ-calculus, and general recursive functions. Hilbert's optimism is the reason the machinery of this entire course exists. Without someone asking "is there always an algorithm?", nobody needed to pin down precisely what an algorithm is.

Part I · Origins

02Gödel's Incompleteness Theorem

Hilbert's dream cracked first in 1931, when Kurt Gödel published his incompleteness theorem — arguably the single most important negative result in 20th-century mathematics.

Gödel's incompleteness theorem (informal)

No consistent system of axioms whose theorems can be listed by an effective procedure (i.e. an algorithm) is capable of proving all truths about the arithmetic of the natural numbers.

Gödel's method was itself a preview of diagonalization: he constructed a formula in the predicate calculus (logic with quantified variables) applied to integers, which asserted of itself that it could be neither proved nor disproved within the system. If the system could prove it, the system would be inconsistent (proving a false, self-negating statement); if the system is consistent, the statement is true but unprovable. Either way, the dream of a complete, mechanically-checkable mathematics dies.

Self-reference is the recurring villain of this whole course

Keep this feeling in your pocket. Gödel's "this statement cannot be proved" sentence, the Barber of Seville paradox you'll meet in §15, and the diagonalization language Ld you'll build in §13 are all the same trick wearing different costumes: build an object that talks about itself in a way that makes every possible answer contradictory. This isn't a coincidence — it's the one move that keeps proving limits exist.

Part I · Origins

03Church, Turing, and the Church–Turing Thesis

Gödel's proof needed a rigorous notion of "effective procedure," and in the mid-1930s, three independent attempts to pin that notion down converged on the same answer.

YearWhoFormalism
1933Gödel & Jacques HerbrandGeneral recursive functions — a class of functions built from primitives and composition/recursion rules
1936Alonzo ChurchThe λ-calculus — functions defined purely by abstraction and application
1936Alan Turing (independently, before learning of Church's work)The Turing machine — a theoretical model of a machine manipulating symbols on a tape

Church and Turing then proved that these three formally defined classes of computable functions coincide: a function is λ-computable if and only if it is Turing-computable if and only if it is general recursive. Three wildly different starting points, one destination.

The Church–Turing thesis

The (unprovable) assumption that any general way to compute will let us compute exactly the general recursive functions — equivalently, exactly what a Turing machine can compute.

Why "unprovable"? Because "general way to compute" is an informal, intuitive notion — you can't take the formal proof techniques of mathematics and apply them to an informal concept. What convinces us is the sheer weight of independent convergence: every reasonable formalization anyone has proposed since — register machines, cellular automata, your laptop — has turned out to be exactly as powerful as a Turing machine, never more. (You've already seen a piece of this equivalence: TMs and real computers simulate each other with only polynomial overhead, from the previous lecture.)

Trap: "unprovable" doesn't mean "unfalsifiable" or "untested"

The Church–Turing thesis could in principle be refuted — someone would just need to exhibit a genuinely general computing procedure that provably computes something no TM can. Nobody ever has, across nearly a century and every computing paradigm proposed (including quantum computers, which change efficiency but not the set of computable functions). "Unprovable" here means it can't be proven true the way a theorem is proven — it's a claim about the relationship between an informal, everyday notion and a formal one, and informal notions aren't the kind of thing formal proofs apply to.

A fun, sharp edge

The mortal matrix problem is known to be undecidable for a set of six or more 3×3 matrices, or just two 15×15 matrices. Undecidability doesn't need an exotic-looking problem — it can hide inside ordinary-looking linear algebra once the dimensions or the count cross a threshold.

Today's lecture builds the single sharpest tool for proving results like these: we're going to exhibit a language that isn't just undecidable — it's so pathological that no Turing machine accepts it at all, not even one that's allowed to loop forever on rejects. That's strictly stronger than undecidability, and it's where we're headed.

Part II · The Landscape

05Recursively Enumerable (RE) Languages

Definition

A language L is recursively enumerable (abbreviated RE) if L = L(M) for some Turing machine M.

This is just "some TM accepts exactly this language" — the broadest, most permissive class we'll talk about. But RE languages split naturally into two very different flavors, based on what the machine does on rejecting input:

Halts on everything. The TM not only recognizes the language, it also halts eventually regardless of whether it accepts or rejects. This is what we intuitively call "an algorithm" — you're always guaranteed an answer in finite time.

May loop forever on rejects. The TM accepts if the input is in the language (and then it halts), but if the input is not in the language, the machine might run forever, never telling you "no." You'll eventually find out "yes" if the answer is yes — but "no" may never arrive.

This asymmetry — confidently reporting "yes," but only sometimes reporting "no" — is the single most important intuition in this entire lecture. Keep it close; it explains why Ld, the language we're about to build, breaks even this weaker guarantee entirely.

Part II · The Landscape

06Recursive Languages

Definition

A language L is recursive if L = L(M) for some TM M such that:

If w ∈ L, then M accepts (and therefore halts).

If w ∉ L, then M eventually halts, although it doesn't enter an accepting state.

In other words: a recursive language is exactly an RE language of the first, stronger flavor above — the machine always halts, in both directions. This is the formal version of "an algorithm exists."

Vocabulary you'll use for the rest of the course

A problem (phrased as a language) is decidable if it is a recursive language, and undecidable if it is not. From here on, "decidable" and "recursive" are synonyms — same for "undecidable" and "not recursive."

Trap: "undecidable" is not the same as "not RE"

This is the single most common confusion students carry out of this lecture. Undecidable means "not recursive" — there's no TM that always halts and correctly accepts/rejects. That still leaves room for a TM that accepts the language but loops forever on some rejects (the Halting Problem is exactly this: undecidable, but RE). Not RE is strictly stronger — it means no TM whatsoever accepts the language, not even a badly-behaved one that might loop forever. Ld, our target today, is not RE — a much harder thing to prove than mere undecidability, and it's why the proof technique (diagonalization) has to be so much more careful than "just" showing no algorithm exists.

Part II · The Landscape

07The Three-Way Split

Putting the last two sections together, every language over a given alphabet falls into exactly one of three bins:

The three-way split: recursive ⊊ RE ⊊ all languages. Today's proof places L_d outside even the middle ring.

Recursive — decidable, an algorithm always terminates with an answer.

RE but not recursive — recognizable, but no algorithm that's guaranteed to halt on every input.

Non-RE — not even recognizable; no TM at all, of any kind, accepts the language.

Some decidable languages you already have the tools for

Before we go build a non-RE language, it's worth grounding the "recursive" ring with genuine examples — languages about automata and grammars that are themselves decidable questions.

LanguageMeaning
A_DFA = {⟨B,w⟩}B is a DFA that accepts input string w
A_NFA = {⟨B,w⟩}B is an NFA that accepts input string w
A_REX = {⟨R,w⟩}R is a regular expression that generates w
E_DFA = {⟨A⟩}A is a DFA and L(A) = ∅
EQ_DFA = {⟨A,B⟩}A, B are DFAs and L(A) = L(B)
A_CFG = {⟨G,w⟩}G is a CFG that generates w
E_CFG = {⟨G⟩}G is a CFG and L(G) = ∅
The one that breaks the pattern

EQ_CFG = {⟨G,H⟩ | G, H are CFGs and L(G) = L(H)} is undecidable — even though its DFA cousin EQ_DFA is perfectly decidable (you built exactly this via the product construction and symmetric difference back in Lecture 5). Regular languages are closed under enough operations to make equivalence checking easy; context-free languages aren't closed under intersection or complement, and that closure gap is precisely where the decidability breaks.

Why is A_DFA decidable but we'll soon find TM-acceptance is not?

Because a DFA is guaranteed to halt on every input — it consumes the string symbol by symbol and, at the end, is deterministically either in an accepting state or not. There's no possibility of looping forever; simulating a DFA on w is just |w| steps of table lookup. The analogous question for Turing machines — "does M accept w?" — has no such guarantee, because M itself might never halt. That missing halting guarantee is exactly the gap the rest of this lecture (and the next) is built to exploit.

Part III · Encoding TMs

08The Big Goal

We're aiming at one specific, concrete non-RE language. Let's state exactly what we're trying to break:

Target

Consider the set of pairs (M, w) such that:

M is a Turing machine (suitably coded, in binary) with input alphabet {0,1},

w is a string of 0's and 1's, and

M accepts w.

If we can show this problem — restricted to the binary alphabet — is not RE, then the more general problem (TMs over any alphabet) is automatically not RE too: the binary case is a special case of the general one, so if even the easier restricted version has no algorithm at all, the harder general version certainly doesn't either.

Why binary alphabet specifically?

Restricting to {0,1} isn't a loss of generality — it's a convenience that lets us do something crucial in the next section: treat a Turing machine's own description as just another binary string, on equal footing with the strings it might take as input. That equal footing is what makes diagonalization possible: we're about to feed a machine's own code to itself.

Part III · Encoding TMs

09Enumerating the Binary Strings

Before we can talk about "the i-th Turing machine," we need to talk about "the i-th binary string" — a way to line up every string in {0,1}* against the positive integers, with no repeats and nothing left out.

The 1w trick

If w is a binary string, treat 1w (prepend a 1) as a binary integer i. Call w the i-th string, written wi.

Why prepend a 1 instead of just reading w directly as a binary number? Because leading zeros would collide — the strings 0, 00, and 000 would all read as the integer 0. Gluing on a leading 1 first makes every string, including the empty string and strings with leading zeros, map to a distinct positive integer. Equivalently: strings are ordered by length first, then lexicographically within each length — exactly how you'd naturally list them out.

Interactive · index ↔ stringverified against length-then-lex order

Give an index, get the string — or give a string, get its index. Try i=1 (should be ε) or type w=101.

Hereafter, we refer to the i-th string as wi: w₁ = ε, w₂ = 0, w₃ = 1, w₄ = 00, and so on. Hang on to this table — the exact same numbering scheme is about to double as our numbering scheme for Turing machines.

Part III · Encoding TMs

10Coding a Turing Machine

We now want a binary code for TMs so that "the i-th TM," written Mi, makes sense — using the exact same integer-to-string correspondence from §9. To represent M = (Q, {0,1}, Γ, δ, q₁, B, F) as a binary string, we first fix integer labels for every state, tape symbol, and direction.

ObjectRule
States q₁,…,qrq₁ is always the start state. q₂ is always the (unique) accepting state — we may assume a TM halts the instant it enters an accepting state, so one accept state is always enough.
Tape symbols X₁,…,XsX₁ is always the symbol 0, X₂ is always 1, X₃ is always the blank B. Any remaining tape symbols get the rest of the integers, in any order.
DirectionsD₁ is always L, D₂ is always R.
Fixed vs. free assignments

q₁, q₂, X₁, X₂, X₃, D₁, D₂ are pinned down by convention. Everything else (extra states, extra tape symbols) can be numbered in whatever order you like — which is exactly why a single TM will have multiple valid codes (more on this in §12).

Encoding one transition

Once every state/symbol/direction has an integer, we can encode δ itself. Suppose one rule is δ(qi, Xj) = (qk, Xl, Dm) for integers i, j, k, l, m. We code this rule as the string:

0i 1 0j 1 0k 1 0l 1 0m

Five blocks of 0's — one per component of the rule — separated by single 1's. Since i,j,k,l,m ≥ 1 always (every label starts at 1), no valid transition code ever contains two consecutive 1's. That single fact is about to become our tool for parsing codes back apart.

Encoding the whole machine

Glue the transition codes together, separated by pairs of 1's — a marker that can never be confused with anything inside a transition, precisely because single transitions never contain "11":

c₁ 11 c₂ 11 ⋯ 11 cn

where each c is the code for one transition of M, listed in some order.

Part III · Encoding TMs

12Validity & Non-Uniqueness

Not every binary string is a valid code

We now have a way to turn a TM into a string. But we also need the enumeration to go the other direction — every string needs to name a TM, even if it's gibberish, so that "the i-th Turing machine" is defined for every single i with no gaps.

Interactive · is this a valid TM code?same parser as the encoder above

Type a binary string and check it, or try a preset.

11001 (slide example) 0010111010010100 (slide example) M's code from §11 just "0"

Must start with 0. A string like 11001 can't be a code — every transition code begins with 0i for i ≥ 1.

No three consecutive 1's. 0010111010010100 is invalid — a run of "111" can never arise, since single 1's separate the five fields within a transition and double 1's separate transitions; three in a row parses as neither.

Clean block structure. Splitting on "11" should yield nonempty transition-blocks, each of which splits on single "1" into exactly five nonempty runs of 0's.

The escape hatch: invalid codes still name a TM

If wi is not a valid TM code, we let Mi be the trivial TM with one state and no transitions — a machine that halts immediately on any input, without accepting. So L(Mi) = ∅ whenever wi fails to be a valid code. This is what makes "the i-th Turing machine" a total function of i — defined for every positive integer, no exceptions.

One machine, many codes

Notice §10 pins down q₁, q₂, X₁, X₂, X₃, D₁, D₂ but leaves everything else free — extra states and extra tape symbols can be numbered in any order, and the transitions themselves can be listed in any order within the final concatenation. For our 4-transition example, that's 4! = 24 different valid codes for the exact same machine. This multiplicity is harmless for what follows — we'll show no encoding, of any TM, can produce Ld, so it doesn't matter which of the 24 codes anyone happens to pick.

Part IV · Diagonalization

13Defining Mi and Ld

We now have everything in place. §9 numbers every binary string; §10–§12 turn every binary string into (a code for) a Turing machine, with invalid strings mapping to the empty-language machine. Put them together:

The i-th Turing machine

Mi, "the i-th Turing machine," is the TM whose code is wi, the i-th binary string. If wi is not a valid TM code, Mi is the one-state, no-transition machine, so L(Mi) = ∅.

This single definition is doing something remarkable: it gives us an enumeration of every Turing machine with input alphabet {0,1} — M₁, M₂, M₃, … — that is guaranteed to include every such TM somewhere in the list (possibly more than once, since each machine has up to 24 valid codes, but that's fine — we only need "at least once").

The diagonalization language

Ld is the set of strings wi such that wi ∉ L(Mi). Equivalently: Ld consists of all strings w such that the TM M whose code is w does not accept w — its own code — when given itself as input.

Read that definition three times. Here's the plain-English version.

Every binary string w plays two roles at once: it's a candidate input, and — since we've numbered TMs the same way we numbered strings — it's simultaneously a candidate machine description. Ld asks a self-referential question of every string: "does the machine you describe reject you, when fed yourself?" If yes, you're in Ld. This "feed the machine its own description" move is exactly what makes the argument work — and exactly what will make it blow up in the proof of §16.

Part IV · Diagonalization

14The Diagonal Table

Here's why "diagonalization" is the right name. Imagine an infinite table: row i is labeled by Mi, column j is labeled by wj, and cell (i,j) holds a 1 if Mi accepts wj, else 0. Row i is exactly the characteristic vector of the language L(Mi) — its 1's mark which strings belong to that language.

Interactive · an illustrative accept/reject tablevalues are hypothetical — real TM behavior is uncomputable to display in general

Each cell: does Mi (row) accept wj (column)? The diagonal — does Mi accept wi? — is outlined. Click Build Ld to complement it.

To build Ld's own characteristic vector, we read off the diagonal (cell (i,i), whether Mi accepts wi) and then complement every bit. In the table above, the diagonal begins 0, 1, 1, 1, …, so the complemented diagonal — Ld's row — begins 1, 0, 0, 0, …. That means Ld contains w₁ = ε, but not w₂, w₃, w₄ (which are 0, 1, 00), and so on.

The punchline, before we even formalize it

By construction, Ld's row disagrees with every single row of the table at exactly one position: its own diagonal position. Row i says "Mi accepts wi? — (some bit)." Ld's row says the opposite at position i. So Ld cannot be row i, for any i — it differs from every row somewhere. Since the rows are every language a TM can accept, Ld is nobody's language. Use the row-picker in the widget above to see this concretely for any row.

Part IV · Diagonalization

15The Barber of Seville

Before formalizing the proof, it's worth sitting with the classic paradox that has exactly the same shape.

The paradox

The barber of Seville is that inhabitant of Seville who shaves every man in Seville who does not shave himself. Question: does the barber shave himself?

If the barber does not shave himself, then — by the definition — he must shave himself, since he shaves everyone who doesn't shave themselves.

If the barber does shave himself, then — by the definition — he cannot, since he only shaves those who don't shave themselves.

Both answers are contradictions. The honest resolution is that no such barber can exist — the definition describes an impossible object.

The dictionary between the two paradoxes
Barber of SevilleLd
Men of SevilleBinary strings w₁, w₂, w₃, …
"Shaves himself""Mi accepts wi"
The barber (shaves exactly those who don't shave themselves)Ld (contains exactly those wi that Mi doesn't accept)
"Does the barber shave himself?""Is wi — the code of the machine that (hypothetically) accepts exactly Ld — in Ld?"
Conclusion: no such barber existsConclusion: no such TM exists — Ld is not RE

The only structural difference: the Barber paradox concludes the barber simply can't exist (it's a statement about a flawed definition of a person). Our version has real mathematical content because Mi, the enumeration, and "accepts" are all rigorously defined objects — which turns "no such barber" into a genuine, provable theorem about Turing machines.

Part V · The Proof

16Ld is not Recursively Enumerable

Everything above was intuition-building. Here is the formal theorem, proved with full rigor — the exact argument the lecture slides give, and where our companion stops.

Theorem

Ld is not a recursively enumerable language. That is, there is no Turing machine that accepts Ld.

Full proof — by contradiction

Assume for contradiction that Ld were L(M) for some Turing machine M.

Since Ld is a language over the alphabet {0,1}, M is a TM with input alphabet {0,1} — so M must appear somewhere in the list of TMs we constructed in §13, since that list includes every TM with input alphabet {0,1}.

So there is at least one code for M — call its position i; that is, M = Mi.

Now ask the fatal question: is wi in Ld?

Case A — suppose wi ∈ Ld. Since Ld = L(M) = L(Mi), this means Mi accepts wi. But by the very definition of Ld (§13), Ld contains only those wj such that Mj does not accept wj. So wi ∈ Ld forces wi ∉ Ld. Contradiction.

Case B — suppose wi ∉ Ld. Since Ld = L(Mi), this means Mi does not accept wi. But then, by definition of Ld, wi is exactly the kind of string that belongs to Ld. So wi ∉ Ld forces wi ∈ Ld. Contradiction.

Conclude. Since wi can neither be in Ld nor fail to be in Ld, our assumption that M exists is contradicted. That is, Ld is not a recursively enumerable language. ∎

What exactly did we just prove?

Not "no algorithm decides Ld" (that would only be undecidability). We proved something strictly stronger: no TM accepts Ld at all — not even a TM that's allowed to loop forever on rejected strings. Ld lives entirely outside the RE ring in the §7 diagram, in the "non-RE" region alongside the mortal matrix problem and the Entscheidungsproblem.

Trace the contradiction back through the diagonal table widget

This is exactly the "row picker" experiment from §14, made rigorous. If M accepted Ld, then row i of the table (the characteristic vector of L(Mi)) would have to be identical to Ld's row — they're the same language, so the same set of strings. But we built Ld's row by flipping the diagonal, which guarantees it disagrees with row i at column i specifically. Two rows can't be identical and disagree at one position — that's the contradiction, stated as a picture instead of a sentence.

Part V · The Proof

17Why This Matters

It's easy to read a diagonalization proof and file it away as a cute trick. Here's why it's load-bearing for the rest of the course:

It's the strongest kind of impossibility result. We didn't just fail to find an algorithm — we proved none can exist, of any kind, even a badly-behaved one. Every future undecidability proof (Halting Problem included) will reduce a new problem to something like this, rather than re-deriving diagonalization from scratch.

Self-reference is now a formal tool, not just a philosophical curiosity. "Feed a machine its own description" is the move that will resurface constantly — it's the engine behind the Halting Problem's undecidability proof, which is next on the syllabus.

The RE/recursive split is now concrete, not just definitional. You've seen a language that's not even RE — a genuine inhabitant of the outermost region of the §7 diagram, not a hypothetical placeholder.

Coming up next

The lecture continues from here into closure properties of recursive and RE languages — e.g. recursive languages are closed under complement, and a language whose complement is also RE turns out to be recursive. That's genuinely useful machinery for the Halting Problem proof to follow, but it's outside the scope of this companion, which stops exactly at the Ld result above.

Part VI · Review

18Trap Museum

Every one of these has shown up in a real exam answer somewhere. Read them once now, then again the night before the exam.

"Undecidable" and "not RE" are not the same thing

Undecidable means not recursive: no TM that always halts correctly on both accepts and rejects. That still allows a TM that accepts correctly but might loop on some rejects (RE-but-not-recursive, like the Halting Problem). Not RE is strictly stronger: no TM whatsoever accepts the language, badly-behaved or not. Ld is not RE — a harder claim than mere undecidability, which is why it needs the sharper diagonalization tool rather than a garden-variety reduction.

Every string plays two roles at once — don't lose track of which

In Ld's definition, wi is simultaneously "the input string" and "the code of machine Mi." Students often silently assume these are two different, unrelated strings — but the entire proof depends on them being the same string, run through two different lenses (once as data, once as a machine description).

The proof never needs to compute whether M_i accepts w_i

Deciding "does Mi accept wi?" for an arbitrary i is itself (spoiler for next lecture) undecidable! The diagonalization argument doesn't need to actually compute this — it only reasons logically about the two cases (accepts / doesn't accept) and shows both lead to contradiction, assuming M exists. This is a proof by contradiction about existence, not an algorithm that inspects machines.

Multiple codes per TM is not a hole in the proof

Students sometimes worry: "but M has 24 different valid codes — which one is Mi?" The proof only needs at least one code position i with M = Mi — step 3 of the proof says exactly this ("there is at least one code for M"). It doesn't matter that other codes for the same M sit at other positions in the enumeration; the contradiction fires at whichever i you pick.

Fixed assignments (q₁, q₂, X₁, X₂, X₃, D₁, D₂) are not "free choices you happened to make"

It's tempting to think the whole numbering scheme is arbitrary. It isn't: the start state is always integer 1, the unique accept state is always integer 2, 0/1/blank are always 1/2/3, and L/R are always 1/2. Only extra states and extra tape symbols beyond these get a free assignment. Miscounting which parts are fixed is a fast way to build a string that looks plausible but decodes to nonsense.

L_d is not "the Halting Problem" — it's a different (related) language

Ld = {wi | Mi does not accept wi}, where the input is always the machine's own code. The Halting-Problem-style language you'll meet next lecture, sometimes called A_TM, is {⟨M,w⟩ | M accepts w} for independent M and w — a pair, not a single self-referential string. They're built with the same diagonalization DNA, but they are not the same set, and mixing them up on an exam is an easy way to garble a proof.

Complementing the diagonal is essential — complementing anything else doesn't work

The whole trick relies on comparing Ld's row against row i specifically at column i. Only the diagonal guarantees that, for every row i, the disagreement lands at a column that row actually controls (column i). Flipping some other fixed column (say, always column 1) would only guarantee disagreement with row 1 — every other row could still coincide with the resulting vector.

The "1w as a binary integer" trick isn't just "read w in binary"

If you skip the leading-1 trick and just read w directly as binary, 0, 00, and 000 would all collide at the integer 0 — leading zeros vanish in ordinary binary notation. Prepending a 1 first is exactly what disambiguates strings that differ only in leading zeros, giving every string in {0,1}* — including ε — a unique positive-integer address.

19Practice Problems

Q1. Using the enumeration scheme from §9, what is w₁₂? Show your working (don't just read it off the widget).

Solution

Write 12 in binary: 12 = 1100₂. Strip the leading 1 (the marker bit): remaining bits are 100. So w₁₂ = 100. Sanity check via the widget in §9 confirms this.

Q2. A TM has states q₁, q₂, q₃, q₄ and tape alphabet {0, 1, B, X} (X is an extra symbol). Using the fixed-assignment rules, encode the single transition δ(q₂, X) = (q₄, 1, L).

Solution

States: q₂ → i = 2, q₄ → k = 4. Symbols: X is the one "extra" tape symbol beyond 0, 1, B — since only three symbols (0=X₁, 1=X₂, B=X₃) are fixed, X gets the next free integer, X = X₄ → j = 4 (as the scanned symbol); the written symbol is 1 = X₂ → l = 2. Direction: L = D₁ → m = 1.

Code: 0i10j10k10l10m = 0²1 0⁴1 0⁴1 0²1 0¹ = 00 1 0000 1 0000 1 00 1 0, i.e. 0010000100001001 0 → concatenated: 00100001000010010.

Q3. Is 01011 a valid TM code? Walk through all three validity checks from §12.

Solution

Starts with 0? Yes. No "111" run? Yes, none present. Block structure? Split on "11": we get one piece before any "11" — but look closely, 01011 only contains a single "11" right at the end, splitting it into "010" and "" (empty tail). An empty block after splitting on "11" is invalid (§12, "clean block structure"). Also, even the non-empty piece "010" splits on single "1" into just 3 groups (0, 0, and nothing after) — not the required 5. So 01011 is not a valid TM code — check it against the widget in §12 to confirm.

Q4. True or false, with justification: "Since Ld is not RE, its complement must be RE."

Solution

Nothing proved in this lecture licenses that conclusion. We showed Ld has no accepting TM at all — that's a statement about Ld alone. It says nothing yet about Ld's complement, which needs its own separate argument (this is exactly the "complements of recursive and RE languages" material flagged in §17 as next lecture's territory — don't reach for a theorem before it's been proved).

Q5. Suppose (hypothetically, for a small illustrative table) the diagonal of the accept/reject table were 1, 1, 0, 1, … instead of 0, 1, 1, 1, …. What would the first four bits of Ld's characteristic vector be, and which of w₁…w₄ would be members?

Solution

Complement bit-by-bit: 1,1,0,1 → 0,0,1,0. So Ld would not contain w₁ = ε or w₂ = 0, would contain w₃ = 1, and would not contain w₄ = 00. (You can reproduce this by hand-editing the illustrative table in §14 — the mechanism is the same regardless of which specific diagonal you start from.)

Q6. Why is it safe to restrict the "does M accept w?" problem to a binary input alphabet, rather than proving non-RE-ness directly for TMs over an arbitrary alphabet?

Solution

Because the binary-alphabet version is a special case of the general version (§8). If even the restricted, easier-looking special case has no accepting TM, then the fully general version — which has to handle at least as much, including every binary instance — certainly can't have one either. Proving impossibility for a special case is enough to prove impossibility for the general problem; it's proving existence of an algorithm where you'd need the general case to carry the weight.

20Cheat Sheet

Core definitions

RE: some TM accepts it (may loop on rejects).

Recursive / decidable: some TM accepts and always halts on rejects too.

Not RE: no TM accepts it — stronger than undecidable.

Enumerating strings

Treat 1w as a binary integer i → w is wi. Order: ε, 0, 1, 00, 01, 10, 11, 000, …

Equivalently: length first, then lexicographic.

Fixed encoding assignments

q₁=start, q₂=accept (always)

X₁=0, X₂=1, X₃=B (always)

D₁=L, D₂=R (always)

Encoding formulas

Transition δ(qi,Xj)=(qk,Xl,Dm): 0ⁱ10ʲ10ᵏ10ˡ10ᵐ

Whole machine: transition codes joined by 11

Validity of a code

✓ starts with 0   ✓ no "111" run

✓ splits on "11" into blocks, each splitting on "1" into exactly 5 groups. Invalid → Mi = empty-language machine.

Ld and the proof skeleton

Ld = {wi | Mi doesn't accept wi}

Assume M=Mi accepts Ld → ask "is wi∈Ld?" → both answers self-contradict → no such M → Ld not RE. ∎
