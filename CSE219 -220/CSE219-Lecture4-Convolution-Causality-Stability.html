The shape of this lecture

Orientation · how the 70 slides fit together

Lecture 3 told you why convolution is the universal input–output rule for an LTI system. Lecture 4 does two things: it makes you fluent at actually computing convolutions, and then it shows that two of the most important system properties — causality and stability — are both readable straight off h.

That second point is the quiet punchline of the whole lecture. For a general system, "is it causal?" and "is it stable?" are questions about infinitely many possible inputs. For an LTI system, both collapse into a single one-line test on one signal. That is an enormous reduction, and it is only true because of convolution.

The one sentence to carry out of Lecture 4

Everything an LTI system will ever do — to every input, forever — is encoded in one signal h. Convolution reads that encoding to produce outputs; the support of h decides causality; the area under |h| decides stability.

The recipe, in discrete time

Slides 2–5 · reflect, shift, multiply, add

"Keep the first signal just as is, flip the second, slide rightward, find overlaps, then multiply-and-add." That single sentence from slide 2 is the entire computational content of convolution. The rest is bookkeeping.

y[n] = (x ∗ h)[n] = ∞Σk=−∞ x[k] h[n − k]

Reading the formula honestly

There are two different indices in that expression and confusing them is the single most common source of errors in this course. Keep them strictly separate:

SymbolRoleStatus inside the sum
kThe dummy summation index. It sweeps over every sample of the input.Varies. It is consumed by the sum and does not appear in the answer.
nThe output time you are currently computing.Frozen. Fixed for the whole sum. Change it and you do a brand-new sum.

Slide 4 makes this concrete: you sweep across the entire k-axis of x[k], collapse everything into one number, and that number is deposited at a single location n on the output axis. To fill in the whole output signal, you repeat the sweep once per output sample.

Exam alert · notation discipline

Once you write the sum, n must be treated as a constant. Writing something like "y[n] = Σ x[n]h[n−k]" — an n where a k should be — is fatal, because it silently pulls x out of the sum. Examiners look for this specific slip.

Why h[n − k] is a reflection followed by a shift

Slide 5 walks it in two moves, and it is worth understanding rather than memorising, because the sign conventions trip up almost everyone once.

1

Reflect: h[k] → h[−k]

Replacing k by −k mirrors the signal about the vertical axis. A sample that lived at k = 3 now lives at k = −3.

2

Shift right by n: h[−k] → h[−(k − n)] = h[n − k]

Wherever h[0] sat before, it now sits at k = n. Slide 5 checks exactly this: for h[2 − k], the value h[0] appears at k = 2.

The anchor trick

Never re-derive the reflected picture from scratch. Just find where h[0] lands — it lands at k = n, always — and then remember that the reflected copy extends backwards (to smaller k) along whatever direction h originally extended forwards. If h[k] is nonzero on 0 ≤ k ≤ 2, then h[n−k] is nonzero on n−2 ≤ k ≤ n.

Edge case · which signal do you flip?

Convolution is commutative, so x ∗ h = h ∗ x and you may flip either one. Slide 58 quietly exploits this ("yes, we shuffled it!"). Practical rule: flip the simpler signal — usually the one with shorter or more regular support. Flipping a step function gives you a clean half-line window; flipping a messy finite sequence gives you a mess.

Brainstorm · what is convolution really doing?

Look at the sum again: y[n] = Σ x[k]h[n−k]. Read it as: the output now (at time n) is a weighted sum of every input sample, where an input that arrived at time k gets weight h[n−k] — a weight that depends only on how long ago it arrived. That "how long ago" is exactly the elapsed time n − k. So h is a memory profile: it says how strongly the system still remembers an impulse that hit it m samples ago. Causality will say the system cannot remember the future; stability will say the total memory must be finite.

The same recipe, in continuous time

Slides 6–8 · the sum becomes an integral

Nothing conceptual changes. Samples become a continuum, the dummy index k becomes a dummy variable τ, and "add over k" becomes "integrate over τ".

y(t) = (x ∗ h)(t) = ∞∫−∞ x(τ) h(t − τ) dτ
StepDiscrete timeContinuous time
1 · Pick the output locationchoose nchoose t

What actually gets harder in continuous time

The recipe is identical, but two practical differences matter for exam work:

  • The answer comes in cases, and the case boundaries are the interesting part. In discrete time you can often just tabulate. In continuous time you must determine, as a function of t, what the limits of integration are. Slide 7 shows the general picture: the overlap runs between limits that themselves depend on t (the slide marks them a, t−d, b, t−c).
  • Most of the work is finding the overlap, not doing the integral. The integrals in this lecture are all elementary — exponentials. If you set the limits correctly, the calculus takes one line.
The overlap procedure that never fails

Write down two inequalities and intersect them.

  1. Where is x(τ) nonzero? → an inequality in τ alone.
  2. Where is h(t−τ) nonzero? → an inequality in τ involving t. Solve it for τ, and remember that dividing by −1 flips the inequality.
  3. Intersect. If the intersection is empty for some range of t, that range gives y(t) = 0.
  4. The values of t where the intersection changes form are your case boundaries.
Exam alert · the sign flip in step 2

This is where marks are lost. Example: h(t−τ) = u(t−τ) is nonzero when t − τ ≥ 0, i.e. τ ≤ t — not τ ≥ t. The reflection turns a right-sided signal into a left-sided window. Slide 49 uses exactly this. Write the inequality in the original argument first, then solve; never guess the direction.

Edge case · why single points don't matter

In continuous time, whether you write τ ≤ t or τ < t has no effect on the integral: a single point has zero measure. This is why slide 39's answer works whether you call the boundary t > 0 or t ≥ 0. In discrete time the opposite is true — including or excluding k = n changes the answer by a whole sample. Be strict discretely, relaxed continuously.

Brainstorm · why is it t − τ and not τ − t?

Suppose the system were memoryless with h = δ. Then y(t) = ∫x(τ)δ(t−τ)dτ = x(t) by the sifting property — the identity system, exactly as it should be. Now try the wrong sign: ∫x(τ)h(τ−t)dτ with h = δ gives the same answer, because δ is even — so this test doesn't distinguish them. Use a delay instead: h(t) = δ(t−2). The correct formula gives y(t) = x(t−2), a delay. The wrong sign gives x(t+2), an advance — a system that predicts the future. The minus sign is what keeps time pointing forwards.

Example 2.2 — two short finite sequences

Slides 11–19 · the fully mechanical case

Both signals have finite support, so the whole convolution is a handful of additions. Do this one until it is muscle memory, because every harder example is this one with an infinite sum bolted on.

The problem

x[k] has two nonzero samples: x[0] = 0.5 and x[1] = 2.
h[k] = 1 for k = 0, 1, 2 and zero elsewhere — a three-tap running sum.

Compute y[n] = x[n] ∗ h[n].

Before computing: predict the answer's support

This costs three seconds and catches most errors. If x is nonzero on [a₁, b₁] and h on [a₂, b₂], then y is nonzero only on [a₁+a₂, b₁+b₂]. Here: [0+0, 1+2] = [0, 3]. So expect exactly four possibly-nonzero output samples, and expect zeros outside. Slide 19 confirms it.

Support and length rule

Supports add. Lengths obey length(y) = length(x) + length(h) − 1. Here 2 + 3 − 1 = 4. ✓

The five regimes of the slide

Recall the anchor trick: h[n−k] occupies k ∈ [n−2, n], as slide 12 draws. Now slide it past x, which sits at k ∈ {0, 1}.

Brainstorm · why the plateau at 2.5?

h is a window of three ones — a moving sum of the last three inputs. Since x is only two samples long, once the window fully covers x it keeps covering it for a while, and the output flattens. Widen h to four ones and the plateau lengthens by one; narrow it to a single 1 and h = δ and the output is just x back again. The width of h is the system's memory span.

Edge case · a zero sample inside x

Suppose x were {0.5, 0, 2} at k = 0, 1, 2. The support rule now predicts [0, 4], five samples — and it is right, even though one input sample is zero. Support is about the outermost nonzero samples, not the count of nonzero samples. Don't compress out interior zeros.

Exam alert · showing your work

Full credit here almost always requires the sketch of h[n−k] for at least one representative n, plus an explicit statement of the overlap range in each regime. A bare table of numbers, even if correct, tends to be marked down. Draw one picture, state the ranges, then tabulate.

Example 2.3 — the geometric sum

Slides 20–27 · αnu[n] ∗ u[n], with 0 < α < 1

The first example with infinite support. The mechanics are unchanged; what's new is that the overlap now contains infinitely many candidate samples, and you must recognise the resulting sum.

The problem

x[n] = αnu[n],   h[n] = u[n],   0 < α < 1. Find y[n].

Step 1 · the reflected step is a left-facing window

h[k] = u[k] is nonzero for k ≥ 0. Reflecting gives u[−k], nonzero for k ≤ 0. Shifting by n gives u[n−k], nonzero for k ≤ n — an infinite window with a hard right edge at k = n, exactly as slide 21 draws.

Memorise this pair

u[k] ⟹ k ≥ 0  (right-sided).   u[n−k] ⟹ k ≤ n  (left-sided, edge at n).
Convolving anything with u therefore means "sum everything up to now" — u is the running-accumulator system. That framing alone predicts the shape of the answer before you compute anything.

Step 2 · intersect the two supports

x[k] lives on k ≥ 0; h[n−k] lives on k ≤ n. Intersection: 0 ≤ k ≤ n. This is empty precisely when n < 0.

Case n < 0

No common k. y[n] = 0.

Case n ≥ 0

Product is αk on 0 ≤ k ≤ n, zero elsewhere (slide 24).

Step 3 · sum the finite geometric series

y[n] = nΣk=0 αk = 1 + α + α2 + ⋯ + αn = 1 − αn+11 − α
y[n] = 1 − αn+11 − α  u[n]
Exam alert · count the terms

The sum from k = 0 to n has n + 1 terms, not n. That is precisely where the exponent n+1 in the numerator comes from. The single most common error in this example is writing (1 − αn)/(1 − α). Sanity-check at n = 0: the sum is just the single term 1, and (1 − α¹)/(1 − α) = 1 ✓, whereas the wrong version gives 0 ✗.

Step 4 · read the shape

As n → ∞, αn+1 → 0, so y[n] → 1/(1 − α). The output climbs monotonically from 1 and saturates — slide 27. Physically: the accumulator keeps adding, but the increments shrink geometrically, so the total converges.

Edge case · what if α ≥ 1?

The problem states 0 < α < 1, and that restriction is load-bearing. Explore the alternatives, because they are natural exam variations:

  • α = 1: the closed form 0/0 breaks down. Go back to the sum: 1 + 1 + ⋯ + 1 = n + 1. So y[n] = (n+1)u[n], a ramp. This is u ∗ u — the accumulator fed a constant.
  • α > 1: the closed form is still algebraically valid for each finite n, but the output now grows without bound. The system is still stable (h = u is not, actually — see below), but the input itself is unbounded.
  • α < 0: formula still holds; the output oscillates as it converges to 1/(1−α).
Brainstorm · the system here is not stable

h[n] = u[n] has Σ|h[n]| = ∞, so by §10's test the accumulator is BIBO unstable. Yet here it produced a bounded output. No contradiction: BIBO stability requires every bounded input to give a bounded output. The particular input αnu[n] happens to decay fast enough. Feed it the bounded input x[n] = u[n] instead and you get the unbounded ramp (n+1)u[n] — one counterexample is all it takes. This example and §10 are designed to be read together.

Example 2.5 — a left-sided input

Slides 28–34 · 2nu[−n] ∗ u[n]

Same accumulator, but now the input lives in the past and grows toward n = 0. The case split moves, and the geometric sum becomes an infinite one.

The problem

x[n] = 2nu[−n],   h[n] = u[n].

Edge case · is 2nu[−n] growing or decaying?

Read it carefully. u[−n] is nonzero for n ≤ 0, so we only ever evaluate 2n at negative n: 2⁰ = 1, 2⁻¹ = ½, 2⁻² = ¼, … The signal decays as you move left and peaks at n = 0. Despite the base being 2, this is a decaying, absolutely-summable signal. Students routinely mis-sketch this as exploding.

The two cases

x[k] lives on k ≤ 0. h[n−k] lives on k ≤ n. The intersection is k ≤ min(0, n) — and that min is what creates the split. Note there is no n for which the overlap is empty, so y is never identically zero.

CaseOverlapSumValue
n < 0k ≤ nΣk=−∞n 2k = 2n + 2n−1 + ⋯2n+1
n ≥ 0k ≤ 0Σk=−∞0 2k = ⋯ + ⅛ + ¼ + ½ + 12
Summing a left-infinite geometric series

The trick on slides 32–33: factor out the largest term, which is the one at the right edge.

Σk=−∞n 2k = 2n(1 + ½ + ¼ + ⋯) = 2n · 11 − ½ = 2n · 2 = 2n+1

General form: a two-sided-decaying tail summed leftwards from its largest term rn with ratio 1/r gives rn/(1 − 1/r) = rn+1/(r − 1). Here r = 2 and the denominator is 1.

y[n] = 2n+1,n < 0 2,n ≥ 0
Exam alert · check continuity at the seam

At n = −1 the first branch gives 2⁰ = 1; at n = 0 the second gives 2. The jump from 1 to 2 is exactly x[0] = 1, the last sample the accumulator swallowed. Whenever your answer comes in cases, evaluate both branches near the boundary and confirm the step matches the sample you just added. It catches off-by-one errors instantly.

Brainstorm · why does the output stop changing after n = 0?

Because the accumulator has, by then, already collected everything the input will ever deliver — x is zero for all n > 0. From that moment the running total is frozen at Σx[k] = 2. So the final constant value of x ∗ u is always the total sum of the input, whenever that sum converges. Use this as a one-second check on any convolution-with-a-step problem.

Example 2.6 — the continuous integrator

Slides 35–40 · e−atu(t) ∗ u(t), a > 0

The continuous twin of Example 2.3. Convolving with u(t) is integration from −∞ to now, so this is "the running integral of a decaying exponential".

The problem

x(t) = e−atu(t),   h(t) = u(t),   a > 0.

Finding the overlap

  • x(τ) = e−aτu(τ) is nonzero for τ ≥ 0.
  • h(t−τ) = u(t−τ) is nonzero when t−τ ≥ 0, i.e. τ ≤ t.
  • Intersection: 0 ≤ τ ≤ t — empty exactly when t < 0.
y(t) = t∫0 e−aτdτ = [ −1ae−aτ ]0t = 1a (1 − e−at)
y(t) = 1a (1 − e−at) u(t)
Three checks worth doing every time
  • Initial value: y(0) = (1/a)(1 − 1) = 0 ✓ — an integral over a zero-width interval must vanish.
  • Final value: y(∞) = 1/a ✓ — and indeed ∫₀∞e−aτdτ = 1/a, the total area of the input. Same principle as §5's brainstorm.
  • Initial slope: y′(0⁺) = e0 = 1 = x(0⁺) ✓ — the derivative of a running integral is the integrand.
Edge case · a → 0

Let a → 0⁺. Then x(t) → u(t), and the answer should become the ramp t u(t). Check: (1/a)(1 − e−at) ≈ (1/a)(at − a²t²/2 + ⋯) → t ✓. The 1/a blow-up and the vanishing bracket cancel exactly. This is the continuous analogue of the α → 1 case in §4.

Exam alert · don't lose the u(t)

Writing y(t) = (1/a)(1 − e−at) without the trailing u(t) is wrong: that expression is negative for t < 0, whereas the true answer is zero there. Either attach the step or state the cases explicitly. Half-credit lives here.

Distributivity, and Example 2.10

Slides 41–47 · split a hard input into two easy ones

Convolution distributes over addition. That is not a curiosity — it is a problem-solving strategy, and slide 43's "oops, different expressions on the left and right side!" is the cue to use it.

x ∗ (h1 + h2) = x ∗ h1 + x ∗ h2      (x1 + x2) ∗ h = x1 ∗ h + x2 ∗ h
Brainstorm · where does distributivity come from?

Two ways to see it. Algebraically: the convolution sum is linear in each argument, and sums pull apart. Systemically: the second identity is exactly the superposition half of linearity — feed a sum of inputs, get the sum of outputs. The first identity says something different and more interesting: two systems in parallel, with their outputs added, form a single LTI system whose impulse response is h₁ + h₂. Compare with the associativity result (systems in cascade ⟹ impulse responses convolve). Parallel adds, series convolves.

Example 2.10

The problem

x[n] = (½)nu[n] + 2nu[−n],   h[n] = u[n].

Attempting this directly is painful: the formula for x[k] changes at k = 0, so any single sum has to be broken up anyway. Slide 44's move is to break it up at the top instead:

x1[n] = (½)nu[n]   ·   x2[n] = 2nu[−n]   ⟹   y = x1∗h + x2∗h = y1 + y2

And now both pieces are already solved:

Edge case · the algebra in y₁

Example 2.3 gives (1 − αn+1)/(1 − α). With α = ½: denominator ½, so the whole thing is 2(1 − (½)n+1) = 2 − 2·2−n−1 = 2 − 2−n. Check n = 0: 2 − 1 = 1 ✓ (only the k = 0 term contributes). Check n → ∞: → 2 ✓ = total input sum Σ(½)k.

Adding the pieces

Careful: the two answers have different case structures, but both break at n = 0, so they align.

  • n < 0: y₁ = 0 (its u[n] kills it) and y₂ = 2n+1. So y = 2n+1.
  • n ≥ 0: y = (2 − 2−n) + 2 = 4 − 2−n.
y[n] = 2n+1,n < 0 4 − 2−n,n ≥ 0
Sanity check on the final value

The output must converge to the total sum of the input. Σx = Σn≥0(½)n + Σn≤02n − x[0] (the sample at 0 is in both pieces) = 2 + 2 − 1... but careful: here x is defined as the sum of the two expressions, so x[0] = 1 + 1 = 2 and no subtraction is needed. Total = 2 + 2 = 4 ✓, matching y[∞] = 4. Slide 47 draws exactly this.

Exam alert · the double-counted sample

Notice x[0] = (½)⁰ + 2⁰ = 2, because both u[n] and u[−n] equal 1 at n = 0. Slide 42's sketch shows the value 2 at the origin. If a problem instead defines the signal piecewise ("(½)ⁿ for n > 0, 2ⁿ for n ≤ 0"), the origin is counted once and your split must subtract the duplicate. Read which one you were given.

Two one-sided exponentials

Slides 48–52 · when neither signal is a step

Every earlier example had u as one of the two signals, which made the reflected window trivially a half-line. Here both signals decay, so the integrand is genuinely a function of τ on both sides. This is the template for real exam problems.

The problem

x(t) = etu(−t)  (left-sided, peaks at 0),   h(t) = e−tu(t)  (right-sided, peaks at 0).

Step 1 · the two supports

  • x(τ) = eτu(−τ) ≠ 0 ⟺ τ ≤ 0.
  • h(t−τ) = e−(t−τ)u(t−τ) ≠ 0 ⟺ τ ≤ t.
  • Overlap: τ ≤ min(0, t). Never empty — so y(t) is nonzero for all t, and there is no "no overlap" case here.

Step 2 · the integrand simplifies beautifully

x(τ)h(t−τ) = eτ · e−(t−τ) = e−t e2τ

Because t is frozen, e−t is a constant and pulls straight out of the integral. Everything left is ∫e2τdτ.

The general move

Whenever the integrand factors as (function of t only) × (function of τ only), pull the t-part out. What remains is a pure τ-integral whose only t-dependence is in the limits. This is why exponential-times-exponential problems are tractable at all.

Step 3 · two cases from the min

Case t < 0  → min is t
y(t) = e−t∫−∞te2τdτ = e−t·e2t2 = ½ et
Case t ≥ 0  → min is 0
y(t) = e−t∫−∞0e2τdτ = e−t·12 = ½ e−t
y(t) = ½ et,t < 0 ½ e−t,t ≥ 0   =   ½ e−|t|
Brainstorm · why is the answer even?

Look at the inputs: h(t) = e−tu(t) and x(t) = etu(−t) = h(−t). So we are convolving a signal with its own reflection. That operation is autocorrelation, and the autocorrelation of any real signal is always an even function — with its maximum at the origin, which is precisely where the two copies align best. The peak value ½ is ∫h² = ∫₀∞e−2tdt = ½, the energy of h. Recognising this structure lets you predict the entire shape before integrating.

Edge case · what if both decay at the same rate on the same side?

Try x = h = e−tu(t). Now the overlap is 0 ≤ τ ≤ t and the integrand is e−τe−(t−τ) = e−t, independent of τ. The integral is just e−t × (length t), giving y(t) = t e−tu(t). That extra factor of t is the hallmark of a repeated rate — and it is exactly the h₂ from slide 63. Equal rates produce a t; distinct rates a ≠ b produce (e−at − e−bt)/(b − a). Know both.

Exam alert · one signal on each side is the easy case

Because x is left-sided and h right-sided, the overlap is never empty and there are only two cases. If instead both were right-sided you would get "no overlap" plus "partial overlap" — also two cases, but with a zero region. If both have finite support you can get four or five cases. Count your cases before integrating: the number of case boundaries equals the number of distinct "edge collision" events as the sliding signal passes.

Causality — no time travel, please

Slides 53–56 · h(t) = 0 for t < 0

A causal system's output at time t depends only on the input up to time t. For a general system that is a statement about infinitely many inputs. For an LTI system it collapses into a statement about one signal.

Continuous:  causal ⟺ h(t) = 0 for all t < 0
Discrete:  causal ⟺ h[n] = 0 for all n < 0

Why the test is exactly this

Slide 55 gives the intuition and it is worth stating precisely, because "prove the causality condition" is a standard exam item.

⟸

If h(t) = 0 for t < 0, the system is causal.

Write y(t) = ∫h(τ)x(t−τ)dτ (the commuted form). Since h(τ) vanishes for τ < 0, the integral runs only over τ ≥ 0, so it only ever reads x at arguments t − τ ≤ t. Past and present only. ✓

⟹

If the system is causal, then h(t) = 0 for t < 0.

h is by definition the response to δ(t), an input that is zero everywhere before t = 0. A causal system fed an input that is zero up to time 0 must produce an output that is zero up to time 0. Hence h(t) = 0 for t < 0. ✓ Slide 55's caption — "nothing before" the poke — is exactly this argument.

The reading of the picture

Causality is a statement about the support of h, and nothing else. Its size, sign, shape, and growth rate are all irrelevant. Ask one question: is there any nonzero value strictly to the left of the origin? If yes, non-causal. If no, causal.

Edge case · the boundary point t = 0

The condition is h(t) = 0 for t < 0, strictly. A nonzero value at t = 0 is perfectly causal — indeed h = δ(t) is the identity system, the most causal system there is. Likewise h[0] ≠ 0 is fine in discrete time; that is just an instantaneous (memoryless) contribution.

Edge case · causal ≠ memoryless

Two different properties. Memoryless means output at t depends only on the input at t — for LTI that forces h(t) = cδ(t). Causal only forbids the future. Every memoryless system is causal; almost no causal system is memoryless.

Brainstorm · are non-causal systems useless?

Not at all — they are just not implementable in real time. If your independent variable is space rather than time (image processing) there is no arrow of causality to respect: a blur kernel centred on a pixel reads neighbours on both sides. And if you have recorded data stored on disk, "the future" is already available, so offline filtering with a non-causal h is routine. Non-causality is a constraint on streaming, not on mathematics.

Exam alert · causality of an input signal

The lecture also uses the word for signals (slides 68–69): a signal is called causal if it is zero for t < 0. Same condition, different object. Read whether you are being asked about a system's h or about a signal x.

Stability — bounded in, bounded out

Slides 57–59 · absolute integrability of h

BIBO stability: every input bounded by some B must produce an output bounded by some finite number. Again, for LTI systems it reduces to one integral.

Continuous:  stable ⟺ ∞∫−∞ |h(t)| dt < ∞
Discrete:  stable ⟺ ∞Σn=−∞ |h[n]| < ∞

The sufficiency argument (slide 58)

Start from the commuted form — slide 58's "yes, we shuffled it!" is doing real work here, because it puts h in the position with the free variable.

|y(t)| = | ∫ h(τ)x(t−τ)dτ | ≤ ∫ |h(τ)| |x(t−τ)|dτ ≤ B ∫ |h(τ)|dτ

Three moves, each worth naming in an exam answer: (1) the magnitude of an integral is at most the integral of the magnitude — the triangle inequality; (2) |x(t−τ)| ≤ B because a bound that holds for all times holds for the shifted argument too; (3) B is a constant and comes out. If ∫|h| is finite, the output is bounded by B∫|h| — a bound that does not depend on t. Stable.

The necessity half (worth knowing, not on the slides)

The slides prove "absolutely integrable ⟹ stable". The converse is proved by exhibiting one bad input. In discrete time, choose x[n] = sign(h[−n]), which is bounded by 1. Then y[0] = Σh[k]x[−k] = Σh[k]·sign(h[k]) = Σ|h[k]|. So if the sum diverges, this bounded input already blows the output up at a single time instant. Hence stability requires absolute summability. Quoting this construction earns full marks on "prove the condition is necessary and sufficient".

Edge case · decaying is not enough

A signal can tend to zero and still fail the test. h[n] = 1/n for n ≥ 1 decays to zero, but Σ1/n diverges — unstable. Meanwhile h[n] = 1/n² converges — stable. "It goes to zero" is not the test; "the total area is finite" is the test. Exponential decay always passes; polynomial decay needs checking.

Edge case · bounded h does not imply stable

h[n] = u[n] is bounded by 1 everywhere, yet Σ|h| = ∞. The accumulator is unstable. Conversely, h can be unbounded and still stable if the spike is narrow enough — e.g. h(t) = t−1/2 on (0,1] is unbounded at 0 but has finite area 2. Boundedness of h and stability are independent properties.

The two periodicity puzzles

Slides 60–62 · can h be periodic?

Two short questions with the same striking answer: only the trivial h = 0 survives. These are exactly the kind of one-line reasoning problems that appear on exams, so learn the arguments, not just the verdicts.

Question 1

If the LTI system is causal, can h(t) be periodic?

Answer · no, except for h ≡ 0

Suppose h has period T > 0 and is nonzero somewhere, say h(t₀) ≠ 0. Periodicity means the value repeats in both directions:

h(t₀) = h(t₀ − T) = h(t₀ − 2T) = ⋯ = h(t₀ − kT) = ⋯

Since T > 0 and k can be any positive integer, eventually t₀ − kT < 0. So there is a strictly negative time at which h is nonzero — contradicting causality. Therefore only h ≡ 0 can be both.

The idea in one line

Periodicity propagates values backwards without limit; causality forbids anything to the left of the origin. A periodic signal has no beginning, and causality demands one.

Question 2

If the LTI system is stable, can h(t) be periodic?

Answer · again no, except for h ≡ 0

If h is periodic with period T and nonzero, then ∫one period|h| = A > 0. The whole axis contains infinitely many periods, each contributing the same A:

∫−∞∞|h(t)|dt = ⋯ + A + A + A + ⋯ = ∞

which fails the stability test. Only h ≡ 0 is both stable and periodic.

Edge case · why must A be strictly positive?

This is the subtle step. If h is nonzero somewhere in a period, is the integral of |h| over that period necessarily positive? For any reasonable (piecewise-continuous) signal, yes: a nonzero value at a point of continuity persists over a small interval, giving positive area. It could fail only for pathological signals that are nonzero on a set of measure zero — and those are zero as far as every integral in this course is concerned. Note the absolute value is essential: without it, a signal like cos could integrate to zero over each period and the argument would collapse.

Brainstorm · the sharper statement

Question 2's argument never used periodicity in full. All it needed was: |h| does not decay. Generalise it — if h(t) does not tend to 0 as |t| → ∞, the system is unstable. Periodic, constant, oscillating, growing: all unstable. The converse is false (see §10's 1/n example), so "decays to zero" is necessary but not sufficient. That asymmetry is a favourite true/false item.

Exam alert · answer the question that was asked

Both answers are "no, except the trivial case". Say the trivial case out loud. h ≡ 0 is periodic (every T is a period), it is causal, and it is stable — it's the system that outputs nothing. Answers that assert a flat "no" without the exception are usually docked a mark.

Classification practice

Slides 63–69 · decide causality and stability, then the delay problem

Two systems, then a short design question. The method is always the same: sketch the support, then compute the area.

System 1

h1(t) = e2tu(−2 − t)

Step 1 · unpack the step's argument

This is the whole difficulty of the problem. u(−2 − t) = 1 requires its argument to be non-negative:

−2 − t ≥ 0  ⟺  −t ≥ 2  ⟺  t ≤ −2

So h₁ lives entirely on t ≤ −2 — well to the left of the origin.

Exam alert · the inequality flip

Dividing (or multiplying) by −1 reverses the inequality. Going from −t ≥ 2 to t ≥ −2 instead of t ≤ −2 is the single most-repeated mistake in this section, and it flips both answers. Substitute one test value to confirm: at t = −5, is −2 − (−5) = 3 ≥ 0? Yes → t = −5 is in the support → the support extends leftward ✓.

Step 2 · causality

h₁ is nonzero at, for instance, t = −3, which is before t = 0. Not causal. The system responds three seconds before it is poked.

Step 3 · stability

∫−∞∞|h₁(t)|dt = ∫−∞−2e2tdt = [ ½e2t ]−∞−2 = ½e−4 − 0 = ½e−4 < ∞

Stable. Note why: on the region where the signal actually lives, e2t is being evaluated at very negative t, so it is decaying as we move left. The positive exponent is a decoy.

Edge case · when is eatu(−t−c) stable?

Whenever a > 0, because the exponential decays in the leftward direction. If instead a < 0 the same signal would grow without bound as t → −∞ and the system would be unstable. Rule of thumb: a right-sided exponential is stable if the exponent is negative; a left-sided exponential is stable if the exponent is positive. The exponent must always point "inward", back toward the origin.

System 2

h2(t) = t e−tu(t)

Causality

u(t) forces h₂ = 0 for t < 0. Causal.

Stability

On t ≥ 0 the integrand is non-negative, so the absolute value does nothing:

∫0∞t e−tdt = Γ(2) = 1! = 1 < ∞

Stable. By parts: ∫te−t = [−te−t]₀∞ + ∫e−t = 0 + 1.

Worth memorising

∫₀∞tne−atdt = n! / an+1. With n = 1, a = 1 you get 1 instantly. This one integral covers most stability questions involving polynomial×exponential impulse responses.

Brainstorm · exponentials always win

h₂ starts at 0, rises to a maximum at t = 1 (differentiate: e−t(1−t) = 0), then decays. The factor t grows without bound, yet the area stays finite — because exponential decay beats any polynomial growth. So t100e−tu(t) is also stable, with area 100!. Multiplying by a polynomial never destroys stability of an exponentially-decaying h.

The minimum-delay problem

Slides 68–69

Let x(t) = e−2tu(t + 2) and y(t) = x(t − a). Find the minimum a for which y is causal.

u(t + 2) = 1 ⟺ t + 2 ≥ 0 ⟺ t ≥ −2. So x switches on at t = −2 — it is nonzero before the origin and therefore not causal as it stands.

Delaying by a moves the whole signal right by a, so the switch-on point moves from −2 to −2 + a. Causality of y requires the start to be at or after the origin:

−2 + a ≥ 0  ⟹  a ≥ 2  ⟹  amin = 2
Edge case · at exactly a = 2

The signal turns on precisely at t = 0. That satisfies "zero for all t < 0", so a = 2 works and the minimum is attained (it is a closed condition, not an open one). Had the problem said "zero for all t ≤ 0", no minimum would exist — you would need a > 2 with no smallest value. Read the inequality you are given.

Exam alert · x(t − a) shifts right for positive a

Replacing t by t − a delays (shifts right) by a. Verify with one point rather than trusting memory: the sample that was at t = −2 now occurs when t − a = −2, i.e. at t = a − 2, which is to the right of −2 when a > 0 ✓. The exponential factor e−2t is a red herring — only the step's argument determines the support.

Procedure to follow under time pressure

1

Identify the supports of both signals.

Write them as inequalities. Predict the output support by adding endpoints.

2

Decide which signal to flip.

Flip the simpler one. If one is a step, flip the step.

3

Write the flipped support as an inequality in k (or τ) involving n (or t).

Solve carefully; flip the inequality if you divide by −1.

4

Intersect. Identify where the form of the intersection changes.

Those are your cases. Enumerate them before computing anything.

5

In each case, write the product and sum/integrate over the overlap only.

Pull out anything that does not depend on the dummy variable.

6

Assemble, then check.

Boundary continuity, initial value, final value = total input area, and support consistency with step 1.

Lecture 5 asks a different question about the same machinery: instead of computing x ∗ h directly, what if we choose inputs that convolution treats especially simply? Complex exponentials turn out to pass through an LTI system unchanged except for a scale factor — they are eigenfunctions of convolution. That observation converts the messy sliding integral into ordinary multiplication, and it is the reason Fourier analysis exists.

The bridge to Lecture 5

If x(t) = ejωt, then y(t) = ∫h(τ)ejω(t−τ)dτ = ejωt · ∫h(τ)e−jωτdτ. The input comes back out, multiplied by a number that depends only on ω. That number is H(ω) — the frequency response — and notice that it exists precisely when h is absolutely integrable. Stability is what makes the frequency response well-defined.
