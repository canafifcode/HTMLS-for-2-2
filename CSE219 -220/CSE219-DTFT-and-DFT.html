You spent the last several lectures earning the top row and the left column: the CTFS turns a continuous periodic signal into a discrete line spectrum, and the CTFT turns a continuous aperiodic signal into a continuous spectrum. Both are proven, both are yours.

Part I · Series to DFT

Sampling a Periodic Signal

We sample the time axis and immediately meet the object every later formula is built from: the impulse train.

Recap: the CTFS

Start where we left off. A continuous-time periodic signal x(t) with period T₀ satisfies x(t + T₀) = x(t), and with ω₀ ≡ 2π/T₀ it has the familiar pair:

CTFS Pair
$$x(t) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} c_k e^{jk\omega_0 t}, \qquad c_k = \frac{1}{T_0}\int_{T_0} x(t)\,e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt$$

Uniform Sampling

Now sample x(t) uniformly with sampling period Ts, and suppose the period T₀ is captured by exactly N samples: T₀ = N Ts, with N a positive integer. That assumption is doing real work — we are choosing a sampling rate that lines up evenly with the signal's own period, so nothing is left over at the boundary.

The resulting discrete sequence is defined simply as x[n] ≡ x(nTs), for every integer n. Because x(t) repeats every T₀ = NTs, the sequence x[n] inherits periodicity too, but now in the discrete index: x[n + N] = x[n].

Interactive · sample a periodic signal
EDGE CASE
N must be an integer

If the sampling rate does not divide T₀ evenly, x[n] is not exactly N-periodic for any N — you get a sequence that never quite closes on itself. Every derivation from here on quietly assumes T₀ = NTs for an integer N. Watch for exam questions that give you a T₀ and Ts whose ratio is not a whole number — that's usually a trick, or a cue to redefine Ts.

Sampling as an Impulse Train

To carry this sampling operation into an integral (which is the language the CTFS speaks), we represent it multiplicatively. Let p(t) be an impulse train — a Dirac comb — with spikes spaced Ts apart:

$$p(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(t-nT_s)$$

Multiplying the continuous x(t) by this comb produces the sampled signal xp(t), an impulse train whose n-th spike is weighted by exactly the sample value x[n]:

$$x_p(t) = x(t)\,p(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x(nT_s)\,\delta(t-nT_s) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n]\,\delta(t-nT_s)$$
WHY THIS TRICK
xp(t) is still a continuous-time signal

That is the whole point. By writing sampling as multiplication-by-a-comb, xp(t) remains something the CTFS machinery can act on — it's continuous-time and still T₀-periodic. We haven't left the world of integrals yet; we've just made the integral secretly discrete, because the Dirac deltas will sift it down to a sum. That collapse is exactly next lecture's… sorry, next section's derivation.

BRAINSTORM
Why an impulse train, and not just "list the samples"?

We could have jumped straight to treating x[n] as a sequence and defined a discrete sum from scratch. Why bother routing through xp(t) at all? Consider: it lets us reuse a formula you already trust (the CTFS analysis integral) without inventing new machinery, and it makes the connection ck = (1/T₀)X[k] — which we'll meet in Section 05 — almost automatic instead of a coincidence.

Part I · Series to DFT

The Integral Becomes a Sum

The impulses in xₖ(t) sift the CTFS integral down to something a computer can actually evaluate: a finite sum over N numbers.

The sampled signal xp(t) is still T₀-periodic, so — being an honest continuous-time periodic signal — it has a CTFS of its own. Call its coefficients ck:

$$c_k = \frac{1}{T_0}\int_{T_0} x_p(t)\,e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt$$
Derivation, step by step

Step 1 — substitute. Replace xp(t) with the impulse-weighted sum from the last section:

$$c_k = \frac{1}{T_0}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T_0}\left(\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n]\,\delta(t-nT_s)\right) e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt$$

Each δ(t − nTs) sifts the integral, picking out the integrand at t = nTs. Choosing the integration window to be exactly one period (t₀ = −Ts/2 to t₀ + T₀) keeps precisely N impulses inside it, for n = 0, 1, …, N−1:

$$c_k = \frac{1}{T_0}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T_0}\delta(t-nT_s)\,e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt = \frac{1}{T_0}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\,e^{-jk\omega_0 nT_s}$$

Since ω₀Ts = (2π/T₀)Ts = 2π/N (using T₀ = NTs), the exponent collapses to a clean discrete-frequency form:

Result
$$c_k = \frac{1}{T_0}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\,e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N}kn}$$

A continuous integral over one period has become a finite sum of N terms. This is the single most important algebraic move in the whole lecture — everything that follows is bookkeeping around this collapse.

EDGE CASE
Why t₀ = −Ts/2?

The choice of integration window matters only in which N impulses fall inside it — the value of ck does not depend on this choice, because xp(t) is periodic and the CTFS integral over any full period gives the same answer. Taking t₀ = −Ts/2 is just a convenient bookkeeping choice that makes the impulses land exactly at n = 0, 1, …, N−1 rather than some other consecutive block of N integers.

EXAM ALERT
The most commonly tested step

Examiners love asking you to reproduce exactly this collapse — substitute the impulse train, apply sifting, simplify ω₀Ts to 2π/N. Practice writing all three lines without looking. The one place students lose marks: forgetting the 1/T₀ scale factor sitting outside the sum, since it came from the CTFS analysis formula and doesn't disappear.

Part I · Series to DFT

Periodicity of cₖ — Reading the Bins

Sampling in time made the spectrum periodic. This single fact reshapes how we index, store, and read every discrete spectrum from here forward.

Only N Distinct Coefficients

Look at what happens if we push the index from k to k + N in the formula we just derived:

$$c_{k+N} = \frac{1}{T_0}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\,e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N}(k+N)n} = c_k \underbrace{e^{-j2\pi n}}_{=\,1} = c_k$$

The extra factor e−j2πn equals 1 for every integer n, so it vanishes and ck+N = ck. The frequency coefficients are N-periodic. Sampling in time made the spectrum periodic in frequency — this is exactly the duality your brainstorm in Section 00 was fishing for.

Since the sequence of coefficients just repeats forever with period N, there are really only N unique numbers to keep track of. By convention we keep the window k = 0, 1, …, N−1.

Shaded bars: the window we conventionally keep, k = 0 … N−1. Everything outside is the same N numbers, repeating.

Reading the Bins: Where the Negative Frequencies Went

The natural, physical spectrum is centered at k = 0: low frequencies sit in the middle, negative k trails off to the left, positive k rises to the right. But once we insist on keeping only the window 0, …, N−1, periodicity folds every negative coefficient into the upper half of that window:

$$c_{-k} = c_{N-k}$$

Nothing is lost — the negative-frequency information is simply relabeled. Bin N−1 is bin −1, bin N−2 is bin −2, and so on.

Interactive · centered view vs. the DFT window
How to Read N Bins — Memorize This
  • Bins 0 → N/2: positive frequencies, climbing low to high.
  • Bins N/2 → N: negative frequencies, folded in, falling from high back toward dc.
  • A real-valued, lowpass-ish signal therefore peaks near k = 0 and near k = N, and dips near k = N/2.
COMMON MISTAKE
Plotting bins 0…N−1 and calling the right half "high frequency noise"

Students who forget the fold often look at a DFT magnitude plot, see two humps, and describe the second hump (near k = N) as some separate high-frequency artifact. It is not — it's the mirror image of the negative frequencies your signal already has, simply relabeled into positive-looking bin indices. For a real signal, |X[k]| is always symmetric about k = N/2.

Part I · Series to DFT

The Orthogonality Trick → the DFS

We have an analysis formula (time → frequency). We still need its inverse. The CTFS synthesis formula won't give it to us — so we build one from scratch.

Can CTFS Synthesis Give Us Back x[n]? No.

It's tempting to just reuse the CTFS synthesis equation:

$$\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} c_k e^{jk\omega_0 t} = x_p(t)$$

But look at what it actually reconstructs: the continuous-time impulse train xp(t), not the sequence x[n] we want — and it spends infinitely many terms endlessly repeating the same N coefficient values. We need a genuinely discrete synthesis formula, built from only the N unique numbers.

THE PATTERN TO FOLLOW
Analysis uses e−j···, synthesis uses e+j···

That sign flip is the one reliable landmark across every Fourier-type pair you'll ever meet: CTFS, CTFT, DFS, DFT, DTFT — all of them. Use it to sanity-check any formula you write from memory under exam pressure.

The Orthogonality Trick

Write the N unique coefficients (clearing the 1/T₀ scaling by defining T₀ck) as:

$$T_0 c_k = \sum_{m=0}^{N-1} x[m]\, e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N}km}, \qquad k = 0,1,\dots,N-1.$$

Multiply both sides by e+j(2π/N)kn and sum over all N unique values of k:

$$\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(T_0 c_k)\, e^{j\frac{2\pi}{N}kn} = \sum_{m=0}^{N-1} x[m] \left(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} e^{j\frac{2\pi}{N}k(n-m)}\right)$$
The key lemma — evaluate the inner sum

This is a finite geometric series in k. For n ≠ m, the sum telescopes to exactly zero (it's a full revolution around the unit circle divided into N equal steps, summing to nothing). For n = m, every term is e0 = 1, and the sum is simply N. In one line:

$$\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} e^{j\frac{2\pi}{N}(n-m)k} = \begin{cases} N & n=m \\ 0 & n\neq m\end{cases}$$

This is orthogonality: the complex exponentials ej(2π/N)kn, for different k, are mutually orthogonal over one period of n. It is the discrete cousin of the same orthogonality you used to derive CTFS coefficients originally.

Because the inner sum is a spike at m = n and zero everywhere else, the outer sum over m collapses to a single surviving term, and we get an exact, finite inverse:

The Discrete Inverse
$$x[n] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} (T_0 c_k)\, e^{j\frac{2\pi}{N}kn}$$

A finite sum recovers the samples exactly — no impulses, no infinite series.

BRAINSTORM
Notice what both directions only ever touch

Analysis and synthesis both operate purely on the N numbers T₀ck — never on ck alone, never on anything from the continuous world. That combination is asking to be named as its own self-contained object. What would you call it?

The Discrete Fourier Series (DFS)

Forget the continuous ancestor entirely. Let ̃x[n] be any N-periodic sequence — it doesn't need to have come from sampling anything. The same finite sums, renamed ak ≡ ck, form a complete, self-contained series representation:

Discrete Fourier Series (DFS) Pair
$$a_k = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \tilde{x}[n]\, e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N}kn} \quad\text{(analysis)}$$
$$\tilde{x}[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} a_k\, e^{j\frac{2\pi}{N}kn} \quad\text{(synthesis)}$$
CTFS (period T₀)DFS (period N)
Analysis$$c_k=\tfrac{1}{T_0}\int_{T_0} x(t)e^{-jk\frac{2\pi}{T_0}t}dt$$$$a_k=\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\tilde x[n]e^{-jk\frac{2\pi}{N}n}$$
Synthesis$$x(t)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}c_k e^{jk\frac{2\pi}{T_0}t}$$$$\tilde x[n]=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}a_k e^{jk\frac{2\pi}{N}n}$$
Coefficientsinfinitely manyN unique (ak+N = ak)

Seeing the DFS: Winding the N Samples

Just like winding a continuous curve gave you the CTFS coefficient as a centroid, winding the N samples gives you the DFS coefficient the same way. Place sample n at radius x[n], angle −(2π/N)kn; their center of mass is exactly ak. It's a plain average — which is precisely why the integral became a sum.

This is the discrete-time analogue of "c₀ is the average value/dc component," and it's a fast sanity check on any DFS/DFT computation — compute the mean of your samples by eye, and it should match |a₀| (or X[0]/N for the DFT).

Part I · Series to DFT

The DFT — Definition & Three Bridges

Drop the 1/N and give the exponential a name: the DFT is the DFS, rescaled for convenience.

The Twiddle Factor

For a length-N sequence, define WN ≡ e−j2π/N, an N-th root of unity: WNN = 1, and powers of WN cycle with period N. Every DFT formula you'll write from here on is most naturally expressed using WN.

DFT / IDFT Pair
$$X[k] \triangleq \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\, W_N^{kn} = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\, e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N}kn}, \qquad k=0,\dots,N-1$$
$$x[n] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} X[k]\, W_N^{-kn} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} X[k]\, e^{j\frac{2\pi}{N}kn}, \qquad n=0,\dots,N-1$$

Notice the only difference from the DFS: the DFT is just the bare analysis sum, with no 1/N scale factor. All the 1/N bookkeeping is pushed entirely onto the inverse. This is purely a convention — but it is the convention every textbook, every version of MATLAB/numpy's fft, and every exam in this course will use, so it's worth internalizing exactly where the 1/N lives.

COMMON MISTAKE
Putting the 1/N in the wrong place

The single most common DFT slip: writing X[k] with a 1/N in front (that's ak, the DFS coefficient, not X[k]) or forgetting the 1/N entirely on the inverse. Anchor yourself with the bridge X[k] = N·ak below whenever you're unsure.

Three Bridges

We now have three closely related objects that came from the same derivation, and it pays to know exactly how they relate:

BridgeRelationMeaning
1$$c_k = \tfrac{1}{T_0}X[k]$$The CTFS coefficients of the sampled continuous signal xp(t) are the DFT values, scaled by 1/T₀.
2$$X[k] = N\,a_k$$The DFT is the DFS, rescaled — drop the 1/N from analysis, and it reappears on synthesis.
3$$X[k] = X(e^{j\omega})\big|_{\omega=\frac{2\pi}{N}k}$$The DFT samples the DTFT at N evenly spaced points — we prove this properly in Part III.

We Never Needed the Continuous Signal

Look at the DFT formula one more time: it consumes only the N numbers x[0], …, x[N−1]. Nothing in it remembers Ts or x(t). The whole sampling story — the impulse train, the sifting, the CTFS ancestor — was scaffolding. Its only job was to show why the Fourier integral becomes a sum once the signal lives on samples.

WHY IT MATTERS
Any length-N list of numbers has a DFT

An audio buffer, a row of pixels, a sensor log — none of these ever had a continuous ancestor x(t), and none of them need one. DFS: a Fourier series whose integral collapsed to a sum. DFT: the DFS, rescaled. That's genuinely all they are — everything else is application.

Part I in One Picture

We started with continuous-time periodic x(t); sampling gave a periodic sequence x[n] of period N; its frequency representation is uniquely captured by exactly N contiguous coefficients; the DFT/IDFT pair converts perfectly, both directions, between those N time points and those N frequency points. Nothing is approximate here — the DFT is an exact, invertible transform of a finite list of numbers.

Part II · The DTFT

Sampling an Aperiodic Signal

Same move as Part I — sample the time axis — but now the ancestor signal is aperiodic, and a different transform falls out.

Part I's whole machinery leaned on periodicity: T₀ = NTs, N impulses per period, a finite sum. What if the signal simply isn't periodic? Consider x(t) restricted to a finite interval of duration T₀ (or, more generally, any aperiodic signal). Uniformly sampling it yields a finite-length (or, in general, infinite but non-repeating) sequence x[n].

Using exactly the same impulse-train representation as before:

$$x_p(t) = x(t)\,p(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n]\,\delta(t-nT_s)$$

— for a finite-duration capture this reduces to a sum over n = 0, …, N−1, but the derivation works for any aperiodic x[n], finite or not.

CTFT of the Samples: The DTFT

An aperiodic signal calls for the Fourier transform, not the series. So take the CTFT of the sampled impulse train:

$$X_p(j\omega) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n]\,e^{-j\omega n T_s}$$

Now compress the frequency axis by the sampling period — apply the relabeling ωTs ↦ ω. Under that single substitution, the same spectrum becomes the Discrete-Time Fourier Transform:

Discrete-Time Fourier Transform (DTFT)
$$X(e^{j\omega}) \triangleq X_p(j\omega)\Big|_{\omega T_s \mapsto \omega} = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n]\, e^{-j\omega n}$$

So the DTFT is nothing but the Fourier transform of the sampled signal, viewed through a compressed axis. The new ω is digital frequency: radians per sample, not radians per second. For a finite length-N capture, the sum is finite:

$$X(e^{j\omega}) = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\, e^{-j\omega n}$$
EDGE CASE — NOTATION
X(ejω), not X(jω)

The DTFT is conventionally written as a function of ejω, not of ω directly — a small but deliberate notational choice that keeps reminding you the spectrum lives on the unit circle in the z-plane and repeats every 2π. Writing X(ω) instead of X(ejω) is a common style slip that graders do dock marks for.

Winding a Continuous Segment, at Any Rate

Take a finite segment of a signal — nothing repeats outside it, so the winding runs over the entire segment, and its centroid times duration is the CTFT. No rate ω is special anymore (unlike the CTFS, where only harmonics of ω₀ produced a closed loop): every ω gets its own value, which is exactly why the CTFT — and its discrete cousin the DTFT — is a continuous function of frequency, in contrast to the line spectrum of a periodic signal.

Part II · The DTFT

2π-Periodicity & the Inverse DTFT

Sampling in time makes the DTFT spectrum periodic in ω — the exact discrete-time echo of what made the DFS periodic in k.

Only N Points to Wind: Digital Frequency

Sample the continuous segment, x[n] = x(nTs). The winding picture places the points x[n]e−jωn on the same curve as before, and with no periodicity to exploit, every sample counts:

$$X(e^{j\omega}) = \sum_n x[n]\,e^{-j\omega n} = N\cdot(\text{centroid of the wound points})$$

Consecutive points are turned by the same angle ω each step — the digital frequency, in radians per sample, equal to ωphysTs. Turning by ω + 2π per sample places the identical set of points (a full extra revolution changes nothing), so X(ejω) must repeat every 2π.

Period of the DTFT
$$X\!\left(e^{j(\omega+2\pi)}\right) = X\!\left(e^{j\omega}\right)$$

The Inverse DTFT

Same synthesis pattern as always: e+jωn. But now the spectrum is a continuous function of ω, so instead of summing over N unique bins we integrate over one 2π period:

$$\hat{x}[n] \triangleq \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{2\pi} X(e^{j\omega})\, e^{j\omega n}\, d\omega$$
Derivation — orthogonality over a continuous period

Substitute the analysis sum and swap the order of summation and integration:

$$\hat{x}[n] = \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} x[m] \cdot \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{2\pi} e^{j\omega(n-m)}\, d\omega$$

For an integer r = n − m, a complete cycle integrates to zero unless r = 0:

$$\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} e^{j\omega r}\, d\omega = \begin{cases}1 & r=0\\0 & r\neq 0\end{cases} \implies \hat{x}[n]=x[n]$$

Exactly the continuous-frequency analogue of the discrete orthogonality trick from Section 04 — a sum over N bins there, an integral over one 2π period here.

DTFT Pair
$$X(e^{j\omega}) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n]\, e^{-j\omega n}$$
$$x[n] = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{2\pi} X(e^{j\omega})\, e^{j\omega n}\, d\omega$$

Notice the shape of x[n] now: it's a continuum superposition of ejωn over one period — versus the DFS, a finite sum of N of them. That distinction — sum vs. integral on synthesis — is the fingerprint that tells you whether you're looking at a periodic or aperiodic discrete signal.

Sum vs. integral is a favorite short-answer question

"Is the DTFT synthesis formula a sum or an integral, and why?" tests whether you actually understand aperiodicity → continuous spectrum, not just whether you memorized the box. Be ready to explain it in one sentence: aperiodic time means the spectrum doesn't repeat, so there's no finite window of unique frequencies to sum over — every ω in one period is genuinely distinct information.

Part III · DFT via DTFT

The DFT = N Samples of the DTFT

Nothing new to derive here — just look at the definitions side by side, and the third bridge falls out for free.

Compare the DFT formula from Section 05 with the DTFT formula from Section 06. They are the same sum, evaluated at different points:

$$X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\, e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N}kn} = X(e^{j\omega})\Big|_{\omega = \frac{2\pi}{N}k}$$

So the N-point DFT is exactly N evenly spaced samples, taken over one full 2π period of the DTFT, at spacing Δω = 2π/N.

One Transform, Two Views

Periodic view: the DFT bins are Fourier-series coefficients of a periodic sequence (Part I).  Aperiodic view: the DFT bins are N samples of the continuous DTFT curve of a finite-length sequence (Part III). Same N numbers, two completely different stories about where they came from — both valid, depending on whether you think of x[n] as one period of something periodic, or as a finite, standalone burst.

EDGE CASE
What if N is odd?

The slide's convention (bin N/2 is the "highest frequency," exactly at ω = π) assumes N is even, so N/2 is an integer bin. For odd N there is no bin sitting exactly at ω = π — the fold point falls between bins (N−1)/2 and (N+1)/2. Watch for this if a problem hands you an odd-length sequence and asks you to "identify the Nyquist bin."

Part III · DFT via DTFT

Zero-Padding: Same Spectrum, Finer Samples

A direct, practical consequence of "the DFT samples the DTFT" — and a genuinely popular exam and lab topic.

For a length-N sequence x[n], zero-padding means artificially appending zeros to reach some total length M ≥ N. What happens to the spectrum?

$$X_M[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{M-1} x[n]\, e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{M}kn} = X(e^{j\omega})\Big|_{\omega=\frac{2\pi}{M}k}$$

The appended zeros contribute nothing to the DTFT sum — multiplying by zero and adding changes nothing — so the underlying continuous DTFT curve X(ejω) is completely unchanged. What changes is only the spacing at which the M-point DFT samples that same curve: 2π/M instead of 2π/N. A bigger M means a finer grid of samples over the identical curve.

MORE PIXELS, NOT MORE INFORMATION
Zero-padding cannot resolve what N real samples never captured

This is the single most tested conceptual point about zero-padding, and the most commonly misunderstood. Zero-padding interpolates a finer picture of the exact same DTFT — it never adds new information about the signal, and it can never separate two closely spaced frequency components that the original N samples genuinely couldn't distinguish. It is a display/interpolation tool, not a resolution-improving tool. True frequency resolution comes only from capturing more real samples (a longer observation window), never from padding.

BRAINSTORM
Then why ever zero-pad at all?

If it adds no information, what is it actually good for? Think about: making an FFT length a power of two for speed, getting a smoother-looking plot for a report or a peak-picking algorithm, or lining up two different-length signals to the same frequency grid before comparing their spectra.

Part III · DFT via DTFT

Back to Physical Frequency: Bins → Ω → Hertz

Undo the normalization from Section 06 and put real, physical units under every bin index.

Recall from Section 06 that the DTFT axis was normalized: ω = ΩTs, where Ω is physical frequency in rad/s and ωs ≡ 2π/Ts is the sampling rate in rad/s. Undo that relabeling for bin k:

$$\omega_k = \frac{2\pi}{N}k \;\longmapsto\; \Omega_k = \frac{\omega_k}{T_s} = k\,\frac{\omega_s}{N}$$

The fold carries over exactly, just in physical units: bins 0 → N/2 span 0 → ωs/2, and bins N/2 → N span −ωs/2 → 0.

WHY IT MATTERS
ω = π means half the sampling rate

ω = π ↔ Ω = ωs/2. One full 2π period of the DTFT corresponds to exactly one full ωs of physical frequency — the window [−ωs/2, ωs/2]. If that ratio (half the sampling rate) sounds familiar, it should: it's the Nyquist frequency, and you'll meet it formally when we reach Sampling.

From Bins to Hertz

Relabel once more, into Hz: with sampling rate fs = 1/Ts, we get f = Ω/2π, so ωs/2 ↦ fs/2, and the bin spacing becomes:

$$\Delta f = \frac{f_s}{N} = \frac{1}{NT_s} = \frac{1}{T_0}$$

Bin k then corresponds to a physical frequency:

$$f_k = \begin{cases} k\dfrac{f_s}{N} & 0 \le k \le N/2 \\[6pt] (k-N)\dfrac{f_s}{N} & N/2 < k \le N-1 \end{cases}$$

This is exactly the ordering that numpy.fft.fftfreq reports, and exactly why every spectrum-analysis tool you'll ever use shows a range from 0 to fs/2 by default.

Interactive · bin → Hz calculator
EDGE CASE
Δf = 1/T₀ ties bin spacing to your observation window, not your sample count alone

Doubling N while holding fs fixed doubles your observation duration T₀ = NTs, which is what actually shrinks Δf. Doubling N by zero-padding (Section 09) instead — without capturing more real samples — changes nothing about the true frequency resolution, even though the bin spacing on your plot gets finer. Keep "more real samples" and "more padded bins" mentally separate; exams love conflating them.

Discrete in One Domain ⇔ Periodic in the Other

Sampling time made the spectrum 2π-periodic (Part II). Sampling frequency — keeping only N bins — made time N-periodic (Part I). This duality, proven twice over today, is the single deepest idea in the whole lecture. Every time you sample one domain, you force periodicity onto the other.

Reading the Bins — One Last Time

Bins 0 → N/2: positive frequencies, low → high. Bins N/2 → N: negative frequencies, high → low, folded in by periodicity. Say this out loud until it's automatic — it is the single fact you'll reach for most often in every lab, assignment, and exam question involving a computed spectrum.

Self-Check Lab

Eight questions spanning both parts of the lecture. Click an option to see immediate feedback.

1. Sampling a continuous periodic signal x(t) in time causes its frequency coefficients ck to…
Correct idea: ck+N = ck — the extra factor e−j2πn equals 1 for integer n, so shifting k by N changes nothing. This is the core duality of Part I: sample time, and frequency becomes periodic.
2. In the DFT/IDFT convention used in this lecture (X[k] = Σ x[n]WNkn), where does the 1/N scale factor live?
The DFT is the bare analysis sum, no 1/N. All the scaling is pushed onto the inverse: x[n] = (1/N)ΣX[k]WN−kn. Compare with the DFS, where the 1/N sits on analysis (ak) instead — and X[k] = N·ak is exactly that rescaling.
3. For a real-valued length-8 signal, DFT bin 6 is the negative-frequency partner of which bin?
The fold rule is c−k = cN−k, so bin N−k pairs with bin k. Here N = 8, so bin 6 = bin (8−2) pairs with bin 2. In general, for real signals, |X[k]| is symmetric about N/2 = 4.
4. Zero-padding a length-N sequence out to length M = 4N…
Appended zeros add nothing to the DTFT sum, so the curve X(ejω) is unchanged. Only the sampling spacing shrinks, from 2π/N to 2π/M — a finer picture of the same information, not new information. True resolution needs a longer real observation window T₀.
5. The DTFT X(ejω) of a discrete sequence is periodic in ω with period…
X(ej(ω+2π)) = X(ejω) always, for any discrete sequence — because e−j2πn = 1 for every integer n. This periodicity is unconditional; it has nothing to do with whether the underlying signal is finite-length or not.
6. The twiddle factor WN is defined as…
WN ≡ e−j2π/N, an N-th root of unity (WNN = 1). It packages the analysis exponential e−j(2π/N)kn as WNkn, and the synthesis exponential as WN−kn.
7. N = 8 samples are captured at fs = 8 kHz. What is the DFT's bin spacing Δf?
Δf = fs/N = 8 kHz / 8 = 1 kHz per bin. Equivalently, Δf = 1/T₀, where T₀ = NTs is the total observation duration.
8. Which statement correctly relates the DFT to the DTFT?
The DFT is literally N evenly spaced samples of the DTFT, taken over one full 2π period at spacing 2π/N. This is Bridge 3 from Section 05/08 — and the entire conceptual basis for zero-padding.
