FIG. 00 — ORIENTATION

Same numbers. Radically fewer operations.

The FFT does not compute anything new. It computes exactly the DFT you already know — the same X[k] values — but it gets there by exploiting a symmetry hiding inside the twiddle factor WN. That symmetry turns an O(N²) brute-force sum into an O(N log N) algorithm, and that difference is the reason spectral analysis, audio compression, and wireless communication are computationally possible at all.

BEFORE YOU START — A QUESTION TO SIT WITH If the FFT computes the exact same numbers as the DFT, where does the speed-up actually come from? FIG. 01 — RECAP OF LAST CLASS

Two views of the DFT

Before speeding anything up, let's make sure the object we're speeding up is solid in your mind. Last class, the DFT was framed from two different angles.

Periodic signals

  • The DFT can be seen as evaluating the Fourier series coefficients of a sampled periodic signal.
  • Equivalently, it gives the N unique values of the Fourier series of a discrete-time periodic signal (discrete-time Fourier series is itself periodic with period N, so only N values are ever independent).

Aperiodic signals

  • The DFT can be seen as samples of the continuous-time Fourier transform of a sampled aperiodic signal.
  • Equivalently, it gives samples of the discrete-time Fourier transform (DTFT) of a finite-length sequence.

And a preview you'll meet properly later in the course: if the original continuous-time signal is bandlimited, the original signal can be fully reconstructed from its DFT samples — sampling doesn't throw information away, provided you sampled fast enough.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR TODAY Every one of these four viewpoints describes the same N complex numbers X[0], X[1], …, X[N−1]. The FFT is not a new transform living alongside the DFT — it is a computational shortcut for producing these same numbers. Keep this distinction razor-sharp: it is a favourite conceptual exam question.
FIG. 02 — THE NEED FOR SPEED

Naive evaluation of the DFT

Let's put a number on how expensive the direct definition actually is.

DEFINITION
X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] W_N^{kn}, \qquad W_N = e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N}}

For each of the N values of k, computing the sum directly costs:

  • N complex multiplications — one per term x[n]WNkn
  • N − 1 complex additions — to sum the N terms

Multiply that by N values of k, and computing the entire spectrum X[0..N−1] costs O(N²) operations.

EDGE CASE — DON'T CONFUSE "PER-k COST" WITH "TOTAL COST" A very common exam slip: writing O(N) as the answer because you only counted the cost for a single k. The question almost always asks for the cost of computing the whole DFT array, which multiplies the per-k cost by N again.

Why this is a real problem

Consider one second of audio sampled at 44.1 kHz — a completely ordinary sample rate. Here N = 44,100, and the naive DFT needs roughly N² ≈ 2 × 10⁵ operations. That would make real-time spectral analysis, and everything built on it, hopelessly slow.

FIG. 03 — A BRIEF HISTORY OF THE FFT

Discovered, forgotten, rediscovered

The algorithm's history is a good reminder that "new" results in mathematics sometimes aren't new at all.

1805

Carl Friedrich Gauss

Developed the radix-2 algorithm to interpolate asteroid orbits — a purely computational trick to speed up trigonometric interpolation. This predates Joseph Fourier's own work on harmonic analysis by over a decade. Gauss left it as an unpublished note, and it sat essentially unnoticed for a century and a half.

1965

James W. Cooley & John W. Tukey

Independently rediscovered the algorithm while working at IBM and Princeton. Their landmark paper launched the modern field of Digital Signal Processing (DSP) and is why the algorithm we study today is named the Cooley–Tukey FFT.

Many other FFT variants have been developed since — split-radix, prime-factor, Bluestein's algorithm, and more — but Cooley–Tukey is the right place to build your intuition, and it's what we study first.

WHY THE HISTORY IS WORTH KNOWING It's a genuinely common (and fair) short-answer exam question: "Who is credited with the FFT, and why is that attribution slightly misleading?" The honest answer references Gauss predating Fourier's own harmonic-analysis work by a decade, with Cooley and Tukey providing the 1965 rediscovery that made it famous.
FIG. 04 — THE COOLEY–TUKEY ALGORITHM

Splitting the DFT in two

The Cooley–Tukey FFT is an elegant O(N log N) algorithm for computing the DFT by exploiting the symmetry and periodicity built into WN.

We'll study the radix-2 version, which:

  • Successively splits an N-point DFT into two N/2-point DFTs, then recurses.
  • Assumes N is a power of 2 (if it isn't, we zero-pad up to the next power of 2 — covered later).

There are two variants, distinguished by which domain gets split by parity:

VARIANT A

Decimation-in-Time (DIT)

Splits the input (time-domain) sequence into even- and odd-indexed samples.

VARIANT B

Decimation-in-Frequency (DIF)

Splits the output (frequency-domain) index into even- and odd-valued k.

The two symmetry facts everything rests on

Both derivations you're about to see lean on exactly two properties of the twiddle factor. Memorize these — they resurface constantly.

PropertyStatementWhere it's used
Halving symmetry W_N^{\,k+N/2} = -W_N^{\,k} Lets us write the second half of the output using the same half-size DFTs as the first half, with only a sign flip — this is what collapses two separate computations into one shared one.
Exponent reduction W_N^{2} = W_{N/2} Converts the exponent structure of the original N-point sum into a genuine N/2-point DFT expression, which is what makes the recursive split legal in the first place.
BRAINSTORM The name "decimation in time" sounds destructive, but nothing is discarded. Why do you think this operation is called "decimation" at all? (Hint: think about what physically happens to the sequence's index set at each stage — it's being thinned out by a factor of 2, not deleted.)
FIG. 05 — DECIMATION-IN-TIME

Deriving the DIT butterfly

Follow the algebra slowly here — every later diagram is just a picture of these five lines.

Step 1 — split the sum by parity of n

X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] W_N^{kn} = \sum_{r=0}^{N/2-1} x[2r] W_N^{2rk} + \sum_{r=0}^{N/2-1} x[2r+1] W_N^{(2r+1)k}

Step 2 — apply the exponent-reduction property

Since W_N^{2rk} = e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N}\cdot 2rk} = e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N/2}\cdot rk} = W_{N/2}^{rk}, the even-indexed sum becomes a genuine N/2-point DFT. Pulling the constant factor WNk out of the odd sum:

X[k] = \underbrace{\sum_{r=0}^{N/2-1} x[2r]\, W_{N/2}^{rk}}_{\text{N/2-point DFT of evens}} \;+\; W_N^{k}\underbrace{\sum_{r=0}^{N/2-1} x[2r+1]\, W_{N/2}^{rk}}_{\text{N/2-point DFT of odds}}

One N-point DFT is now expressed using two N/2-point DFTs. That's the entire trick, in embryo. What remains is bookkeeping: we need a way to stretch these two N/2-length results back out to cover all N output indices.

Step 3 — name them, and notice periodicity

G[k] = \sum_{r=0}^{N/2-1} x[2r]\,W_{N/2}^{rk}, \qquad H[k] = \sum_{r=0}^{N/2-1} x[2r+1]\,W_{N/2}^{rk}

G[k] and H[k] are only directly defined for 0 ≤ k < N/2 — that's the size of an N/2-point DFT. But because any N/2-point DFT is periodic with period N/2:

G[k + N/2] = G[k], \qquad H[k+N/2] = H[k]

So the naive approach would be: copy the first half of G and H into the second half (a periodic extension), then write X[k] = G[k] + W_N^k H[k] for the full range 0 ≤ k < N. This already works — but it recomputes the twiddle multiplication redundantly, and it hides a much cheaper structure. We can do better.

Step 4 — exploit the halving symmetry for k ≥ N/2

For the second half of the spectrum, using W_N^{k+N/2} = W_N^k e^{-j\pi} = -W_N^k:

X[k+N/2] = G[k+N/2] + W_N^{k+N/2}H[k+N/2] = G[k] - W_N^k H[k], \qquad N/2 \le k < N
RESULT — RADIX-2 DECIMATION-IN-TIME FFT

For 0 ≤ k < N/2:

X[k] = G[k] + W_N^{k}H[k] \qquad\qquad X[k+N/2] = G[k] - W_N^{k}H[k]

This is the punchline: once you have the N/2-point DFTs of the even and odd parts, you get all N output values using nothing more than additions, subtractions, and one complex multiplication per output pair (the "twiddle factor" multiply). No redundant recomputation, no wasted work.

EXAM ALERT — THE INSIGHT EXAMINERS WANT TO SEE If asked to derive the DIT formula, the score-bearing moment is not the algebra manipulation — it's recognizing why periodicity of G and H, combined with the sign-flip property of WN, means you never need to separately compute the second half. Say this explicitly in your answer, don't just arrive at the final formula silently.
FIG. 06 — DIT BUTTERFLY STRUCTURES

From formula to flow graph

Let's build the picture up in stages, exactly as the slides do: N = 2 first, then N = 4, then the fully expanded N = 8 network.

N = 2 — the fundamental butterfly

The atomic operation of the entire algorithm is the 2-point DFT, nicknamed the "butterfly" for the shape its crossing lines make. Since W_2^0 = 1:

X[0] = x[0] + x[1]W_2^0 = x[0]+x[1] \qquad\qquad X[1] = x[0] - x[1]W_2^0 = x[0]-x[1]

N = 4 — two butterflies feeding a combine stage

A 4-point DFT is built from two 2-point DFTs. The outputs G[k] and H[k] of stage 1 are combined using twiddle factors W4k:

X[0]=G[0]+W_4^0H[0] \quad X[1]=G[1]+W_4^1H[1] \quad X[2]=G[0]-W_4^0H[0] \quad X[3]=G[1]-W_4^1H[1]

N = 8 — first iteration, viewed as two boxes

Before fully expanding, it's worth seeing the N = 8 case as two 4-point DFT "black boxes" whose outputs G(k) and H(k) get combined exactly the same way as before, just with M = 8 twiddles:

X[k] = G(k) + W_8^{k}H(k), \qquad X(k+4) = G(k) - W_8^{k}H(k), \quad k=0,\dots,3

N = 8 — the full algorithm, fully expanded

Expanding all the 4-point and 2-point DFTs into their own butterflies yields the complete network below. Two things to notice immediately:

  • The inputs arrive in bit-reversed order.
  • The outputs emerge in natural order.
BRAINSTORM Count the butterflies in the N = 8 diagram: 3 stages × 4 butterflies per stage = 12 butterflies, each costing one complex multiply and two complex adds. FIG. 07 — UNDERSTANDING BIT-REVERSED ORDER

Why the inputs land where they do

Bit-reversal isn't a mysterious extra step bolted onto the algorithm — it falls straight out of how the recursive even/odd split reorganizes the index set.

For N = 8, the physical reshuffling happens across three stages:

  1. Stage 1: Even indices (0, 2, 4, 6) grouped before odd indices (1, 3, 5, 7).
  2. Stage 2: Even-even (0, 4), odd-even (2, 6), even-odd (1, 5), odd-odd (3, 7).
  3. Stage 3: Fully separated into single elements: x[0], x[4], x[2], x[6], x[1], x[5], x[3], x[7].

Putting even before odd is the same operation as sorting by the rightmost (least-significant) bit. Putting even-even before odd-even is sorting by the second-rightmost bit, and so on. Do this for every stage, and you've sorted the input sequence by the bit-reversal of each index's binary representation.

Choose N below to generate the index → binary → bit-reversed → new position table live.

EXAM ALERT — THE MOST COMMON MISTAKE HERE Students frequently reverse the decimal digits (turning 12 into 21) instead of reversing the fixed-width binary representation. Bit-reversal always operates on a binary string whose width equals log₂N bits — for N = 8 that's 3 bits, so index 1 (001) reverses to 100 = 4, not to "1" reversed as a decimal digit. Always write out the binary first, with leading zeros to the correct width.

FIG. 08 — RADIX-2 DIT FFT: PSEUDOCODE & COMPLEXITY

From flow graph to algorithm

The diagrams you just built map directly onto this iterative, in-place algorithm.

// Iterative Radix-2 DIT FFT
Bit-reverse array x of length N (N = 2^v)
for s = 1 to log2(N):              // for each stage
    M = 2^s                        // 2, 4, 8, ..., N
    W_M = e^(-j*2*pi/M)
    for l = 0 to N-M step M:       // M-point DFT blocks in this stage
        W = 1                      // running twiddle factor
        for k = 0 to M/2 - 1:      // butterfly operation
            g = x[l+k]                       // G[k]
            h = W * x[l+k+M/2]                // W_M^k * H[k]
            x[l+k]     = g + h                // X[k]
            x[l+k+M/2] = g - h                // X[k+M/2]
            W = W * W_M
Piece of codeWhat it corresponds to
Bit-reversal passPuts the input into the order the fully expanded flow graph expects, so the algorithm can proceed in place — no auxiliary array needed.
Outer loop over sWalks through the log₂N stages of the flow graph, left to right.
Loop over lSteps across the independent M-point DFT blocks that exist side by side within a stage.
Loop over kRuns the M/2 butterflies inside a single block — this is literally the "for 0 ≤ k < N/2" from the derivation.

Complexity analysis

  • Bit-reversal: O(N) — each element is swapped with its bit-reversed partner at most once.
  • Outer loop: runs log₂N times (one pass per stage).
  • Inner loops, combined: at every stage, all N elements participate in exactly N/2 butterflies.
  • Total: log₂N stages × N/2 butterflies per stage × O(1) work per butterfly = O(N log₂N) complex multiplications and additions.
WHY THE RECURRENCE GIVES N LOG N

The same result falls out of a divide-and-conquer recurrence — the identical shape you'll see again in merge sort:

T(N) = 2\,T(N/2) + O(N)

Two half-size subproblems, plus O(N) work to combine them (the butterfly stage). Expanding this recurrence level by level (or invoking the Master Theorem, case 2) gives T(N) = O(N\log_2 N). If an exam asks you to prove the complexity rather than just state it, this recurrence — expanded explicitly — is exactly what's expected.

With N/2 butterflies per stage and log₂N stages, that's \tfrac{N}{2}\log_2 N complex multiplications and N\log_2 N complex additions. Don't round both to the same "N log N" if the question is asking for a precise count.
FIG. 09 — WORKED EXAMPLE

Computing a 4-point DIT FFT by hand

Let's run the whole machine on real numbers: x = [1,\,2,\,3,\,4]. Click each step to reveal it — try computing it yourself first.

Step 1 — split into evens and odds▸

Evens: x[0]=1,\ x[2]=3    Odds: x[1]=2,\ x[3]=4

Step 2 — compute G[k], the 2-point DFT of the evens▸
G[0] = 1+3 = 4 \qquad G[1] = 1-3 = -2
Step 3 — compute H[k], the 2-point DFT of the odds▸
H[0] = 2+4 = 6 \qquad H[1] = 2-4 = -2
Step 4 — combine with twiddle factors W₄⁰ = 1, W₄¹ = −j▸
X[0]=G[0]+W_4^0H[0]=4+1(6)=10 \qquad\qquad X[2]=G[0]-W_4^0H[0]=4-6=-2
X[1]=G[1]+W_4^1H[1]=-2+(-j)(-2)=-2+2j \qquad X[3]=G[1]-W_4^1H[1]=-2-(-j)(-2)=-2-2j
Step 5 — sanity check against the direct DFT sum▸

Direct definition: X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{3} x[n]W_4^{kn} with x = [1,2,3,4].

X[0]=1+2+3+4=10 \qquad X[1]=1-2j-3+4j=-2+2j
X[2]=1-2+3-4=-2 \qquad X[3]=1+2j-3-4j=-2-2j

Matches exactly. This cross-check habit — verifying the fast method against the slow definition on a small case — is exactly what you should do on any exam question that gives you time to spare.

FIG. 10 — DECIMATION-IN-FREQUENCY

Deriving the DIF butterfly

DIF takes the mirror-image approach: instead of splitting which input samples we use, it splits the sum by time range first, then separates by the parity of the output index k.

Step 1 — split the sum into first-half and second-half time indices

X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N/2-1}x[n]W_N^{kn} + \sum_{n=0}^{N/2-1}x[n+N/2]W_N^{k(n+N/2)}

Using W_N^{kN/2} = e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N}k\frac{N}{2}} = e^{-j\pi k} = (-1)^k:

X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N/2-1}\big(x[n] + (-1)^k x[n+N/2]\big)W_N^{kn}

Now, rather than splitting the input, we decimate the frequency index k by evaluating even and odd k separately — this is the source of the name "decimation in frequency."

Step 2 — even k = 2m

Since (-1)^{2m}=1 and W_N^{2mn}=W_{N/2}^{mn}:

X[2m] = \sum_{n=0}^{N/2-1}\underbrace{\big(x[n]+x[n+N/2]\big)}_{a(n)}W_{N/2}^{mn} = \mathrm{DFT}_{N/2}\{a(n)\}

Step 3 — odd k = 2m+1

Since (-1)^{2m+1}=-1 and W_N^{(2m+1)n}=W_N^n W_N^{2mn}=W_N^n W_{N/2}^{mn}:

X[2m+1] = \sum_{n=0}^{N/2-1}\underbrace{\big(x[n]-x[n+N/2]\big)W_N^n}_{b(n)}W_{N/2}^{mn} = \mathrm{DFT}_{N/2}\{b(n)\}
THE KEY STRUCTURAL DIFFERENCE FROM DIT Look closely at where the twiddle factor WNn sits in b(n): it's applied after the subtraction, not before. In DIT, the twiddle multiplies H[k] before it's added or subtracted from G[k]. In DIF, the sum and difference happen first, and only the difference branch then gets twiddled before being handed to the next-stage N/2-point DFT. This ordering flip is the single most exam-tested distinction between the two variants.

So the DIT-style even/odd input split has become a DIF-style even/odd output split — both variants land on the same underlying "two N/2-point DFTs" structure, just approached from opposite directions.

FIG. 13 — WHY ZERO-PAD? THE PRACTICAL NECESSITY

Zero-padding

THE RADIX-2 REQUIREMENT

Radix-2 FFT algorithms require the sequence length N to be exactly a power of 2: N = 2^m.

The problem

Real-world data almost never arrives with a sample count that happens to be a power of 2. Call that length M. If we insist on using exactly those M samples, we're stuck falling back to the slow O(M²) naive DFT — the entire point of this lecture evaporates.

The solution

Append zeros until the sequence reaches the next power of 2: N = 2^{\lceil \log_2 M \rceil}.

  • Bonus: zero-padding interpolates the spectrum — it gives a smoother-looking, more finely sampled visualization without altering the true underlying frequency content X(e^{j\omega}). This works because the DTFT of a finite-length sequence is completely unchanged by appending zeros (the sum over nonzero terms is identical); you're simply sampling that same, unchanged continuous spectrum at a denser set of points.
  • Caveat: if the signal being processed is genuinely periodic, zero-padding changes the assumed period and shape of that periodic signal — you're effectively inserting artificial silence into what the DFT will treat as one period of an infinitely repeating pattern, distorting the analysis.
EXAM ALERT — THE #1 CONCEPTUAL TRAP Zero-padding does not improve the true frequency resolution of your data. Resolution is set by how long you actually observed the signal (i.e., by M, the real sample count), not by how many zeros you tack on afterward. Zero-padding only makes the existing spectral shape look smoother by sampling it more densely — it cannot separate two closely-spaced frequencies that weren't already distinguishable in the original M-sample observation window.
CLIFFHANGER — CAN WE DO FFT WITHOUT ZERO-PADDING? The slides end on exactly this open question. Beyond what's covered here, a few real answers worth knowing for enrichment (and for connecting this course to what you'll see in practice):
  • Mixed-radix FFT — generalizes Cooley–Tukey to any N that factors into small primes (not just powers of 2), splitting into N₁ × N₂ sub-DFTs instead of always halving.
  • Rader's algorithm — handles prime-length N by converting the DFT into a cyclic convolution of length N−1.
  • Bluestein's algorithm (chirp-z transform) — handles any N whatsoever, including primes, by rewriting the DFT as a convolution, which can then itself be computed fast using zero-padded FFTs internally. This is the general-purpose trick most production FFT libraries fall back on.
