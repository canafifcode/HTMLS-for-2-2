Section 00 · Motivation

Why leave Fourier Series behind?

"Aperiodic functions crying in the background..."

The one big condition Fourier Series needed

Everything you built in Lectures 5–6 rested on one assumption: the signal repeats forever.

Real signals rarely cooperate. A single handclap, a lightning strike's EM pulse, a heartbeat's one QRS complex, a car horn — these are one-time events. They have a beginning, they have an end, and they never come back. Fourier Series, strictly speaking, has nothing to say about them.

The question this lecture answers Can we still describe a non-repeating signal using frequencies — the way Fourier Series described repeating ones?

The professor's intuition

Think of Fourier Series as a special case, not a separate universe. A periodic signal is just a signal that has been "photocopied" infinitely many times with spacing T. What if we started peeling those copies apart — pushing them further and further away from each other — until only one original remains, alone, with its copies banished to infinity?

That is exactly the trick this lecture performs. We do not derive a brand-new theory from scratch; we stretch Fourier Series until it breaks open into something more general. This is why the Fourier Transform (FT) and Fourier Series (FS) share so much notation — they are relatives, not strangers.

Brainstorm before you scroll on If a periodic signal has a discrete set of frequency components (the harmonics kω₀), and an aperiodic signal is "a periodic signal with infinite period," what do you predict happens to the *spacing* between those frequency components as the period grows? What do you predict happens to the *number* of frequencies present?
Section 01 · Derivation

The Limiting Trick: T → ∞

Step 1 — Cheat first: manufacture a periodic twin

Start with one finite-duration, one-shot signal x(t). Instead of analyzing it directly, build a periodic "clone" by repeating it every T seconds:

Now x̃T(t) is periodic, so every Fourier Series tool from Lecture 5 applies to it.

Step 2 — Stretch the period

Now imagine sliding the copies apart — increasing T. For small T the copies crowd together; for large T they drift apart. As T → ∞, every neighboring copy is pushed infinitely far away, and within any finite window, x̃T(t) looks exactly like the single isolated pulse x(t).

Step 3 — What happens in frequency space?

Recall the fundamental frequency and spacing between harmonics:

Period TSpacing ΔωFrequency-domain picture
Small TLarge ΔωFew, widely-spaced frequency lines
Large TSmall ΔωMany, tightly-packed frequency lines

So increasing T in time squeezes the harmonics closer together in frequency. This is the crucial trade-off: time stretches, frequency compresses.

Picture this There is a smooth, continuous curve X(jω) hovering in the background. The "stems" of the Fourier Series (the values ak) are just needle-thin samples poking up to touch that curve at the points ω = kω₀. As T grows, more and more samples appear, tracing the curve more finely.

Step 5 — Why do the raw coefficients ak shrink to zero?

This confuses almost everyone the first time. If ak = X(jkω₀)/T, then as T → ∞, every individual ak → 0. Does that mean the signal vanishes?

No — it means each individual harmonic carries a vanishingly small amount of energy because there are infinitely many of them, packed infinitely close together. What matters is not the height of one stem, but the density of stems per unit frequency. This is exactly like probability density: P(X = x) = 0 for a continuous random variable, yet the density f(x) is very much alive.

As Δω → 0, the "bins" become infinitesimally thin, and the discrete sum of stems smoothly becomes a continuous spectrum.

  • ω₀ = Δω = 2π/T → 0 — the spacing vanishes

Section 02 · The Result

The Fourier Transform Pair

Analysis and Synthesis, side by side

Analysis (time → frequency)

Take the signal, multiply by a rotating exponential, integrate over all time. Produces the spectrum.

Synthesis (frequency → time)

Take the spectrum, multiply by a rotating exponential, integrate over all frequency (with a 1/2π). Rebuilds the signal.

The one-line summary of the whole lecture The Fourier Transform is not a new idea — it is the Fourier Series after the discrete frequency lines have been allowed to merge into a continuum.

Fourier Series vs. Fourier Transform — a side-by-side map

Fourier SeriesFourier Transform
Applies toPeriodic signalsAny signal (periodic or not, with mild conditions)
Spectrum typeDiscrete (harmonics kω₀)Continuous (all ω)
Coefficientsak — numbersX(jω) — a function
SynthesisSum ΣIntegral ∫
"Weight" of each frequencyDiscrete amplitudeDensity (amplitude per unit ω)

Edge cases and existence questions

  • Does X(jω) always exist? Not automatically. Just like an infinite series needs a convergence condition, the FT integral needs the signal to be "well-behaved" — commonly, absolute integrability ∫|x(t)|dt < ∞ is sufficient (one of the Dirichlet-type conditions, echoing the convergence conditions you saw for Fourier Series). Some idealized signals (like a pure constant, or cos(ω₀t) itself) don't satisfy this strictly, yet still have a well-defined transform once impulses are allowed — this is why δ(ω) shows up so often in FT tables.
  • Is X(jω) generally complex? Yes — same rule as Fourier Series: it carries both a magnitude (how much of that frequency is present) and a phase (how that frequency component is time-aligned).
  • What if x(t) is already periodic? You can still take its Fourier Transform — you just need impulses in frequency, spaced at kω₀, since a periodic signal's "spectrum" is fundamentally discrete. This is a preview for later lectures, but keep it in your back pocket.
Exam alert A very common trap: writing the synthesis integral without the 1/2π factor, or writing the analysis integral with one. Memorize the asymmetry: the 1/2π belongs to the equation that integrates over ω (synthesis), not the one that integrates over t (analysis).
Section 03 · Property

Frequency Shifting (Modulation)

Injecting oscillation into a signal

Take an envelope x(t) and multiply it by a carrier cos(ω₀t). The shape is preserved, but oscillation is injected inside it — this is the core idea behind AM radio, and behind physical phenomena like damped pendulums, vibrating strings with loss, ringing circuits, and fading echoes (all of which look like "envelope × oscillation").

Before tackling the cosine directly, solve the cleaner building block: what does multiplying by a single complex exponential ejω₀t do?

The inverse relationship: wide in time ⟺ narrow in frequency

This is one of the most important qualitative facts in all of signal processing — sometimes called an uncertainty principle for signals:

Time domainFrequency domain
Wide rectangle (large T)Narrow, tall main lobe
Narrow rectangle (small T)Wide, short main lobe

You cannot have a signal that is simultaneously very short in time and very narrow in frequency. Squeeze one domain, the other domain spreads out. (Physically: a very brief pulse must contain a broad mixture of frequencies to build its sharp edges — think of a camera shutter click versus a sustained musical note.)

Interactive · Rectangle ↔ Sinc duality

Sanity check at ω = 0 The formula X(jω) = 2sin(ωT/2)/ω looks like it blows up (0/0) at ω = 0. It doesn't — L'Hôpital (or the small-angle approximation sin(x) ≈ x) gives X(j0) = T, exactly the area under the rectangle. This matches a general rule: X(j0) = ∫x(t)dt, the DC value of the spectrum is always the total area of the signal.
Section 05 · Practice

Worked Examples

Example 1 — Two centered rectangles (plug-and-play)

Given: x₁(t) = 1 for −1 < t < 1, else 0. x₂(t) = 1 for −3 < t < 3, else 0.

Method: Both are centered rectangles — just identify the width T and plug into X(jω) = 2sin(ωT/2)/ω.

Width of x₁ is 2 (spans −1 to 1); width of x₂ is 6 (spans −3 to 3). Notice x₂ is wider in time and its sinc is narrower in frequency — the duality from Section 04 in action.

Example 2 — A shifted rectangle

Given: x(t) = 1 for 4 < t < 10, else 0.

Method: First re-describe it as a centered rectangle, then apply time-shifting. The interval [4,10] has width 6 and midpoint 7 — so this is a width-6 rectangle shifted right by t₀ = 7.

Why this matters This two-step pattern — "de-shift to find the base shape, transform the base shape, then re-apply the shift as a phase factor e−jωt₀" — is the standard strategy for almost every asymmetric rectangle problem you'll see on the exam.

Example 3 — The "Lego block" signal (superposition)

Given: a stepped signal — height 1 on (1,2), height 1.5 on (2,3), height 1 on (3,4), zero elsewhere.

Method: Decompose into a sum of simple rectangles using linearity. Here, the block is a width-3 rectangle (height 1, spanning 1 to 4, centered at 2.5) plus a smaller width-1 rectangle (height 0.5, spanning 2 to 3, centered at 2.5) sitting on top.

Locking a cosine inside a rectangle

This single line quietly combines every idea from this lecture:

  • r(t) alone transforms to a sinc, R(jω) = 2sin(ωT/2)/ω (Section 04)
  • Multiplying by cos(ω₀t) shifts that sinc to ±ω₀ and halves its height (Section 03)

Two sinc-shaped lobes, each half the height of the original, sitting at ω = +ω₀ and ω = −ω₀ instead of at the origin.

Why this specific signal matters so much A "windowed cosine" is literally what happens every time you record a short burst of a musical note, or when a radar/sonar system sends out a finite pulse of a carrier frequency. It is also exactly the building block behind the Short-Time Fourier Transform / spectrograms you may meet later. Recognizing "rectangle × cosine" instantly as "two shifted sincs" will save serious time on exams.

Section 07 · The Extreme Case

The Impulse δ(t) and Its Transform

The narrowest possible signal

The Dirac delta δ(t) is the ultimate limit of "narrow in time": zero width, infinite height, unit area. Push the rectangle-sinc duality from Section 04 to its extreme — shrink T toward 0 while keeping area fixed at 1 — and you get exactly this object.

Exam alert Students often try to compute ∫δ(t)e−jωtdt by treating δ(t) as an ordinary function and attempting u-substitution or integration by parts. Don't. The impulse is defined operationally by the sifting property — always jump straight to "plug in t = t₀ into whatever it's multiplying," never try to "integrate the delta" mechanically.
Section 08 · Why This Matters

Applications — Where the Fourier Transform Quietly Appears

A tour of real systems built on X(jω)

ApplicationWhat the FT does
Audio denoisingSeparates voice-like frequency content from noise-like content, so unwanted frequencies can be attenuated.
Image denoisingTreats an image as a 2D signal; noise often concentrates in high spatial frequencies, so filtering the frequency view cleans the picture.
JPEG compressionTransforms image blocks into frequency coefficients; small, perceptually unimportant high-frequency coefficients are rounded away to shrink file size.
MRIThe scanner physically measures frequency-domain data first ("k-space") and reconstructs the anatomical image via an inverse transform.
Music recognitionIdentifies songs from a short clip by matching characteristic peaks ("fingerprints") in the frequency domain over time.
Vibration diagnosisA healthy machine and a faulty one (cracked bearing, loose part) often look similar in time but strikingly different in frequency.
ECG filteringSeparates the heartbeat signal from power-line interference (a narrow, known frequency) so it can be surgically removed without harming the heartbeat.
The unifying theme In every single case above, the raw time-domain signal hides information that becomes obvious once you look at it in frequency. This is the entire professional value of the Fourier Transform: it's a change of perspective, not just a mathematical exercise.

Section 09 · Before the Exam

Exam Alerts & Common Pitfalls — Consolidated

The full checklist

1. The 1/2π asymmetry Synthesis (frequency → time) has the 1/2π factor. Analysis (time → frequency) does not. Mixing these up is the single most common point-loss on FT problems.
2. Modulation halves amplitude, doubles copies x(t)cos(ω₀t) gives two shifted copies, each scaled by 1/2 — not one full-height copy at ω₀.
3. Width, not the raw interval bounds, drives the sinc For a centered rectangle, only the width T enters 2sin(ωT/2)/ω. For an off-center rectangle, first find width and center, then attach the phase factor e−jωt₀ separately.
4. Never "integrate" a delta function mechanically Always use the sifting property directly: ∫δ(t−t₀)f(t)dt = f(t₀).
5. Check X(j0) as a sanity test X(j0) = ∫x(t)dt always. For a rectangle, that's just its area — a fast way to check your formula didn't pick up a stray sign or factor.
6. Linearity is your best friend for composite shapes Any "Lego block" / staircase / trapezoidal signal should immediately trigger: "decompose into simple rectangles and add their transforms."
7. Existence is not automatic The FT integral needs the signal to be well-behaved (e.g. absolutely integrable). Signals like pure cosines or constants technically need impulses in their spectrum to be represented — don't panic if you see a δ(ω) show up in a "textbook" transform table.
