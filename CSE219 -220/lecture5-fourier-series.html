§ 00 — Motivation

The Big Idea

Every periodic signal, however jagged or strange, is secretly a sum of plain sine and cosine waves. That single fact is the entire engine of this course from here on.

Can we break a signal into simple waves?

Yes — always. Take any periodic signal x(t) with period T. No matter how complicated it looks, it can be rebuilt exactly as a sum of a constant plus sines and cosines at multiples of one base frequency:

A "complicated" periodic signal (top) is literally the sum of three simple sinusoids at increasing frequency (below).

That's it. That is Fourier's claim, and it is one of the most consequential ideas in all of engineering: complicated periodic signal = sum of simple periodic waves.

Why should you care?

Three reasons, straight from the lecture:

  1. Simple waves are easy to analyze. A single sine-shaped vibration, or a single sine-shaped temperature pattern, has an easy, well-understood answer.
  2. Real shapes are rarely simple. A guitar string plucked into a triangular kink, or a metal rod with a random initial heat pattern — these are not nice sinusoids to begin with.
  3. Fourier series is the bridge. Break the messy real shape into simple waves, solve the easy problem for each wave separately, then add the individual answers back together.
Example — Plucked String

A string released from rest in some initial shape f(x). We want to predict its full future shape u(x,t) — position at every point, at every time.

Example — Heat Diffusion

A rod starts with an arbitrary hot/cold pattern f(x). A single sinusoidal heat pattern is trivial to predict as it decays; a random pattern becomes manageable once split into sinusoids.

Beyond the slides — the general strategy

This "solve-the-easy-case, then add" trick reappears constantly in engineering and physics: (1) break the input into simple pieces, (2) let each piece evolve on its own — this only works because the underlying system is linear, (3) add the individually-evolved pieces back together (superposition). You already met the linear half of this idea in Lecture 3 with LTI systems and convolution — Fourier series is what makes that machinery sing for periodic signals.

The repeated strategy

1  Break the initial shape into simple waves  →  f(x) = wave₁ + wave₂ + wave₃ + ⋯
2  Let each simple wave evolve completely on its own
3  Add the evolved waves back together

The complicated future behavior comes entirely from adding many simple future behaviors. Keep this picture in your head — it is the intuition behind every formula in this lecture.

§ 01 — Building Blocks

Harmonics & Frequency

Before we can add up "simple waves," we need to know exactly which frequencies are allowed. The answer comes from a surprisingly playful bell analogy.

Bell, bell, bell — who's there?

Three bells: A rings every 2 minutes, B every 3 minutes, C every 5 minutes. When does the whole pattern first repeat?

Bell A · 2 min
Bell B · 3 min
Bell C · 5 min
Gold ticks mark realignment — the combined pattern repeats after 30 minutes.
$$T_{combined} = \text{lcm}(2,3,5) = 30 \text{ minutes}$$
The combined period is the least common multiple of the individual periods.

The same story, viewed through frequency

Flip periods into frequencies (rings per hour): 30, 20, and 12 times/hour. The combined pattern's fundamental frequency is f₀ = 2 times/hour, and — this is the key observation — every individual frequency is a whole-number multiple of f₀:

$$30 = 15f_0, \qquad 20 = 10f_0, \qquad 12 = 6f_0$$

From bells to signals: the fundamental frequency

For any periodic signal x(t) with period T, the same logic applies. Define the fundamental frequency:

$$\omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{T}$$
radians per second — this is the "one ring of the smallest bell" for your signal.

Therefore, the allowed building blocks are kω₀

Just like the bell frequencies had to be whole multiples of f₀, every frequency component of a period-T signal must be a whole-number multiple of ω₀. This includes negative multiples too — they aren't "unphysical," they simply rotate the opposite way around the complex plane (more on this in the next section).

Allowed harmonics: …, −2ω₀, −ω₀, 0, ω₀, 2ω₀, … — negative harmonics rotate backward, positive harmonics rotate forward, k = 0 is the constant (DC) part.
$$\text{frequencies used: } k\omega_0, \quad k \in \mathbb{Z} = \{\dots,-2,-1,0,1,2,\dots\}$$
⚠ Exam Trap

Students often think only positive frequencies "exist" and treat negative k as a bookkeeping trick to throw away. Don't. Both +kω₀ and −kω₀ carry real information — together they combine into a real-valued sinusoid. Dropping the negative side silently loses half your signal.

§ 02 — Notation

Complex Exponentials

Instead of juggling separate sine and cosine terms, we package both into one compact object: the complex exponential.

Euler's formula packages sine and cosine together

$$e^{j\omega_0 t} = \cos(\omega_0 t) + j\sin(\omega_0 t)$$
One rotating object carries both the cosine (real part) and the sine (imaginary part).

Geometrically, e^{jω₀t} is a unit vector spinning counter-clockwise around the origin of the complex plane at angular speed ω₀. Its horizontal shadow traces out cos(ω₀t); its vertical shadow traces out sin(ω₀t).

So the Fourier series will look like this

$$x(t) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} a_k\, e^{jk\omega_0 t}$$
e^{jkω₀t} is the k-th simple wave (spinning at speed kω₀); a_k says how much of that wave is present.

This is called the complex exponential Fourier series — the synthesis equation. It rebuilds x(t) once you know every coefficient a_k.

a_k = 0 means that frequency is absent; a_k ≠ 0 means it's present, with strength |a_k| and phase ∠a_k.

Worked micro-example — a plain cosine already contains two complex waves

Using Euler's formula in reverse:

$$\cos(\omega_0 t) = \frac{1}{2}e^{j\omega_0 t} + \frac{1}{2}e^{-j\omega_0 t}$$
Only the +ω₀ and −ω₀ components are present, each with strength ½.

This is the pattern to internalize: any real sinusoid splits into a symmetric pair of complex exponentials, one spinning forward and one spinning backward, each carrying half the amplitude. You'll use this constantly.

✓ Shortcut

Memorize both Euler pairings — you will use them in nearly every problem this course:

$$\cos\theta = \frac{e^{j\theta}+e^{-j\theta}}{2} \qquad\qquad \sin\theta = \frac{e^{j\theta}-e^{-j\theta}}{2j}$$

§ 03 — Derivation

Finding the Coefficients

We know the synthesis formula. Now: given x(t), how do we actually extract each a_k? This is the single most exam-tested derivation in the chapter.

A small detour — averaging a rotating exponential

Here is the one trick the whole derivation rests on. Average e^{jkω₀t} over one full period:

$$\frac{1}{T}\int_T e^{jk\omega_0 t}\,dt = \begin{cases}1, & k=0 \\ 0, & k\neq 0\end{cases}$$
watch the average of each phasor as it spins:
k = 0 — does not rotate.
average remains 1.
k ≠ 0 — rotates uniformly.
average cancels to 0.

Why does it cancel? A phasor spinning at constant speed sweeps through every angle equally often over a full period, and the contributions on opposite sides of the circle exactly cancel. Only the non-rotating (k = 0) term survives averaging.

Step 1 — finding a₀ is easy: just average x(t)

Write out the synthesis sum. Every term with k ≠ 0 is a rotating phasor scaled by a_k, so it averages to zero. Only the a₀ term survives:

$$a_0 = \frac{1}{T}\int_T x(t)\,dt$$
a₀ is nothing more than the average (DC) value of x(t) over one period.

Step 2 — finding a₁: make the a₁ term stop rotating

Multiply the whole equation by e^{-jω₀t}. Every term's rotation speed shifts down by ω₀ — in particular, the a₁ term becomes non-rotating, while every other term still rotates (just at a different speed):

$$x(t)e^{-j\omega_0 t} = \cdots + a_{-1}e^{-j2\omega_0 t} + a_0 e^{-j\omega_0 t} + a_1 + a_2 e^{j\omega_0 t} + \cdots$$

Average both sides over one period — everything rotating vanishes, leaving only a₁:

$$a_1 = \frac{1}{T}\int_T x(t)e^{-j\omega_0 t}\,dt$$

The general pattern — shift a_k to zero frequency, then average

before — a_k sits at kω₀
→
after — multiply by e^{−jkω₀t}, a_k now sits at 0

The full derivation, done rigorously

Start from the synthesis equation with a dummy index m, multiply by e^{-jkω₀t}, and integrate over one period:

$$\frac{1}{T}\int_T x(t)e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt = \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} a_m \left[\frac{1}{T}\int_T e^{j(m-k)\omega_0 t}\,dt\right]$$

By the orthogonality result above, the bracketed term is 1 only when m = k, and 0 for every other m. The entire infinite sum collapses to a single surviving term:

$$\boxed{a_k = \frac{1}{T}\int_T x(t)e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt}$$
The analysis equation. The integral can be taken over any single full period, ω₀ = 2π/T, k = 0, ±1, ±2, …
⚠ Exam Trap

The sign in the exponent flips between the two equations: synthesis uses +jkω₀t, analysis uses −jkω₀t. Mixing these up is the single most common algebra slip on this topic — it silently conjugates every coefficient you compute.

§ 04 — Worked Problem

Read by Inspection

A trig polynomial like this one never needs the integral formula. If x(t) is already a finite sum of sinusoids, its Fourier coefficients can be read straight off.

Problem

$$x(t) = 1 + \sin(\omega_0 t) + 2\cos(\omega_0 t) + \cos\!\left(2\omega_0 t + \frac{\pi}{4}\right), \qquad T_0 = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_0}$$

Do we really need to compute a_k = (1/T₀) ∫ x(t) e^{-jkω₀t} dt for every k? No. Every term is already a complex-exponential pair in disguise — we just have to unpack each one with Euler's formula.

Step 1 — expand the ω₀ terms

$$\sin(\omega_0 t) + 2\cos(\omega_0 t) = \frac{1}{2j}\left(e^{j\omega_0 t}-e^{-j\omega_0 t}\right) + \left(e^{j\omega_0 t}+e^{-j\omega_0 t}\right) = \left(1+\tfrac{1}{2j}\right)e^{j\omega_0 t} + \left(1-\tfrac{1}{2j}\right)e^{-j\omega_0 t}$$
Since 1/(2j) = −j/2, this simplifies to (1 − j/2)e^{jω₀t} + (1 + j/2)e^{−jω₀t}.

Step 2 — expand the phase-shifted 2ω₀ term

$$\cos\!\left(2\omega_0 t+\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right) = \tfrac{1}{2}e^{j(2\omega_0 t+\pi/4)} + \tfrac{1}{2}e^{-j(2\omega_0 t+\pi/4)} = \left(\tfrac{1}{2}e^{j\pi/4}\right)e^{j2\omega_0 t} + \left(\tfrac{1}{2}e^{-j\pi/4}\right)e^{-j2\omega_0 t}$$
ℹ Don't forget the constant

The lone "1" in x(t) is itself a Fourier term: 1 = 1·e^{j0·ω₀t}, so it contributes directly to a₀ = 1.

Step 3 — collect terms spinning at the same frequency

Group every coefficient by which e^{jkω₀t} it multiplies:

§ 05 — Worked Problem

The Square Wave

The single most-tested Fourier series example in the entire course. Master this derivation cold.

Problem

$$x(t)=\begin{cases}1,&|t|

Step 1 — pick a convenient period, and note where the signal is zero

Choose the interval −T/2 < t < T/2. Outside −T₁ < t < T₁ the signal is exactly zero, so the integral collapses to a short window:

$$a_k = \frac{1}{T}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2} x(t)e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt = \frac{1}{T}\int_{-T_1}^{T_1} e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt$$

Step 2 — evaluate for k ≠ 0

$$a_k = \frac{1}{T}\left[\frac{e^{-jk\omega_0 t}}{-jk\omega_0}\right]_{-T_1}^{T_1} = \frac{2\sin(k\omega_0 T_1)}{Tk\omega_0} = \frac{\sin(k\omega_0 T_1)}{k\pi}$$

Step 3 — handle k = 0 separately (it's just the average)

$$a_0 = \frac{1}{T}\int_{-T_1}^{T_1}1\,dt = \frac{2T_1}{T} = \frac{\text{time spent at height 1}}{\text{total period}}$$
⚠ Exam Trap

The formula sin(kω₀T₁)/(kπ) has a 0/0 at k = 0 — it is only valid for k ≠ 0. Always state a₀ separately as the average value; don't try to plug k = 0 into the sinc formula.

Interactive: the specific case T₁ = T/4

With T₁ = T/4: a₀ = 1/2 and a_k = sin(kπ/2)/(kπ) for k ≠ 0. Notice every even k gives sin(kπ/2) = 0 — the even harmonics vanish entirely for this particular pulse width, leaving only odd k with alternating sign.

a_k for the square wave, T₁ = T/4, k = −7 … 7. The envelope decays like 1/k — a fingerprint of the jump discontinuity (see §08–§09).

§ 06 — Worked Problem

The Triangular Ramp

A harder integral — piecewise, and this time a_k comes out genuinely complex. Excellent practice for exam-style algebra.

Problem

$$x(t)=\begin{cases}t, & 0\le t<1\\ \dfrac{3-t}{2}, & 1\le t<3\end{cases}\qquad x(t+3)=x(t)\quad(T=3)$$

Step 1 — find a₀: the average height over a period

$$a_0=\frac{1}{3}\left[\int_0^1 t\,dt+\int_1^3\frac{3-t}{2}\,dt\right]=\frac{1}{3}\left[\frac12+1\right]=\frac12$$
✓ Shortcut — do it geometrically

The signal traces a triangle of base 3 and height 1 over one period. Area = ½·3·1 = 3/2. Then a₀ = area ÷ period = (3/2)/3 = 1/2 — same answer, zero integration.

Step 2 — set up the coefficient integral for k ≠ 0

$$a_k=\frac13\left[\int_0^1 t\,e^{-jk\omega_0 t}dt+\int_1^3\frac{3-t}{2}\,e^{-jk\omega_0 t}dt\right],\qquad \omega_0=\frac{2\pi}{3}$$

Step 3 — evaluate each piece (integration by parts)

$$\int_0^1 t\,e^{-jk\omega_0 t}dt = -\frac{e^{-jk\omega_0}}{jk\omega_0}+\frac{1-e^{-jk\omega_0}}{(jk\omega_0)^2}$$ $$\int_1^3\frac{3-t}{2}\,e^{-jk\omega_0 t}dt = \frac{e^{-jk\omega_0}}{jk\omega_0}+\frac{e^{-jk\omega_0}-e^{-j3k\omega_0}}{2(jk\omega_0)^2}$$
Since ω₀ = 2π/3, note e^{−j3kω₀} = e^{−j2πk} = 1 — this kills the last term's second piece.

Step 4 — the ± 1/(jkω₀) terms cancel, leaving

$$a_k=\frac13\cdot\frac{1-e^{-jk\omega_0}}{2(jk\omega_0)^2}=\frac{e^{-jk\omega_0}-1}{6k^2\omega_0^2}\qquad\left[(jk\omega_0)^2=-k^2\omega_0^2\right]$$
$$\boxed{a_0=\frac12,\qquad a_k=\frac{e^{-jk\omega_0}-1}{6k^2\omega_0^2},\ k\neq0,\ \ \omega_0=\frac{2\pi}{3}}$$
ℹ Beyond the slides — worth noticing

Unlike the square wave, these a_k are genuinely complex (not purely real) — because this triangular ramp is neither an even nor an odd function of t. Also notice the envelope decays like 1/k² here, versus 1/k for the square wave. That difference is not an accident — see §08 and §09 for why.

§ 07 — Bonus Intuition

The Vector-Space Analogy

A beautiful (optional, but genuinely clarifying) reframing: Fourier coefficients are just coordinates in a very unusual coordinate system.

A coordinate system turns a point into numbers

The point (3, 2) means: 3 steps along the x-axis, 2 steps along the y-axis. The "coordinates" are just: how much of each basis direction do we need?

Functions can be given coordinates too

Take all functions of the form f(t) = a + bt. Choose 1 and t as "basis functions" — then any such f(t) has coordinates (a, b):

$$f(t) = a\cdot 1 + b\cdot t \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad (a,b) \qquad\qquad 3+2t \ \Longleftrightarrow\ (3,2)$$

More axes describe more complicated functions

$$f(t)=a_0+a_1t+a_2t^2+a_3t^3+\cdots \ \Longleftrightarrow\ \text{axes } 1,t,t^2,t^3,\dots\ \Longleftrightarrow\ (a_0,a_1,a_2,\dots)$$

Even e^t = 1 + t + t^2/2! + t^3/3! + \cdots has coordinates (1, 1, 1/2!, 1/3!, \dots) in this polynomial coordinate system — that's the Taylor series, a distant cousin of what we're about to do.

Fourier series simply picks a different coordinate system

Polynomial Axes

$$1,\ t,\ t^2,\ t^3,\dots$$$$f(t)=a_0+a_1t+a_2t^2+\cdots$$

Fourier Axes

$$\dots,e^{-j\omega_0t},1,e^{j\omega_0t},e^{j2\omega_0t},\dots$$$$x(t)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}a_ke^{jk\omega_0t}$$

In this picture, cos(ω₀t) has Fourier-coordinates (…, 0, 0, ½, 0, ½, 0, 0, …) — only k = −1 and k = 1 are nonzero. Every other coordinate is exactly zero.

Coefficients as projections

Vectors

coordinate along u = v·u

Signals

coordinate along e^{jkω₀t} = ⟨x(t), e^{jkω₀t}⟩

projection ⟹ Fourier coefficient a_k

For signals, the "dot product" becomes an average over one period, and the k-th Fourier axis is z(t) = e^{jkω₀t}, so z*(t) = e^{−jkω₀t}:

$$\langle x(t),z(t)\rangle = \frac{1}{T}\int_T x(t)z^*(t)\,dt \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a_k=\frac{1}{T}\int_T x(t)e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt$$
Exactly the analysis equation from §03 — now you can see why it has that shape: it's a projection formula in disguise.
✓ Why this matters for exams

This reframing explains, in one shot, why orthogonality (§03's averaging trick) is the entire mechanism behind the coefficient formula — orthogonal basis vectors are exactly what makes a projection formula give you a clean, single coordinate instead of a tangled mess.

§ 08 — Beyond The Slides

Convergence & Gibbs Phenomenon

Not on the original slide deck, but essential for any exam that asks "does the Fourier series actually converge to x(t)?" — and it explains that stubborn ringing you saw in §05.

When does the sum actually equal x(t)?

The synthesis equation writes x(t) as an infinite sum. A fair question: does that infinite sum actually converge back to x(t)? The Dirichlet conditions give sufficient conditions:

Dirichlet Conditions (sufficient, not strictly necessary)
  1. x(t) is absolutely integrable over one period: ∫_T |x(t)| dt < ∞
  2. x(t) has a finite number of maxima and minima in any one period (bounded variation)
  3. x(t) has a finite number of discontinuities in any one period, each of finite size

Virtually every signal you'll meet in this course (including both worked examples above) satisfies all three. Where the conditions hold, the series converges to x(t) at every point of continuity — and at a jump, it converges to the average of the left- and right-hand limits:

$$x_N(t_0) \xrightarrow{N\to\infty} \frac{x(t_0^-)+x(t_0^+)}{2} \quad \text{at a jump discontinuity } t_0$$

Gibbs phenomenon — the ringing that never fully disappears

Look again at the square wave reconstruction from §05. Near the jump, the partial sum always overshoots above the top value and undershoots below the bottom value — no matter how many harmonics you add. This is the Gibbs phenomenon.

⚠ The counterintuitive part

Adding more harmonics does not shrink the overshoot's height. The overshoot locks in at roughly 8.9% of the jump size and stays there forever as N → ∞. What does shrink is the width of the ringing region — it squeezes closer and closer to the discontinuity, but the peak height stays put.

Square Wave (has a jump)
Triangular Ramp (no jump)

Compare the two curves as you raise N: the square wave's overshoot height barely budges past a certain point (it's stuck near 8.9%), while the triangular ramp — which has no jump discontinuity, only a corner — converges smoothly with no overshoot at all.

ℹ Why the difference?

Gibbs phenomenon is a signature of jump discontinuities specifically. The square wave has one (at t = ±T₁); the triangular ramp from §06 is continuous everywhere — it only has corners (discontinuous slope) — so it never rings. This connects directly to the coefficient decay rates you'll formalize in §09.

§ 09 — Beyond The Slides

Symmetry & Parseval's Theorem

Two more tools not in the original deck, but both are exam favorites: symmetry shortcuts that halve your work, and a theorem connecting energy in time to energy in frequency.

Symmetry properties of a_k

These follow directly from the analysis equation and are extremely useful shortcuts — check them before grinding an integral.

If x(t) is…Then a_k satisfies…
real (always true for physical signals)a₋ₖ = a_k*  (conjugate symmetry)
real & evena_k is real, and a₋ₖ = a_k
real & odda_k is purely imaginary, and a₋ₖ = −a_k
half-wave symmetric: x(t+T/2) = −x(t)a_k = 0 for every even k
✓ Check against our own worked examples

The square wave in §05 is real and even (symmetric about t = 0) — and indeed we found every a_k came out purely real. The triangular ramp in §06 is neither even nor odd — and indeed its a_k came out genuinely complex. That's not a coincidence; it's this table in action.

Parseval's theorem — power in time equals power in frequency

$$\frac{1}{T}\int_T |x(t)|^2\,dt = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}|a_k|^2$$
The average power of x(t), computed directly in time, exactly equals the sum of the squared magnitudes of every harmonic.

This is powerful because it means no information (and no energy) is lost by switching to the frequency-domain description — the Fourier series is a lossless repackaging of the signal, just viewed through a different coordinate system (recall §07). It also gives you a fast way to sanity-check a computed set of coefficients: plug them into the right-hand side and see if it matches the obviously-computable left-hand side.

The time–bandwidth tradeoff

Look back at the square wave envelope in §05: as the pulse width T₁ shrinks (a narrower spike in time), the coefficients a_k = sin(kω₀T₁)/(kπ) spread out over more harmonics before decaying — you need higher and higher k to represent a sharper edge. Conversely, a smooth, gently-varying signal needs only a few low-order harmonics.

narrow in time  ⟺  spread out across many harmonics (wide "bandwidth")
spread out in time  ⟺  concentrated in a few low harmonics (narrow "bandwidth")

This is a distant cousin of the Heisenberg uncertainty principle: a signal cannot be made arbitrarily concentrated in both time and frequency at once.

Coefficient decay rate reveals smoothness

Compare the two worked examples directly:

SignalSmoothness|a_k| decays like
Square wave (§05)Jump discontinuity1/k
Triangular ramp (§06)Continuous, corner only1/k²

General rule: the smoother a periodic signal is (the more continuous derivatives it has), the faster its Fourier coefficients decay — and the fewer harmonics you need for a good approximation. A jump costs you a full order of decay compared to a mere corner.

Common pitfalls

⚠ Sign of the exponent

Synthesis uses +j, analysis uses −j. Swapping them conjugates every coefficient.

⚠ Forgetting the 1/T normalization

The analysis integral is an average, not a raw integral — always divide by T (or by the length of whatever period you integrated over).

⚠ a₀ from the sinc formula

Formulas like sin(kω₀T₁)/(kπ) are undefined (0/0) at k = 0. Always compute a₀ separately as a plain average.

⚠ Picking an awkward integration interval

You may integrate over any full period — choose the one that makes the algebra easiest (e.g. centering on where a pulse is nonzero, as in §05), not necessarily the interval the problem was originally written on.

⚠ Ignoring negative harmonics

Negative k is not a redundant mirror to discard — both ±k terms are needed to reconstruct a real signal (§01, §09).

⚠ Forgetting ω₀ changes with T

If a problem redefines the period, ω₀ = 2π/T must be recomputed — don't reuse a value of ω₀ from an earlier part of the problem.

Quiz

Six questions spanning the whole lecture. Try each one honestly before revealing the answer.

Q1What does a₀ represent physically, for any periodic signal?
Answer: The average (DC) value of x(t) over one period — the height the signal would sit at if perfectly flattened out. This holds for every signal, with no exceptions.
Q2For x(t) = 3 + 4cos(ω₀t) − 2sin(2ω₀t), find a₂ without integrating.
Answer: Write −2sin(2ω₀t) = −2·(e^{j2ω₀t}−e^{−j2ω₀t})/(2j) = (j)e^{j2ω₀t} + (−j)e^{−j2ω₀t}. So a₂ = j (and a₋₂ = −j). Check: a₋₂ = a₂* ✓ since a₂ = j is purely imaginary and x(t) is real & odd about its non-constant part.
Q3If you shrink the pulse width T₁ of a square wave while keeping T fixed, does the spectrum |a_k| get narrower or wider across k?
Answer: Wider. A narrower pulse in time needs more harmonics to reconstruct its sharp edges — this is the time–bandwidth tradeoff from §09.
Q4x(t) is real and even. What can you immediately say about every a_k?
Answer: Every a_k is purely real, and a₋ₖ = a_k (even in k too). You saw this confirmed directly by the square wave in §05.
Q5Why doesn't Gibbs overshoot shrink even with 1000 harmonics?
Answer: The Fourier series does not converge uniformly near a jump discontinuity. The overshoot's height locks in at about 8.9% of the jump and stays there for any N; only the width of the ringing region shrinks toward zero as N grows.
Q6State the synthesis equation and the analysis equation from memory.
Answer: Synthesis: x(t) = Σ_{k=−∞}^{∞} a_k e^{jkω₀t}. Analysis: a_k = (1/T)∫_T x(t) e^{−jkω₀t} dt, with ω₀ = 2π/T. If you can write both cold, with the correct sign on each exponent, you're in good shape for this topic.
