Section 00 · Orientation

Why Properties Matter

Before we touch a single new formula — a change of mindset. This lecture is not about learning new signals. It's about learning a toolbox that lets you reuse work you've already done.

Back in Lecture 5, you learned the hard way to find Fourier coefficients: set up $a_k = \frac{1}{T}\int_T x(t)e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt$ and grind through an integral. That works, but it's slow, and exam time is not the time to be slow.

The trick the professor is teaching in this lecture: most signals you'll ever meet are just old, familiar signals wearing a disguise — shifted, flipped, stretched, differentiated, added together, or multiplied. If you already know the Fourier series of the signal underneath the disguise, you don't need to integrate again. You just need to know the rule for the disguise.

Core Idea
$$ \text{known: } x(t)\longleftrightarrow a_k \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \text{operation on } x(t) \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{simple operation on } a_k $$

Shift it, scale it, add it, differentiate it — and simply transfer the coefficients, without starting from scratch.

§Every Signal Is a Stack of Simple Rotating Waves

Here's the picture to keep in your head for the entire lecture. The synthesis equation

$$ x(t) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} a_k\, e^{jk\omega_0 t} $$

says x(t) is really a sum of little rotating phasors — one per harmonic k — each spinning at its own speed kω0, with its own size and starting angle given by ak. When you shift, reverse, scale, or differentiate x(t), you are secretly doing the exact same thing to every one of those little wheels at once. Properties are just the "what happens to one wheel" rule, applied uniformly.

Section 01 · Property One of Eight

Linearity

The gentlest property, and the one every other property secretly leans on.

Suppose you know two signals sharing the same fundamental period T (so the same ω0):

$$ x(t) \longleftrightarrow a_k, \qquad y(t) \longleftrightarrow b_k $$

Because their Fourier axes line up exactly — same k, same ω0 — adding the signals just means adding the ingredients sitting at each frequency slot:

Linearity Property
$$ \alpha\,x(t) + \beta\,y(t) \;\longleftrightarrow\; \alpha a_k + \beta b_k $$

Mix in time  ⇔  mix the Fourier coefficients, harmonic by harmonic.

Play with the mixing knobs below. Two toy spectra, ak and bk, sit side by side. Slide α and β and watch the combined spectrum αak+βbk update live, bar by bar.

⚠
Edge case examiners love

Linearity as stated requires the same period. If x(t) has period T1 and y(t) has period T2, their sum is periodic only if T1/T2 is rational — and the correct fundamental period to use is the LCM of T1 and T2 (exactly the LCM trick you already used for discrete-time periodicity back in Lecture 2). You then need to re-express both ak and bk against that shared, finer harmonic grid before adding.

§Worked Example

Practice

Let $x(t)\leftrightarrow a_k$ with $a_1=a_{-1}=\tfrac12$, all other $a_k=0$ (i.e. $x(t)=\cos\omega_0 t$), and let $y(t)\leftrightarrow b_k$ with $b_0=1$, all other $b_k=0$ (i.e. $y(t)=1$). Find the coefficients of $p(t) = 3x(t) - 2y(t)$.

Apply linearity directly

$p(t) = 3x(t) + (-2)y(t) \leftrightarrow 3a_k + (-2)b_k = 3a_k - 2b_k$

Evaluate per harmonic

$c_0 = 3(0) - 2(1) = -2$.   $c_{\pm1} = 3(\tfrac12) - 2(0) = \tfrac32$.   all other $c_k = 0$.

Sanity check

$p(t) = 3\cos\omega_0 t - 2$, and indeed a signal of that form has exactly a DC term of $-2$ and a $\pm1$ pair of $\tfrac32$ each. Matches.

§Quick Check

Self-Check
If $x(t)$ has fundamental period 4s and $y(t)$ has fundamental period 6s, what fundamental period should you use to write the Fourier series of $x(t)+y(t)$?
Correct: 12 s. The combined signal repeats only once both component signals complete a whole number of cycles simultaneously — that first common point is the LCM(4,6) = 12, exactly the same reasoning you used for periodicity of sums of discrete-time exponentials in Lecture 2.
Section 02 · Property Two of Eight

Time Shifting

Delay the signal in time. Does the frequency content change? Think before you scroll.

No — of course not. Delaying a recording doesn't change which notes are being played, only when they start relative to your clock. Every rotating phasor underneath just needs to be re-synced to a new zero. Starting from $x(t) = \sum_k a_k e^{jk\omega_0 t}$:

$$ x(t-t_0) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} a_k\, e^{jk\omega_0(t-t_0)} = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} \underbrace{\left(a_k e^{-jk\omega_0 t_0}\right)}_{\text{new coefficient}} e^{jk\omega_0 t} $$
Time-Shifting Property
$$ x(t-t_0) \;\longleftrightarrow\; a_k\, e^{-jk\omega_0 t_0} $$

A delay in time becomes a phase shift in frequency. Nothing about the size changes.

§Why Only the Phase Moves

Multiplying any complex number by $e^{j\phi}$ is a pure rotation: it keeps the radius exactly the same and adds $\phi$ to the angle. Since $\left|e^{-jk\omega_0 t_0}\right| = 1$ always, the coefficient's magnitude is completely unchanged — only its phase rotates, by $\angle = -k\omega_0 t_0$. And because that rotation is proportional to $k$, higher-frequency harmonics swing through a larger angle for the very same delay $t_0$.

✓
Exam tip

If a question asks for $|a_k|$ or the power spectrum $|a_k|^2$ of a time-shifted signal, the answer is identical to the original — you don't even need $t_0$. Time shift only ever shows up in questions that ask about phase, or that ask you to reconstruct the coefficients themselves (as complex numbers).

§Worked Example

Slide Practice — Shift by 3

Suppose $x(t)\leftrightarrow a_k$. Find the coefficients of $y(t) = x(t-3)$ in terms of $a_k$.

Identify t₀

Here $t_0 = 3$.

Apply the property directly

$y(t) = x(t-3) \leftrightarrow a_k e^{-j3k\omega_0}$

Interpret

The magnitudes $|a_k|$ stay exactly the same; only the phases rotate, and by an amount that grows with $k$.

§Quick Check

Self-Check
A signal $x(t)$ is delayed to give $y(t)=x(t-t_0)$. Which statement is true?
Correct: the phase change is $-k\omega_0t_0$, which is proportional to k. The $k$-th harmonic rotates $k$ times faster than the fundamental for the same delay — magnitude never changes.
Section 03 · Property Three of Eight

Time Reversal

Play the signal backward. Every hidden rotating wave flips direction.

$$ x(-t) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} a_k\, e^{jk\omega_0(-t)} = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} a_k\, e^{j(-k)\omega_0 t} $$

The term that used to live at harmonic $+k$ now lives at harmonic $-k$. Relabelling the dummy index gives:

Time-Reversal Property
$$ x(-t) \;\longleftrightarrow\; a_{-k} $$

Reversal in time  ⇔  index reversal in frequency. Not conjugation — just a relabel of which slot each old coefficient sits in.

Visually: imagine playing a video of a rotating phasor backward. A wheel spinning counter-clockwise at $k\omega_0$ appears, on the reversed tape, to spin clockwise — i.e. at $-k\omega_0$. That's the whole property, animated below.

⚠
Don't confuse with conjugation

Time reversal gives $a_{-k}$. It does not conjugate the coefficient. If you separately know $x(t)$ is real, then $a_{-k}=a_k^{*}$ happens to also hold (from Lecture 5's symmetry properties) — but that's a coincidence of realness, not something time reversal itself does. Don't merge the two facts by accident on an exam.

§Worked Example — Combining Shift and Reversal

Slide Practice

Suppose $x(t)\leftrightarrow a_k$ and $y(t)\leftrightarrow b_k$. Find the coefficients of $x(4-t) + y(t-3)$ in terms of $a_k$ and $b_k$.

Handle x(4−t): shift first, then reverse

Write $x(4-t)$ as $x(-(t-4))$. First shift: $x(t+4)\leftrightarrow a_k e^{j4k\omega_0}$ (a shift by $t_0=-4$). Now reverse on $t$: replace $t\to -t$ everywhere, which sends $k\to -k$ in the coefficient: $$x(-t+4) \leftrightarrow a_{-k}\,e^{j4(-k)\omega_0} = a_{-k}\,e^{-j4k\omega_0}$$

Handle y(t−3): plain shift, t₀ = 3

$y(t-3) \leftrightarrow b_k\,e^{-j3k\omega_0}$

Add by linearity

$$x(4-t) + y(t-3) \;\longleftrightarrow\; a_{-k}\,e^{-j4k\omega_0} + b_k\,e^{-j3k\omega_0}$$

↔
The safe recipe for x(A − Bt)-style arguments

Whenever the time argument is a combination like $4-t$, don't try to apply "reversal" and "shift" to the messy expression at once. Instead: (1) temporarily replace $t\to -t$ conceptually only after first shifting so that the argument becomes a pure reversal of a pure shift, i.e. rewrite $x(4-t)$ as first shifting $x(t)$ to $x(t+4)$, then reversing the result. Always shift, then reverse — reversing first and shifting second gives the wrong sign on the shift. Section 04 extends this same recipe to include scaling as a third, final step.

§Quick Check

Self-Check
If $x(t)$ is an even signal ($x(t)=x(-t)$), what must be true of its Fourier coefficients?
Correct: since $x(t)=x(-t)$, their coefficients must match too: $a_k = a_{-k}$. This is exactly the even-signal symmetry property from Lecture 5 — now you can see why it's true, straight from the time-reversal property.
Section 04 · Property Four of Eight

Time Scaling

The odd one out: this is the only property that touches ω0 itself, not just the coefficients.

$$ x(\alpha t) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} a_k\, e^{jk\omega_0(\alpha t)} = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} a_k\, e^{jk(\alpha\omega_0)t} $$

Compare this sum to the standard synthesis form $\sum_k c_k e^{jk\omega_0' t}$. The coefficients multiplying each exponential are still exactly $a_k$ — but the thing playing the role of the fundamental frequency is no longer $\omega_0$, it's $\alpha\omega_0$.

Time-Scaling Property
$$ x(\alpha t) \;\longleftrightarrow\; a_k \quad\text{with new fundamental frequency}\quad \omega_0' = \alpha\omega_0 $$

The coefficient sequence never changes. The frequency ruler underneath it does.

This is genuinely two different pictures of the same fact, and exams love to test whether you can tell them apart:

  • Against index k — the value attached to the $k$-th harmonic slot is unchanged: $b_k = a_k$.
  • Against actual frequency ω — the spectral line that used to sit at $k\omega_0$ physically moves to $k\alpha\omega_0$. For $\alpha>1$ (time compression) the lines spread farther apart; for $0<\alpha<1$ (time stretching) they crowd closer together.
⚠
Edge case: negative α

What if $\alpha<0$? Then $x(\alpha t) = x(-|\alpha|t)$ is a scale by $|\alpha|$ combined with a time reversal. You'd chain both properties: coefficients become $a_{-k}$ (from the sign flip), fundamental frequency becomes $|\alpha|\omega_0$. Slides that write $p(t)=x(4-3t)$ are secretly hiding exactly this case, since the effective scale factor on $t$ is $-3$.

§Worked Example — Shift, Reverse, Scale in One Signal

Slide Practice

Let $x(t)\leftrightarrow a_k$ with fundamental frequency $\omega_0$. Find the Fourier series coefficients of $p(t) = x(4-3t)$ in terms of $a_k$.

Strategy: think in stages — $x(t) \to x(t+4) \to x(4-t) \to x(4-3t)$.

Stage 1 — shift left by 4

$x(t+4) \leftrightarrow a_k\, e^{j4k\omega_0}$

Stage 2 — reverse

$x(4-t) \leftrightarrow a_{-k}\, e^{-j4k\omega_0}$  (replace $k\to -k$ in the previous result)

Stage 3 — scale time by 3

Scaling never touches the coefficient values themselves — only $\omega_0$. So the coefficient expression is unchanged: $$x(4-3t) \leftrightarrow a_{-k}\, e^{-j4k\omega_0}, \qquad \omega_0' = 3\omega_0$$

§Quick Check

Self-Check
$x(t)$ has period $T=2$s. What is the period of $y(t) = x(4t)$?
Correct: 0.5 s. Time compression by a factor of 4 squeezes one full cycle into a quarter of the original duration: $T' = T/\alpha = 2/4 = 0.5$s, consistent with $\omega_0' = \alpha\omega_0 = 4\omega_0$.
Section 05 · Property Five of Eight

Differentiation

Differentiate the signal — differentiate every little wheel underneath it.

$$ \frac{d}{dt}x(t) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} a_k\,\frac{d}{dt}e^{jk\omega_0 t} = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} (jk\omega_0)\,a_k\; e^{jk\omega_0 t} $$

using $\frac{d}{dt}e^{jk\omega_0 t} = jk\omega_0\, e^{jk\omega_0 t}$.

Differentiation Property
$$ \frac{d}{dt}x(t) \;\longleftrightarrow\; (jk\omega_0)\,a_k $$

§Where Does the jkω0 Actually Come From?

Think of the $k$-th phasor as a bead moving on a circle of radius $|a_k|$, going around at angular speed $k\omega_0$ — this is circular motion, straight out of first-year mechanics. Differentiating position gives velocity, and for circular motion, speed is $\omega r$: here $\omega = k\omega_0$ and $r=|a_k|$, so the derivative's magnitude is $|k\omega_0|\,|a_k|$ — that explains the $k\omega_0$ magnitude.

But where's the extra j from? Velocity in circular motion is always tangent to the position vector, i.e. rotated $90°$ ahead of it — and multiplying a complex number by $j$ is exactly a $90°$ rotation. So $j$ isn't decoration; it's the statement "velocity points sideways to position."

§Worked Example — Chaining Onto Section 04's Result

Slide Practice

Reusing the Section 04 result $x(4-3t)\leftrightarrow a_{-k}e^{-j4k\omega_0}$ with $\omega_0'=3\omega_0$, find the coefficients of $p(t)=\frac{d}{dt}x(4-3t)$.

Differentiate using the CURRENT ω₀′, not the original

The signal $x(4-3t)$ now has fundamental frequency $\omega_0'=3\omega_0$ — that's the value that belongs in the differentiation formula, since it's the frequency the harmonics of this specific signal actually spin at.

Apply differentiation

$$p(t) \leftrightarrow (jk\omega_0')\left(a_{-k}e^{-j4k\omega_0}\right) = j3k\omega_0\, a_{-k}\, e^{-j4k\omega_0}$$

⚠
The single most common slip on this topic

When you differentiate a signal that has already been time-scaled, always plug in the new fundamental frequency $\omega_0'=\alpha\omega_0$ — never the original $\omega_0$. This is the mistake that quietly costs marks on combined-property problems.

§Jump Discontinuities Create Impulses

For smooth stretches, the derivative is just the slope. But at a jump of height $A$ at $t=t_0$, the derivative isn't defined in the ordinary sense — it contains an impulse: $A\,\delta(t-t_0)$. So in general:

$$ \frac{d}{dt}x(t) = \big(\text{ordinary slope between jumps}\big) + \big(\text{impulses at each jump, weighted by jump height}\big) $$

Teal segments = ordinary slope between jumps. Rust arrows = impulses, one per jump, with area (weight) equal to the jump's height. An upward jump gives a positive impulse; a downward jump gives a negative one.

Section 05b · A Necessary Detour

The Sifting Property & Impulse Trains

Before Integration, we need one tool from the impulse function's toolbox — because impulses from Section 05 need to be integrated against things.

Start discrete, where it's obvious. Sum an arbitrary signal $x[n]$ against a shifted unit impulse:

$$ \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n]\,\delta[n-a] = x[a] $$

Why? $\delta[n-a]$ is zero everywhere except at $n=a$, so every term in the sum vanishes except the one where $n=a$ — only that single term survives. The continuous-time version does exactly the same job, with an integral standing in for the sum:

$$ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)\,\delta(t-a)\,dt = x(a) $$

"A giraffe only feels the height of the grass at the exact point where it's standing." — the impulse is a pure value-picker: it doesn't care about the shape of x(t) anywhere except at one instant.

⚠
One condition you must always check

The impulse only extracts a value if the integration window actually contains it: $$ \int_L^R x(t)\,\delta(t-a)\,dt = \begin{cases} x(a), & L< a< R \\ 0, & a\notin[L,R] \end{cases} $$ A boundary case ($a=L$ or $a=R$ exactly) is typically treated as ambiguous/undefined and rarely tested cleanly — but "outside the window" is a favorite trap.

Drag the slider to move the impulse location $a$ against a fixed window $[L,R]=[0,5]$ and watch when it "counts."

§Worked Example

Slide Practice — Sifting in Action

Let $x(t)=e^t$. Find (a) $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)\delta(t)\,dt$, (b) $\int_{1}^{\infty}x(t)\delta(t)\,dt$, (c) $\int_{-5}^{5}x(t)[\delta(t+1)+2\delta(t-2)]\,dt$.

(a)

Impulse at $t=0$ is inside $(-\infty,\infty)$: value is $e^0=1$.

(b)

Impulse at $t=0$ lies outside the window $[1,\infty)$: result is $0$.

(c)

Both impulses ($t=-1$ and $t=2$) sit inside $[-5,5]$: $e^{-1} + 2e^{2}$.

§The Payoff — Fourier Series of an Impulse Train

Now use sifting for something genuinely useful: the Fourier series of a perfectly periodic train of impulses, spaced $T$ apart —

$$ x(t) = \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} \delta(t-mT) $$

Choose the period window $-T/2 < t < T/2$, which contains exactly one impulse (at $t=0$):

$$ a_k = \frac{1}{T}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2} \delta(t)\,e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt \;\overset{\text{sifting}}{=}\; \frac{1}{T}\,e^{-jk\omega_0\cdot 0} = \frac{1}{T} $$
Impulse Train Spectrum
$$ \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(t-mT) \;\longleftrightarrow\; a_k = \frac{1}{T}, \quad k=0,\pm1,\pm2,\dots $$

Every single harmonic carries exactly the same weight — a perfectly flat spectrum.

↔
Connecting back to Lecture 5's time–bandwidth tradeoff

This is the extreme case of the time–bandwidth tradeoff you already met: a signal maximally concentrated in time (an infinitely narrow spike) is maximally spread out in frequency (every harmonic, equally). Squeeze a signal in one domain and it necessarily spreads in the other — the impulse train sits at the very edge of that tradeoff.

This single result — differentiating a jump-containing periodic signal produces a periodic train of impulses, and a periodic train of impulses has this beautifully flat spectrum — is exactly the toolkit the differentiation property needs to shine. Let's use both together on a full example.

§Capstone Example — Differentiating a Periodic Pulse

Full Verification Problem

Let $x(t)$ be a periodic rectangular pulse: $x(t)=1$ for $|t|

$$ a_0 = \frac{2T_1}{T}, \qquad a_k = \frac{\sin(k\omega_0 T_1)}{k\pi}\;\;(k\neq0), \qquad \omega_0=\frac{2\pi}{T} $$

Differentiate $x(t)$ directly, find $\frac{d}{dt}x(t)$'s Fourier coefficients using sifting, and check the answer against the differentiation property from Section 05.

Step 1 — Sketch the derivative

Rising edge at $t=-T_1$ gives $+\delta(t+T_1)$; falling edge at $t=T_1$ gives $-\delta(t-T_1)$. Repeating every $T$: $$\frac{d}{dt}x(t) = \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\Big[\delta(t+T_1-mT) - \delta(t-T_1-mT)\Big]$$

Step 2 — Find its coefficients directly, via sifting

Over one period, using $b_k$ for these new coefficients: $$ b_k = \frac{1}{T}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}\big[\delta(t+T_1)-\delta(t-T_1)\big]e^{-jk\omega_0t}\,dt = \frac{1}{T}\left[e^{jk\omega_0T_1} - e^{-jk\omega_0T_1}\right] = \frac{2j}{T}\sin(k\omega_0T_1) $$

Step 3 — Predict the same thing using the differentiation property

The property says $\frac{d}{dt}x(t)\leftrightarrow jk\omega_0 a_k$. For $k\neq0$: $$ jk\omega_0 a_k = jk\omega_0\cdot\frac{\sin(k\omega_0T_1)}{k\pi} = j\frac{\omega_0}{\pi}\sin(k\omega_0T_1) = \frac{2j}{T}\sin(k\omega_0T_1) $$ using $\omega_0=2\pi/T$.

Step 4 — Perfect match

Both routes agree exactly: $b_k = (jk\omega_0)\,a_k$. This also automatically gives $b_0=0$ — differentiating a periodic signal always removes its DC component, which is a preview of exactly the warning coming up in Integration.

Section 06 · Property Six of Eight

Integration

The mirror image of differentiation — and the one property with an asterisk attached.

Differentiation multiplies by $jk\omega_0$. Integration should undo that, so it must divide by the same factor:

Integration Property
$$ \int x(t)\,dt \;\longleftrightarrow\; \frac{a_k}{jk\omega_0}, \qquad k\neq 0 $$
⚠
The constant of integration strikes back

Integration cannot recover $a_0$, ever. Why? Because $\frac{d}{dt}\big(x(t)+C\big) = \frac{d}{dt}x(t)$ for any constant $C$ — differentiation is completely blind to a DC offset, so there's no way to reverse-engineer it back out. Whenever you use "integrate the derivative to get back $x(t)$," you must find $a_0$ by a separate route — usually the direct average-value integral $a_0 = \frac{1}{T}\int_T x(t)\,dt$.

Every curve above has exactly the same derivative (same slope everywhere) — integration alone can never tell you which one is "the real" x(t) without an extra piece of information about the DC level.

↔
Connecting back to Lecture 5

Recall $a_0$ is exactly the average value / DC component of $x(t)$ over one period — the very same $a_0$ you computed directly via integration in Lecture 5. That's precisely the "separate route" this warning is asking you to fall back on.

§Worked Example — Sawtooth via Differentiate-then-Integrate-Back

Slide Practice

Let $x(t)=t$ for $0\le t<1$, periodic with $x(t+1)=x(t)$ (a sawtooth). Find its Fourier coefficients by first finding the series for $y(t)=\frac{d}{dt}x(t)$, then integrating back.

Step 1 — Differentiate the sawtooth

Between jumps the slope is $1$. At each integer the signal drops by $1$ (a downward jump), contributing $-\delta(t-m)$: $$ y(t) = \frac{d}{dt}x(t) = 1 - \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(t-m) $$

Step 2 — Coefficients of y(t)

The constant "1" contributes only to $k=0$: coefficient $1$. The unit-spaced impulse train ($T=1$) contributes $\frac{1}{T}=1$ to every $k$ (from the impulse-train result above). Subtracting: $$ b_k = \begin{cases} 1-1=0, & k=0 \\ 0-1=-1, & k\neq0 \end{cases} $$

Step 3 — Integrate back for k ≠ 0

Since $y(t)=\frac{d}{dt}x(t)$, we have $b_k = jk\omega_0 a_k$, so $a_k = b_k/(jk\omega_0)$. Here $\omega_0=2\pi$ (since $T=1$) and $b_k=-1$: $$ a_k = \frac{-1}{j2\pi k} = \frac{j}{2\pi k}, \qquad k\neq0 $$

Step 4 — a₀ must be found separately

Integration gave nothing for $k=0$ — go straight to the average-value definition: $$ a_0 = \int_0^1 t\,dt = \frac{1}{2} $$ Final answer: $a_0=\frac12$, $\;a_k = \dfrac{j}{2\pi k}$ for $k\neq0$.

§Quick Check

Self-Check
You differentiate $x(t)$, find the Fourier coefficients of the result, then integrate back to recover $a_k$ for $x(t)$. Which coefficient(s) can never be trusted from this procedure alone?
Correct: only $a_0$ is lost, because differentiation kills any constant (DC) offset. Every $k\neq0$ coefficient survives the round trip perfectly.
Section 07 · Property Seven of Eight

Multiplication

Linearity told us what addition does. Now: what does multiplying two signals do to their coefficients?

Write both signals as sums of phasors, using separate dummy indices $p$ and $q$ so the two sums don't collide:

$$ x(t)=\sum_{p=-\infty}^{\infty}a_p e^{jp\omega_0t}, \qquad y(t)=\sum_{q=-\infty}^{\infty}b_q e^{jq\omega_0t} $$

Substitute $z=e^{j\omega_0t}$, so $e^{jp\omega_0t}=z^p$. Suddenly $x(t)=\sum_p a_p z^p$ and $y(t)=\sum_q b_q z^q$ look exactly like polynomials in $z$ — and multiplying two power series is something you already know how to do.

One term from each: $a_p e^{jp\omega_0t}\cdot b_q e^{jq\omega_0t} = a_pb_q\,e^{j(p+q)\omega_0t}$. This lands on harmonic $k$ exactly when $p+q=k$, i.e. $q=k-p$. Summing over every way to make $k$:

Multiplication Property — Convolution Returns!
$$ x(t)y(t) \;\longleftrightarrow\; c_k = \sum_{p=-\infty}^{\infty} a_p\, b_{k-p} = (a*b)_k $$

Multiplication in time  ⇔  convolution in frequency.

↔
Yes — that convolution, back from Lecture 3

This is the identical reflect–slide–multiply–add recipe you learned for LTI system responses, just relocated: instead of convolving two functions of time, you're now convolving two sequences of coefficients indexed by $k$. Same algorithm, brand-new arena.

Try it yourself: pick two short coefficient sequences below and step through the discrete convolution that builds $c_k$.

§Worked Example — Squaring a Simple Signal

Slide Practice

Let $x(t) = 1+\cos(\omega_0t)$, so its only nonzero coefficients are $a_{-1}=\tfrac12$, $a_0=1$, $a_1=\tfrac12$. Find the Fourier coefficients of $p(t)=x^2(t)$.

Route 1 — Convolve the coefficient sequence with itself

$p(t)=x(t)x(t)$, so $c_k=(a*a)_k=\sum_p a_pa_{k-p}$. Only $p\in\{-1,0,1\}$ contribute anything nonzero, giving: $$ c_{-2}=\tfrac14,\;\; c_{-1}=1,\;\; c_0=\tfrac32,\;\; c_1=1,\;\; c_2=\tfrac14 $$

Route 2 — Cross-check with ordinary trigonometry

$$ x^2(t) = (1+\cos\omega_0t)^2 = 1+2\cos\omega_0t+\cos^2\omega_0t = \tfrac32 + 2\cos\omega_0t + \tfrac12\cos2\omega_0t $$ Reading off coefficients: $c_0=\tfrac32$, $c_{\pm1}=1$, $c_{\pm2}=\tfrac14$ — a perfect match with Route 1.

Exam habit worth building

Whenever the signal is a short trig expression, this trig-identity cross-check is a fast way to verify a convolution answer in under a minute — use it to catch arithmetic slips before submitting.

The order-of-operations recipe, one more time

For an argument like $x(A - Bt)$: (1) shift, (2) reverse, (3) scale — always in that order — then apply differentiation/integration last if needed, always using the updated $\omega_0'$ once scaling has happened.

Section 09 · Exam Prep

Self-Check Lab

Ten mixed questions, spanning everything in this lecture. No pressure — this is a practice mat, not a graded quiz.

Q1 · Linearity
$x(t)\leftrightarrow a_k$ and $y(t)\leftrightarrow b_k$ share the same period. What are the coefficients of $2x(t)-y(t)$?
Linearity distributes directly onto the coefficients: $2a_k - b_k$.
Q2 · Time Shift
If $x(t)\leftrightarrow a_k$, what are the coefficients of $x(t+2)$?
$x(t+2) = x(t-(-2))$, so $t_0=-2$: coefficients become $a_k e^{-jk\omega_0(-2)} = a_k e^{j2k\omega_0}$.
Q3 · Time Reversal
Which best describes what $x(-t)\leftrightarrow a_{-k}$ means physically?
Reversal relabels which index each coefficient value belongs to — it's an index swap, not a change in the values themselves.
Q4 · Time Scaling
$x(t)$ has $\omega_0=\pi$. What is the fundamental frequency of $y(t)=x(t/2)$?
Here $\alpha=1/2$ (time-stretch), so $\omega_0' = \alpha\omega_0 = \pi/2$.
Q5 · Differentiation
A periodic signal with no jump discontinuities is differentiated. What happens to its Fourier coefficients?
Differentiation always multiplies by $jk\omega_0$ regardless of smoothness — impulses only appear in the signal itself when there are jumps, they don't change the property's formula.
Q6 · Sifting
$\displaystyle\int_{2}^{10} \sin(t)\,\delta(t-1)\,dt$ equals:
The impulse sits at $t=1$, which is outside the window $[2,10]$ — the integral is $0$.
Q7 · Integration
Why can't the integration property alone ever tell you $a_0$?
Any constant added to $x(t)$ vanishes under differentiation, so there's no trace of it left to integrate back from — $a_0$ must come from the direct average-value formula instead.
Q8 · Multiplication
Multiplying two periodic signals in time corresponds to what operation on their Fourier coefficients?
Multiplication in time becomes convolution in the frequency/coefficient domain — the mirror image of how convolution in time became multiplication of transforms in other parts of the course.
Q9 · Combining Properties
To find the coefficients of $p(t) = \frac{d}{dt}\,x(5-2t)$, in what order should you apply the properties?
Build up the argument transformation first (shift → reverse → scale) to get the coefficient expression and the updated fundamental frequency, then differentiate last using that updated $\omega_0'$.
Q10 · Impulse Trains
Why does a periodic impulse train have a perfectly flat Fourier spectrum ($a_k$ the same for every $k$)?
This is the extreme end of the time–bandwidth tradeoff from Lecture 5: total concentration in time forces total (equal) spread across every frequency.
